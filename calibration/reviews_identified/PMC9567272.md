### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

Narunsky-Haziza, Sepich-Poore, Livyatan et al. investigate the presence, community composition, multi-domain interactions, and diagnostic/prognostic utility of fungal nucleic acids and cells across 17,401 tissue, blood, and plasma samples spanning 35 cancer types in four cohorts: (1) a Weizmann Institute of Science (WIS) cohort of 1,183 tumor, normal adjacent tissue (NAT), and normal breast samples (plus 104 paraffin and 191 extraction controls) profiled by ITS2 amplicon sequencing, 5.8S qPCR, and histological staining (anti-$\beta$-glucan, anti-*Aspergillus*, 28S rRNA FISH, and GMS); (2) The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort of 15,512 WGS and RNA-seq samples aligned against 320 fungal genomes in RefSeq release 200 (`rep200`) using Bowtie2/Woltka; and (3) two cell-free plasma WGS cohorts from Johns Hopkins ($n = 537$) and UCSD ($n = 169$). The authors report low-biomass fungal DNA across all studied cancer types, define three pan-cancer fungal-bacterial-immune "mycotypes" (`F1`–`F3`) via MMvec that stratify survival, and train gradient boosting and random forest classifiers on raw and `Voom-SNM` batch-corrected fungal counts to distinguish cancer types in tumor tissue and blood (AUROC 83–94%) and to diagnose stage I cancer from cell-free plasma (AUROC 94–98% when combined with bacterial features). Raw WIS sequencing data (PRJNA786764) and analysis scripts (`[GITHUB_REPO_REDACTED]`) are publicly deposited.

While the experimental arm in the WIS cohort—combining 295 physical negative controls, 5.8S qPCR, ITS2 sequencing, and multi-modal tissue microarray staining—provides useful empirical evidence that sparse fungal signals can be detected in a subset of human tumors (particularly mucosal/skin-adjacent sites), the manuscript's headline pan-cancer machine learning, liquid-biopsy, and multi-domain ecology claims rest on deeply flawed bioinformatic and statistical pipelines in the TCGA, Hopkins, and UCSD shotgun datasets. Specifically: (1) applying supervised `Voom-SNM` normalization across the full dataset prior to cross-validation leaks label information and imprints target/batch structure onto sparse count matrices; (2) mapping ultra-short reads (mean 57.4 bp, minimum 45 bp) with permissive Bowtie2 parameters (`-k 16`) against an unmasked reference database (`rep200`) without filtering low-complexity sequences, host/vector contamination in fungal assemblies, or fractional read-splitting artifacts inflates apparent taxonomic richness and drives spurious correlations between total bacterial and fungal reads ($\rho = 0.76$–$0.84$); and (3) the in silico decontamination pipeline overrides statistical contaminant calls whenever a taxon has been reported in prior literature or in WIS/HMP, retaining ubiquitous environmental and skin contaminants (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, *Malassezia*, *Candida*, *Aspergillus*). Resolving these issues requires eliminating supervised `Voom-SNM` batch correction, purging human-homologous/contaminant contigs from reference fungal genomes, and re-evaluating all classifiers under strict cross-center holdout validation.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Pan-cancer machine learning classifiers trained on batch-corrected TCGA tumor and blood mycobiomes accurately discriminate 32 cancer types (AUROC 83–94%): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  The authors report that technical batch variables (experimental strategy: WGS vs. RNA-seq, and sequencing center) account for 79% of total variance in the TCGA fungal count table (49% and 30%, respectively; STAR Methods, "TCGA and UCSD cohorts: Batch correction"). To remove this variance, they apply `Voom` followed by Supervised Normalization of Microarrays (`SNM`) across the cohort before performing 10-fold cross-validation (`Figure 5E, 5H`) or split-half validation (`Data S5.6C,D`). Because TCGA cancer types are heavily confounded with sequencing center and experimental strategy (as the authors note, 7 of 8 centers performed only WGS or only RNA-seq, and individual centers processed specific subsets of cancer types), and because `Voom-SNM` transforms zero-inflated discrete counts (where a sample with 0 reads for a taxon receives a continuous residual adjusted by its center/strategy and biological group) across the full matrix prior to train/test splitting, the normalization step leaks batch-disease structure into every sample's feature vector. Even within a single center (e.g., HMS; `Figure 5A, 5F`), different cancer types were extracted and sequenced on distinct plates (`plate-center` batches), allowing gradient boosting trees (`n.minobsinnode=1`) to classify cancer types via plate-level reagent background rather than biological tumor colonization.

* **Shotgun reads in TCGA, Hopkins, and UCSD plasma accurately quantify 224 decontaminated intratumoral and cell-free fungal species: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In TCGA, non-human reads mapped to fungi had a median length of 51 bp (mean 57.4 bp, minimum 45 bp) and were aligned using Bowtie2 (`--very-sensitive -k 16`), splitting each multi-mapped read as $1/k$ across up to 16 reference genomes and rounding summed frequencies to the nearest even integer. Critically, eukaryotic microbial reference genomes in RefSeq (`rep200`) are well known to harbor unmasked human sequence contamination, cloning vectors, rRNA repeats, and low-complexity regions that survive standard `GRCh38` host depletion on 45–57 bp reads. This artifact is directly evidenced by three internal patterns in the manuscript: (1) near-collinear Spearman correlations between total fungal and bacterial read proportions in TCGA primary tumors ($\rho = 0.76$), NATs ($\rho = 0.84$), and blood ($\rho = 0.84$, $p < 2.2 \times 10^{-308}$), whereas WIS extraction controls show no richness correlation (`Figure 3B`); (2) per-sample genome coverage in TCGA is tiny for almost all 224 species (only 31 species reach $\ge 1\%$ coverage even after pooling all 15,512 samples; `Table S4`), and the marker-based tool `EukDetect` detects almost none of the low-coverage species (22 species total; `Table S8.1`); and (3) the "decontamination" protocol (`STAR Methods`) explicitly overrode `decontam` statistical contaminant flags and retained any fungus previously reported in WIS, HMP, or 131 literature papers—guaranteeing that common laboratory, reagent, food, and skin contaminants (*S. cerevisiae*, *Malassezia restricta*, *C. albicans*) were kept as "decontaminated" biomarkers.

* **Fungal DNA and cells are detectable at low biomass in a subset of human tumors and localize to cancer cells and macrophages: [Moderately Supported].**  
  In the WIS cohort, where 104 paraffin controls and 191 extraction controls were processed alongside 1,183 tissue samples, 5.8S qPCR (`Figure 1B`) and ITS2 amplicon sequencing (floored at 1,000 reads/ASV/sample to suppress index hopping) demonstrate statistically higher fungal signal in several tumor types (notably colon, lung, pancreas, breast, and melanoma) than in negative controls, corroborated by 0–25% positivity across >600 TMA cores stained with anti-$\beta$-glucan, anti-*Aspergillus*, and 28S rRNA FISH (`Figure 2A`). However, the authors note that tumor mycobiome profiles do not significantly differ from matched normal adjacent tissue (NAT) in any TCGA cancer type by PERMANOVA (`Table S5`) and co-cluster with NAT in WIS (`Figure 3G`), indicating tissue-site background rather than tumor-specific recruitment. Furthermore, in `Figure 1B`, paraffin-only controls themselves had significantly higher fungal 5.8S qPCR signal than extraction controls ($p = 5.8 \times 10^{-4}$), and multiplying raw ASV counts by gel-band dilution factors (5- to 20-fold) after applying a flat 1,000-read floor (`STAR Methods`) introduces a nonlinear distortion into quantitative abundance comparisons.

* **Pan-cancer "mycotypes" (`F1`–`F3`) and taxon-specific clinical associations stratify immune subtypes, overall survival, and early-stage plasma liquid biopsy (AUROC 94–98%): [Fragile].**  
  First, in the MMvec co-occurrence analysis (`Figure 4`), the authors state in `STAR Methods` that the model predicting fungal-immune co-occurrences in the MD Anderson center had $Q^2 \le 0$ (performing no better than a null model with latent dimension 0). Moreover, pan-cancer survival stratification (`Figure 4D`) pools 20 cancer types with vastly different baseline mortalities (e.g., GBM and pancreatic cancer vs. breast and prostate cancer); because mycotype log-ratios differ across cancer types (`Figure 4B`), unstratified pan-cancer Kaplan-Meier curves (`Figure 4D`) confound cancer-type mortality with fungal log-ratios. Second, the WIS clinical associations (`Figure 6A–G`) rely on small positive sample sizes (e.g., $n = 11$ *M. globosa*-positive breast cancer patients in `Figure 6C`; $n = 9$ Phaeosphaeriaceae-positive ovarian cancer patients in `Figure 6F`) tested across dozens of taxa at an permissive threshold of $\text{FDR} \le 0.2$ without multivariable adjustment for age, stage, or treatment. Third, in the Hopkins and UCSD plasma cohorts (`Figure 6H–K`), cases and healthy controls were not confirmed to be collected, extracted, and sequenced in randomized, balanced batches within identical hospital phlebotomy workflows, so high GBM classification AUROCs on unmapped trace reads can reflect pre-analytical collection-tube or batch artifacts rather than tumor-shed fungal cfDNA.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Eliminate Supervised `Voom-SNM` Batch Correction and Evaluate Classifiers via Cross-Center Holdouts
* **WHY:** Applying `Voom-SNM` to zero-inflated microbial count matrices using biological labels (`sample_type`, `disease_type`, or `treatment_response`) and batch covariates (`center`, `strategy`) that are confounded with cancer type—and doing so prior to cross-validation—induces severe data leakage and artificial class separation on zero-count entries.
* **WHAT:** Remove `Voom-SNM` entirely from all machine learning pipelines and evaluate whether cancer-type or tumor-vs.-NAT classifiers generalize across completely independent sequencing centers.
* **HOW:** Using the raw (unnormalized) TCGA WGS count tables, train the gradient boosting and random forest classifiers exclusively on samples from one sequencing center (e.g., Harvard Medical School) and evaluate AUROC and AUPR on an external sequencing center that sequenced the same cancer types (e.g., Baylor College of Medicine or Broad Institute), without any prior batch adjustment or minority-class upsampling before splitting.

#### 2. Purge Host/Vector Contamination from Reference Genomes and Enforce Strict Alignment Filters
* **WHY:** Aligning 45–57 bp reads with Bowtie2 `-k 16` against unscreened eukaryotic genomes in `rep200` maps human pericentromeric repeats, ribosomal RNA, and sequencing adapters to fungal scaffolds, inflating species richness and generating artificial read-fraction correlations with bacteria.
* **WHAT:** Screen all 320 fungal genomes in `rep200` against human references (`GRCh38` + `T2T-CHM13` + human rDNA + UniVec), require unique high-identity alignments of $\ge 75\text{ bp}$, and verify uniformity of genome coverage per sample.
* **HOW:**
  1. Mask or remove all regions in the 320 `rep200` fungal assemblies that share $\ge 85\%$ identity over $\ge 40\text{ bp}$ with `T2T-CHM13`, `GRCh38`, human 45S rDNA (`NR_145819.1`), or NCBI `UniVec`.
  2. Re-align host-depleted reads requiring read length $\ge 75\text{ bp}$, unique mapping (discarding multi-domain and multi-genus reads rather than dividing by $1/k$), and breadth of coverage across at least 3 distinct non-overlapping genomic loci per sample before calling a species present.
  3. Recompute the TCGA fungal richness (`Figure 3A`) and bacterial-fungal read correlations (`Data S1.3C–E`) after this filtering.

#### 3. Correct the Statistical Decontamination Pipeline and NMI Formula Typo
* **WHY:** Retaining any species flagged by `decontam` simply because it appeared in WIS, HMP, or prior literature defeats the purpose of statistical decontamination, since ubiquitous reagent/skin contaminants (*S. cerevisiae*, *Malassezia*, *Candida*, *Aspergillus*) are precisely the taxa most frequently reported in human microbiome papers. Additionally, the Normalized Mutual Information (NMI) equation in `STAR Methods` contains a mathematical typo.
* **WHAT:** Apply `decontam` filters without literature-based whitelisting, and fix the NMI denominator in `STAR Methods`.
* **HOW:**
  1. In TCGA, Hopkins, and UCSD, remove all taxa flagged by `decontam` (`frequency` or `prevalence` mode) regardless of whether they are human commensals or present in HMP/WIS, and report how many of the "top 20 Hopkins plasma fungi" (`Table S8.2`) survive strict statistical decontamination.
  2. In `STAR Methods` ("WIS cohort: Construction and analysis of the multi-domain interaction networks"), correct the denominator of the NMI formula from the product $H(X)H(Y)$ to the square root $\sqrt{H(X)H(Y)}$ (or arithmetic mean $[H(X)+H(Y)]/2$), as $I(X,Y) / [H(X)H(Y)]$ is not bounded by 1 when $H(X), H(Y) < 1$.

#### 4. Stratify Survival Models by Cancer Type and Stage and Tighten FDR Thresholds
* **WHY:** Pooling 20 cancer types in Kaplan-Meier survival curves (`Figure 4D`) confounds fungal mycotype ratios with baseline cancer-type lethality, and testing unadjusted clinical associations in small WIS cohorts ($n = 9$ or $n = 11$ positive patients in `Figure 6C,F`) at $\text{FDR} \le 0.2$ risks high false-positive rates.
* **WHAT:** Report multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for cancer type, stage, age, and sex in the main text, and enforce a standard $\text{FDR} \le 0.05$ threshold across all clinical association tests.
* **HOW:** Replace the unadjusted pan-cancer Kaplan-Meier plots in `Figure 4D` with a forest plot of within-cancer-type hazard ratios (and 95% CIs) adjusted for stage, age, and sequencing center, and adjust theWIS breast (`Figure 6C`) and ovarian (`Figure 6F`) survival models for patient age, receptor status (HER2/ER/PR), and tumor stage.
