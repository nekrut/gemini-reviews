### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

Dudas et al. analyze 1,610 Ebola virus (EBOV) genomes sampled between March 2014 and October 2015 across Guinea, Sierra Leone, and Liberia—representing ~5.8% of all recorded Ebola virus disease (EVD) cases during the 2013–2016 West African epidemic—to reconstruct the spatiotemporal drivers of viral dispersal and local proliferation. Using a Bayesian continuous-time Markov chain (CTMC) phylogeographic generalized linear model (GLM) with two-way location random effects and stochastic search variable selection (SSVS) across 25 candidate predictors over 56 affected administrative regions in BEAST, the authors demonstrate that EBOV movement followed a gravity-like process driven positively by origin ($\text{BF} > 50$) and destination ($\text{BF} > 50$) population sizes and shared international borders ($\text{BF} > 50$), and negatively by geographic distance ($\text{BF} > 50$; 50% of jumps $<72\text{ km}$) and international versus national boundaries ($\text{BF} > 50$). Extending the framework to an epoch change-point GLM and a negative binomial GLM of regional case counts, they show that cross-border dispersal attenuated after August–September 2014 (80% posterior support)—too late to prevent epidemic establishment—that unaffected neighboring countries were spared primarily by lower importation risk rather than intrinsic resistance to local proliferation, and that the epidemic was sustained by metapopulation seeding of short-lived local transmission clusters (mean size 4.3 sequenced cases, mean persistence 41.3 days). All sequence alignments, BEAST XML specifications, posterior tree sets, and analysis scripts are publicly archived on GitHub and Edinburgh DataShare.

This study is a benchmark in phylodynamic epidemiology: it introduces two-way random effects to prevent spurious predictor inclusion in phylogeographic GLMs, carefully masks interferon-inducible ADAR T-to-C hypermutation tracts so they do not distort molecular clock rates, validates that the same five core dispersal drivers are recovered using a mid-epidemic sequence cutoff (October 2014, $N=787$), and maintains disciplined causal framing around border closures. Only minor technical issues warrant refinement: (1) discrete-trait CTMC phylogeography (even with GLM random effects) can be sensitive to lower sampling intensity in Liberia (3.8% vs. 8.0%–9.2% in Sierra Leone and Guinea); (2) including two collinear urban-accessibility predictors (`tt50K` and `tt100K`) in the negative binomial case-count SSVS splits posterior inclusion probability between them; and (3) a few minor numerical/notational discrepancies exist between the text, equations, and figure captions.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **The West African EBOV epidemic originated in Guéckédou Prefecture, Guinea, in late 2013 and spread via a gravity-like process modulated by national borders and shared international frontiers: [Well Supported].**  
  Relaxed-clock Skygrid dating places the epidemic tMRCA at 2014.06 (95% CI: 2013.96–2014.14) in Guéckédou with high posterior probability, and all five core GLM predictors (`Nat/Int`, `Distances`, `OrPop`, `DestPop`, `IntBoSh`) achieve posterior inclusion probabilities of $1.0$ ($\text{BF} > 50$; Table 1) even when accounting for origin and destination random effects $\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_j$ and when restricted to the October 2014 real-time subset ($N=787$; Figure 5).

* **The epidemic was sustained by repeated metapopulation seeding of relatively small, transient regional clusters rather than homogeneous mass-action persistence: [Well Supported].**  
  Markov jump reconstructions across the posterior tree distribution reveal an average inter-regional migration rate of 0.85 events/lineage/year (95% CI: 0.72–0.97), translating to a 3.6% translocation probability per 15.3-day serial interval, with repeated re-introductions into Guinea (21; 95% CI: 16–25) and Sierra Leone (9; 95% CI: 6–12) and rapid local cluster turnover (Figures 10–12). The authors appropriately note that incomplete sampling ($5.8\%$) splits true transmission chains conservatively while biasing detected cluster sizes upward relative to undetected singletons.

* **International border closures coincided with reduced cross-border dispersal rates after August–September 2014, but occurred after self-sustaining epidemics were already seeded in Sierra Leone and Liberia: [Well Supported].**  
  The epoch change-point GLM (Equation 3, Figure 4b) estimates an increase in the `Nat/Int` log-coefficient from 1.15 to 2.83 around August–September 2014 (80.0% posterior support), while phylogenetic lineage tracking (Figures 2 and 4a) demonstrates that the major Kailahun/Conakry/Montserrado transmission chains crossed borders in April–May 2014, months prior to border closures. The authors properly caution that the August–September shift cannot be uniquely attributed to border closures versus concurrent control interventions.

* **Local EBOV case burden was driven by population size, urban proximity (`tt50K`), lower temperature seasonality (`TempSS`), and higher precipitation (`Precip`): [Moderately Supported].**  
  In the negative binomial GLM of regional case counts across 56 administrative units (Table 2), `TempSS` ($\text{BF} > 50$), `tt50K` ($\text{BF} = 32.4$), and `PopSize` ($\text{BF} = 29.6$) show strong support, while `Precip` ($\text{BF} = 4.4$, inclusion probability $0.18$) and `tt100K` ($\text{BF} = 3.8$, inclusion probability $0.16$) show weak support. Support is moderated because entering correlated travel-time variables (`tt50K` and `tt100K`) and correlated climatic variables simultaneously into SSVS dilutes marginal inclusion probabilities across collinear predictors, and the regional case-count regression does not explicitly model spatial autocorrelation among neighboring administrative units.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Evaluate Structured Coalescent / Down-Sampled Sensitivity to Lower Liberian Sequencing Coverage
* **WHY:** Sequencing coverage in Liberia (209 genomes; 3.8% of cases) was less than half that in Sierra Leone (982 genomes; 8.0%) and Guinea (368 genomes; 9.2%), and dropped sharply in Liberia during late 2014. Although adding origin/destination sample-size residual predictors to the CTMC GLM showed no support, ancestral state reconstruction in discrete-trait CTMC models can still be influenced by uneven tip sampling across countries.
* **WHAT:** Verify that country-level introduction counts (e.g., the 21 re-introductions into Guinea and 9 into Sierra Leone) remain consistent when equalizing sampling fractions across countries.
* **HOW:** Down-sample the Sierra Leone and Guinea sequences randomly (or proportional to weekly regional incidence) to match Liberia's 3.8% sampling fraction across 10 replicate tree sets drawn from the posterior, re-estimate Markov jump counts between countries, and report the sensitivity range alongside the full-dataset estimates.

#### 2. Prune Collinear Predictors (`tt50K` vs. `tt100K`) and Check Spatial Autocorrelation in the Case-Count GLM
* **WHY:** In Table 2, both `tt50K` (travel time to a settlement of $\ge 50,000$) and `tt100K` (travel time to a settlement of $\ge 100,000$) are included in the negative binomial SSVS model. Because `tt50K` and `tt100K` measure the same underlying urban-accessibility construct, SSVS splits posterior mass between them (0.62 and 0.16), depressing the marginal Bayes factor of each.
* **WHAT:** Orthogonalize or select one urban travel-time metric and test Moran's $I$ on the negative binomial GLM residuals.
* **HOW:** Re-run the negative binomial GLM in BEAST retaining only `tt50K` (or the first principal component of urban accessibility) and report the updated inclusion probability and Bayes factor for urban proximity and `PopSize`. Compute Moran's $I$ on the deviance residuals across the 56 regions to confirm that climatic associations (`TempSS`) are not driven by unmodeled spatial autocorrelation between Guinea/Sierra Leone/Liberia.

#### 3. Clarify the Stationary Frequency Term $\pi_i$ in Equation (2)
* **WHY:** In the "Geographic history reconstruction" Methods section (line 114), the expected number of transitions from origin $i$ to destination $j$ is written as $\zeta_{ij} = \tau_i \mu \Lambda_{ij} \pi_i / c$, where $\tau_i$ is the posterior waiting time (Markov reward) spent in state $i$ across the phylogeny. In a continuous-time Markov chain where $q_{ij} = \mu \Lambda_{ij} / c$ is the instantaneous transition rate out of state $i$ to $j$, the expected number of jumps from $i$ to $j$ conditional on total waiting time $\tau_i$ in state $i$ is simply $\tau_i q_{ij} = \tau_i \mu \Lambda_{ij} / c$ (whereas multiplying by the stationary frequency $\pi_i$ would double-weight state $i$ if $\tau_i$ is already the realized time in state $i$).
* **WHAT:** Clarify the exact definition of $c$ and $\pi_i$ in Equation (2).
* **HOW:** Check the script used to compute $\zeta_{ij}$ in the GitHub repository and update Equation (2) and its accompanying text to specify whether $\pi_i$ enters only inside the BEAST rate-matrix normalization constant $c = -\sum_k \pi_k Q_{kk}$ rather than multiplying $\tau_i$ in the numerator.

#### 4. Technical, Mathematical, and Editorial Cleanup
* **WHY:** A few minor numerical and editorial inconsistencies appear between the main text, tables, and figure captions.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  1. **Country sequence sum discrepancy (Methods, line 79):** Line 79 states that the dataset contains *"1,610 publicly available full Ebola virus (EBOV) genomes"* comprising *"209 sequences from Liberia (3.8% of known and suspected cases), 982 from Sierra Leone (8.0%) and 368 from Guinea (9.2%)"*. However, $209 + 982 + 368 = 1,559$ (leaving 51 sequences unaccounted for, whereas line 94 states that only $N=2$ lacked country information). Reconcile the country totals so they sum to 1,610.
  2. **Cluster size 95th percentile discrepancy (Main text line 60 vs. Figure 11c caption):** Line 60 reports *"a mean cluster size of 4.3 and only 5% larger than 17 in our sample; Figure 11c"*, whereas the Figure 11c caption states *"with at least 2 sampled cases: 50% < 5.3, 95% < 32"*. Explicitly note in line 60 that `17` is the 95th percentile across *all* introductions (including singletons of size 1), whereas Figure 11c plots the distribution conditional on cluster size $\ge 2$ (where the 95th percentile is `32`).
  3. **Missing predictor `tt50K` in Table 3:** Table 2 reports `tt50K` as the second strongest predictor of regional case counts ($\text{BF} = 32.4$), and line 134 states that all location-level predictors are listed in Table 3, but Table 3 lists only `orTT100k` and `destinationTT100k` and omits `tt50K`. Add `tt50K` to Table 3.
  4. **Broken GitHub URLs in Methods (lines 79, 89, 146):** Lines 79 and 89 write `https://github/ebov/space-time/data/` (missing `.com`), and line 146 has a trailing parenthesis `https://github.com/ebov/space-time)`. Correct all three to `https://github.com/ebov/space-time`.
