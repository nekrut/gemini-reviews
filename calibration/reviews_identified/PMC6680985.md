# Review of PMC6680985

**Manuscript Title:** Insights into the Potential of the Atlantic Cod Gut Microbiome as Biomarker of Oil Contamination in the Marine Environment  
**Authors:** Juline M. Walter, Andrea Bagi, Daniela M. Pampanin  
**Journal / Preprint:** Microorganisms (2019) | DOI: 10.3390/microorganisms7070209  
**Overall Score:** **3.6 / 10.0** (Flawed / Overstated)  
* **Score Breakdown:** Rigor: 0.9/3.5 | Claims: 0.7/2.5 | Biology: 0.9/2.0 | Reproducibility: 1.1/2.0

### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

Walter et al. re-analyze 13 publicly available 16S rRNA V4 amplicon sequencing datasets from the gut microbiota of Atlantic cod (*Gadus morhua*) drawn from two prior studies: 7 wild-caught cod from northern Norway (`PRJEB22384`, Riiser et al.: 4 from Lofoten, 3 from Sørøya) and 6 laboratory-maintained cod from southern Norway (`PRJEB21667`, Bagi et al.: 3 unexposed control fish and 3 fish experimentally exposed to 0.01 ppm dispersed crude oil in laboratory tanks). Using both an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) pipeline (DADA2 with SILVA v132) and an operational taxonomic unit (OTU) pipeline (QIIME v1.8 / UPARSE with SILVA v119), they evaluate α-diversity, β-diversity (Bray–Curtis and weighted UniFrac PCoA/NMDS, PERMANOVA $R^2 = 0.467, p < 0.002$), and differential taxon abundance via DESeq2. Although no genus-level differences survive Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction, the authors highlight unadjusted $p \le 0.05$ differences and the presence of low-count OTUs assigned to putative hydrocarbon-degrading genera (*Novosphingobium*, *Sphingobium*, *Sphingomonas*, *Alcanivorax*, *Cycloclasticus*, and family *Deferribacteraceae*) in the 6 southern Norway samples, concluding that these taxa reflect higher anthropogenic oil pollution in southern Norwegian waters and can serve as gut microbiome biomarkers of marine oil contamination.

While developing non-destructive microbiome bioindicators for marine polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) exposure is a worthwhile ecotoxicological goal, the study design and statistical interpretation are fundamentally flawed. First, comparing 7 *wild-caught* Arctic cod from Study A (`PRJEB22384`) against 6 *laboratory-acclimated* North Sea cod (half of which were directly dosed with 0.01 ppm crude oil in tanks) from Study B (`PRJEB21667`) completely confounds geographic pollution differences with laboratory captivity/diet, experimental tank oil dosing, water temperature/latitudinal biogeography, and DNA extraction/sequencing batch effects. Second, zero taxa survive Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction across the two groups ($N = 7$ vs. $N = 6$), yet the authors explicitly disregard the multiple-testing-corrected results (`"we were motivated to interpret the results beyond the traditional p value"`) and switch from DADA2 ASVs back to legacy QIIME 1.8 OTUs (and raw read counts of 1–3 reads) when the ASV pipeline fails to support their candidate PAH degraders. Because the study re-uses only 13 mismatched archival samples with no chemical measurements of environmental or biliary PAHs, the data cannot support claims of environmental oil-contamination biomarkers without new, matched field sampling or a strictly controlled meta-analysis.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Gut microbial community composition (β-diversity) differs significantly between the northern Norway (`PRJEB22384`) and southern Norway (`PRJEB21667`) cod samples (PERMANOVA $R^2 = 0.467, p < 0.002$): [Well Supported].**  
  Ordination of Bray–Curtis and weighted UniFrac distances (Figure 2) and PERMANOVA confirm a large compositional separation between the 7 samples from `PRJEB22384` (dominated by *Fusobacteria* and *Photobacterium*) and the 6 samples from `PRJEB21667` (dominated by *Firmicutes*). However, this separation simply reflects the combined effect of Study A vs. Study B (wild-caught vs. laboratory-maintained fish, different DNA extraction protocols, and $70^\circ\text{N}$ vs. $59^\circ\text{N}$ latitude) rather than ambient marine oil contamination.

* **Differences in gut microbiota and presence of hydrocarbonoclastic genera in the southern Norway samples are caused by anthropogenic marine oil contamination in southern Norway: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  Three severe confounders invalidate attributing differences between the two datasets to ambient marine oil pollution in southern Norway:
  1. *Wild vs. laboratory captivity:* The 7 northern cod were sampled directly from the wild, whereas the 6 southern cod were held in laboratory tanks prior to sampling, a transition well known to reshape fish gut microbiota via tank water, fasting/artificial diet, and confinement stress.
  2. *Inclusion of experimentally oil-dosed fish:* Three of the 6 southern Norway samples were experimentally exposed to 0.01 ppm crude oil in the laboratory (`low` group in Figure 2c). Pooling 3 laboratory oil-dosed fish with 3 tank controls and attributing their microbial profile to *ambient environmental pollution* in southern Norwegian fjords is circular.
  3. *Complete study/batch collinearity and absence of PAH measurements:* All northern samples come from one ENA BioProject (`PRJEB22384`) and all southern samples come from another (`PRJEB21667`), with zero shared controls and zero chemical measurements of water, sediment, or fish bile PAH concentrations at the collection sites.

* **Sixteen bacterial genera and candidate biomarker taxa (*Alistipes*, *Anaerotruncus*, *Parabacteroides*, *Porphyromonas*, *Ruminococcus*, *Deferribacteraceae*) are significantly differentially abundant between southern and northern cod: [Overstated].**  
  In the Discussion (Section 4, line 69), the authors acknowledge that *"results analyzed for false discovery rate by Benjamini–Hochberg tests were all above the corrected p value."* With only $N = 7$ vs. $N = 6$ samples tested across dozens of genera, reporting unadjusted $p \le 0.05$ hits after all Benjamini–Hochberg $q$-values fail significance guarantees a high rate of false positives. Moreover, in Section 3.3 (line 62), the authors note that the putative PAH-degrading genera were *not* significant in the DADA2 ASV analysis, prompting them to switch to legacy QIIME 1.8 97% OTUs (against an older database, SILVA v119) to report unadjusted $p$-values of $0.01\text{--}0.05$ and singleton/doubleton read presences (1–3 reads).

* **16S rRNA V4 genus assignments (*Alcanivorax*, *Cycloclasticus*, *Novosphingobium*, *Sphingomonas*, *Lactobacillus*, *Ruminococcus*) identify active gut PAH biodegradation: [Fragile].**  
  Amplicon sequencing of the ~250 bp 16S rRNA V4 region cannot detect catabolic hydrocarbon-degradation genes (`alkB`, `ndoB`, `nidA`, `rhd`), which are frequently carried on mobile plasmids and vary across strains. Furthermore, most of the statistically highlighted genera in Section 3.3 (*Alistipes*, *Anaerotruncus*, *Parabacteroides*, *Porphyromonas*, *Ruminococcus*, *Lactobacillus*) are common anaerobic vertebrate gut commensals rather than marine hydrocarbonoclastic bacteria.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Separate the Unexposed Control Fish ($n=3$) from the Laboratory Oil-Dosed Fish ($n=3$) in All Analyses
* **WHY:** Combining 3 fish that were experimentally dosed with 0.01 ppm dispersed crude oil in laboratory tanks (`low`) with 3 unexposed fish (`ctrl`) to represent "natural southern Norway waters" contaminates the baseline environmental comparison with laboratory oil exposure.
* **WHAT:** Stratify the southern Norway dataset (`PRJEB21667`) into its true experimental groups (`ctrl`, $n=3$ vs. `low`, $n=3$—and include the $0.05$ and $0.1\text{ mg}\cdot\text{L}^{-1}$ oil-exposed groups from the same `PRJEB21667` study as positive exposure references) rather than pooling `ctrl` and `low`.
* **HOW:** Re-run the PCoA ordinations, PERMANOVA, and DESeq2 contrasts across all treatment arms of `PRJEB21667` alongside `PRJEB22384` (`Northern_Wild` vs. `Southern_Lab_Ctrl` vs. `Southern_Lab_0.01ppm` vs. `Southern_Lab_0.05ppm` vs. `Southern_Lab_0.1ppm`). This will immediately test whether the putative PAH-degrading taxa track the controlled laboratory oil dose within `PRJEB21667` or merely reflect a baseline batch/captivity difference between the two studies.

#### 2. Report Benjamini–Hochberg Adjusted $q$-Values and Harmonize the ASV and OTU Pipelines
* **WHY:** Switching between DADA2 ASVs (SILVA v132) in Section 3.2 and QIIME 1.8 OTUs (SILVA v119) in Section 3.3—and reporting only unadjusted $p \le 0.05$ values when all FDR-adjusted $q$-values are non-significant—creates analytical cherry-picking.
* **WHAT:** Use a single, consistent taxonomic database (SILVA v132) for both ASVs and OTUs, and report exact read counts, log2 fold changes, unadjusted $p$-values, and Benjamini–Hochberg $q$-values side-by-side in a unified table.
* **HOW:** Add a summary table in the main text listing every genus claimed in Sections 3.2 and 3.3, showing its mean/median read count in `Northern_Wild` ($n=7$), `Southern_Ctrl` ($n=3$), and `Southern_LowOil` ($n=3$), its DESeq2 $\log_2\text{FC}$, raw $p$-value, and BH-adjusted $q$-value under DADA2 ASVs, and explicitly label all taxa with $q > 0.05$ as exploratory/non-significant trends.

#### 3. Reframe Title, Abstract, and Conclusions to Acknowledge Study/Captivity Confounding
* **WHY:** Because all 6 southern Norway fish were maintained in laboratory tanks and sequenced in a separate study (`PRJEB21667`) from the 7 wild-caught northern fish (`PRJEB22384`), the data cannot isolate environmental marine oil pollution from captivity, diet, temperature, and batch effects.
* **WHAT & HOW:** Revise the Abstract, Section 4, and Section 5 to state explicitly that the northern vs. southern comparison is a cross-study exploratory comparison confounded by laboratory maintenance versus wild capture, and frame the manuscript as a preliminary feasibility assessment highlighting the need for matched wild-caught sampling with concurrent biliary PAH metabolite measurements.
