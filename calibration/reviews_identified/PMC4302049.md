### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

Love, Huber, and Anders present DESeq2, an empirical Bayes negative binomial generalized linear model (GLM) framework for differential analysis of high-throughput sequencing count data. Building on the median-of-ratios size factor normalization of DESeq, DESeq2 introduces: (1) empirical Bayes shrinkage of gene-wise Cox–Reid adjusted dispersion estimates ($\alpha_i^{\text{gw}}$) toward a parametric mean-dependent trend $\alpha_{\text{tr}}(\bar{\mu}_i) = a_1/\bar{\mu}_i + \alpha_0$ using a log-normal prior whose variance $\sigma_d^2 = \max(s_{\text{lr}}^2 - \psi_1((m-p)/2), 0.25)$ subtracts the theoretical trigamma sampling variance of log-$\chi^2_{m-p}$ estimators while exempting upper dispersion outliers ($>2 s_{\text{lr}}$ above the trend); (2) empirical Bayes ridge shrinkage of logarithmic fold changes ($\beta_{ir}$) using a zero-centered normal prior matched to the top 5% empirical MLE quantiles across expanded design matrices that preserve level symmetry; (3) Wald tests for both point ($\beta_{ir} = 0$) and composite thresholded null hypotheses ($|\beta_{ir}| \le \theta$ and $|\beta_{ir}| \ge \theta$), paired with Basu's theorem-justified independent filtering on mean normalized count and Cook's distance outlier handling; and (4) a regularized logarithm (`rlog`) transformation that accounts for sample-specific size factors. The authors evaluate DESeq2 on negative binomial simulations ($10,000$ genes, $m \in \{6, 8, 10, 20\}$) and empirical RNA-seq datasets (Pickrell et al. human lymphoblastoid cell lines for null 5 vs. 5 permutations; Bottomly et al. mouse strains for 3 vs. 3 discovery against 7 vs. 8 verification splits; Hammer et al. rat dorsal root ganglion for `rlog` clustering), providing complete Sweave reproducibility vignettes in the `DESeq2paper` package.

This is an exemplary methodological manuscript characterized by mathematical transparency, careful attention to edge cases (such as expanded design matrices to prevent reference-level asymmetry under ridge penalties, and disabling LFC shrinkage when testing equivalence $|\beta_{ir}| \ge \theta$), and symmetric benchmarking across competing tools (`edgeR`, `edgeR-robust`, `DSS`, `voom`, `EBSeq`, `SAMseq`, `Cuffdiff 2`). The few statistical limitations are subtle and fully addressable: (1) the Wald test plugs the final MAP dispersion $\alpha_i^{\text{MAP}}$ into the sandwich covariance matrix $\Sigma_i = (X^t W X + \vec{\lambda} I)^{-1} (X^t W X) (X^t W X + \vec{\lambda} I)^{-1}$ as if $\alpha_i^{\text{MAP}}$ were known without error and compares the ratio $z = \hat{\beta}_{ir} / \text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_{ir})$ to a standard normal $N(0,1)$ rather than a $t$-distribution, which can be slightly anti-conservative at very small sample sizes ($m - p \le 4$); (2) unlike the dispersion prior width $\sigma_d^2$, the LFC prior width $\sigma_r^2$ is estimated from the 95th percentile of noisy raw MLEs without deconvolving their sampling variance; and (3) choosing the independent filtering cutoff to maximize the number of rejections on the observed $P$-value vector introduces a small post-selection optimization bias.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Empirical Bayes shrinkage of Cox–Reid dispersion estimates with a trigamma sampling-variance subtraction and an upper-outlier escape rule controls false positives while boosting sensitivity over the original DESeq maximum rule:** **[Well Supported].**  
  Both negative binomial simulations (Fig. 6) and 30 mock 5-vs-5 null splits of the Pickrell et al. dataset (Fig. 7) show that DESeq2 controls the empirical false positive rate near the nominal $\alpha = 0.01$ and FDR $= 0.10$ thresholds while eliminating the severe conservatism of original DESeq.

* **Zero-centered normal prior shrinkage of LFC estimates substantially improves split-sample fold-change reproducibility and top-gene ranking stability without sacrificing high-count accuracy:** **[Well Supported].**  
  In balanced split-half comparisons of the Bottomly et al. dataset (Fig. 3 and Fig. S3), MAP LFC shrinkage reduces root-mean-square error between independent halves and increases top-100 rank overlap from 21/100 (unshrunken MLE) to 81/100, while outperforming fixed pseudocount ratios (`edgeR` predictive LFC) across non-differentially expressed genes and outperforming `GFOLD` on differentially expressed genes at larger sample sizes (Figs. S12–S16).

* **The regularized logarithm (`rlog`) transformation stabilizes variance across the count dynamic range and outperforms standard $\log_2(K_{ij}/s_j + 1)$ and unweighted VST when library size factors vary widely:** **[Well Supported].**  
  Simulation benchmarks varying size-factor dynamic range (Fig. S17, evaluated via adjusted Rand index) and empirical hierarchical clustering of the Hammer et al. dataset (Fig. 5) confirm that `rlog` prevents both low-count noise inflation and library-size distortion.

* **The ridge-shrunken Wald statistic $\hat{\beta}_{ir}^{\text{MAP}} / \text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_{ir}^{\text{MAP}})$ follows an exact standard normal distribution under the null hypothesis:** **[Moderately Supported].**  
  Using the ridge sandwich covariance $\Sigma_i = (X^t W X + \vec{\lambda} I)^{-1} (X^t W X) (X^t W X + \vec{\lambda} I)^{-1}$ (Part 2, line 292) ensures that the shrinkage scaling cancels to first order in the ratio $\hat{\beta}_{ir} / \sqrt{\Sigma_{i,rr}}$ for a single contrast. However, treating $\alpha_i^{\text{MAP}}$ (and hence $W$) as fixed neglects dispersion estimation uncertainty in small designs (e.g., 2 vs. 2 or 3 vs. 3), and comparing a plug-in Wald ratio to $N(0,1)$ rather than a $t$-distribution with effective degrees of freedom slightly inflates tail probabilities when residual degrees of freedom $m - p$ are small.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Deconvolve Sampling Noise When Estimating the LFC Prior Variance $\sigma_r^2$
* **WHY:** In the "Empirical prior estimate" subsection (Part 2, line 229), the authors explicitly note that—unlike for dispersion prior estimation—they do not subtract the expected sampling variance from the empirical distribution of $\beta_{ir}^{\text{MLE}}$ when matching the 95% quantile to set $\sigma_r$. In datasets with very small sample sizes or low sequencing depth, the 95th percentile of $\beta_{ir}^{\text{MLE}}$ is dominated by Poisson/NB sampling noise from low-count genes, making $\sigma_r^2$ too wide and under-shrinking noisy LFCs.
* **WHAT:** Weight or filter the empirical quantile matching of $\beta_{ir}^{\text{MLE}}$ by the inverse Fisher information (or restrict quantile calculation to genes above the median normalized count), or deconvolve the expected MLE variance $1 / J_m(\hat{\beta}_{ir})$.
* **HOW:** Compute the 95% quantile $Q_{|\beta_r|}(0.95)$ weighting each gene $i$ by bounded Fisher information $w_i = \min(J_m(\hat{\beta}_{ir}), c)$ (or subtracting the median asymptotic variance $\text{median}_i[(X^t W_i X)^{-1}_{rr}]$ from the matched variance), and compare LFC RMSE on the low-count simulation strata in Figs. S12–S16.

#### 2. Evaluate a $t$-Distribution Approximation for the Wald Statistic at Low Degrees of Freedom ($m - p \le 4$)
* **WHY:** While the log-normal dispersion prior borrows strength across genes, individual dispersion outliers (which bypass shrinkage when $\log \alpha_i^{\text{gw}} > \log \alpha_{\text{tr}}(\bar{\mu}_i) + 2 s_{\text{lr}}$) rely solely on $m - p$ gene-wise degrees of freedom. Evaluating the Wald statistic against $N(0,1)$ for unshrunken outlier genes in a 3-vs-3 design ignores the heavy tails induced by estimating $\alpha_i^{\text{gw}}$ from $m - p = 4$ degrees of freedom.
* **WHAT:** Compare the normal tail integral against a Student's $t$-distribution with $df = m - p$ (for unshrunken dispersion outliers) or empirical-Bayes effective degrees of freedom $df_{\text{eff}} = (m - p) + 2 / (\sigma_d^2 \cdot \psi_1((m-p)/2))$ (for MAP-shrunken genes).
* **HOW:** On the 3-vs-3 evaluation splits of the Bottomly dataset and the $m = 6$ simulation benchmark (Fig. 6), compute FDR and sensitivity using $t_{df}$ versus $N(0,1)$ specifically stratified by whether genes were flagged as dispersion outliers.

#### 3. Quantify the Threshold-Optimization Bias in Automatic Independent Filtering
* **WHY:** While Basu's theorem guarantees that for any *fixed* threshold $\theta_{\text{filter}}$ on $\bar{\mu}_i$, the filtered null $P$-values remain uniform, choosing $\hat{\theta}_{\text{filter}}$ dynamically to maximize the number of BH rejections on the realized data (Part 1, line 115) takes the supremum of an empirical process over the filter grid, which can slightly inflate the realized FDR when true signal is zero or sparse.
* **WHAT:** Quantify the null rejection rate of the greedy threshold maximization on a purely null dataset ($0\%$ DE genes) and document the smoothing/quantile rule used to stabilize threshold selection.
* **HOW:** Run the default automatic independent filtering pipeline on the 30 Pickrell 5-vs-5 null permutations at target $\text{FDR} = 0.10$ and report the fraction of permutations yielding $>0$ false discoveries with vs. without greedy threshold optimization.
