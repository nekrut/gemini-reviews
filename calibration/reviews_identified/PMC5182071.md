### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

Field et al. introduce the Singleton Density Score (SDS), a coalescent-based method for inferring recent allele frequency changes (~75 generations, or ~2,000–3,000 years in humans) from contemporary whole-genome sequences. Because recent positive selection shortens the terminal (tip) branches of the genealogy carrying the favored allele, haplotypes bearing the favored variant accumulate fewer singleton mutations; SDS uses the flanking distances to the nearest singletons across individuals of each genotype at a test SNP to compute a maximum-likelihood estimate of the log-ratio of mean tip-branch lengths for derived versus ancestral alleles, standardized to zero mean and unit variance within derived allele frequency (DAF) bins. Applying SDS to 3,195 whole genomes from the UK10K Project across 4.5 million autosomal SNPs (MAF > 5%), the authors identify genome-wide significant single-locus selection signals at the lactase (*LCT*, $p = 1 \times 10^{-23}$), *MHC* ($p = 2 \times 10^{-15}$), and *WDFY4* ($p = 3 \times 10^{-11}$) loci, as well as enrichment across pigmentation variants ($p = 3 \times 10^{-9}$). Polarizing SDS by the sign of GWAS effect sizes (trait-SDS, or tSDS), they further report genome-wide polygenic adaptation signals for increased height (mean $\text{tSDS} = 0.30, p = 4 \times 10^{-11}$ at 551 GWAS hits; Spearman $\rho = 0.078, p = 9 \times 10^{-74}$ across all SNPs using GIANT, and $\rho = 0.094, p = 9 \times 10^{-163}$ in a family-based GWAS) and nominal polygenic signals across several additional complex traits. Precomputed SDS scores and open-source implementation code are publicly archived on Dryad and GitHub.

The formulation of SDS is a creative, mathematically well-grounded methodological advance in population genetics that fills a critical temporal gap (~50–100 generations) where haplotype homozygosity tests such as iHS lack specificity and statistical power. The single-locus inferences and oligogenic pigmentation enrichments are strongly supported because the derived and ancestral alleles at each SNP serve as internal matched controls for local mutation rate, recombination rate, and sequencing accessibility. However, the genome-wide polygenic adaptation claims (Figure 4) rest on aggregating tiny directional biases across millions of non-significant SNPs ($\rho \approx 0.08$), a regime where subtle, uncorrected north-south European population stratification in GWAS summary statistics or spatial/coverage heterogeneity in singleton detection across the UK10K sub-cohorts can generate strong false-positive correlations between GWAS effect signs and SDS. While the authors appropriately perform block-jackknife, family-GWAS, and bivariate LD score regression checks and explicitly acknowledge in the final paragraph that residual population structure remains an open question, the abstract and main text frame pervasive polygenic adaptation on height and other complex traits more definitively than warranted by the fragility of whole-genome sign correlations to stratification.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **SDS accurately estimates recent terminal-branch length distortions (~75 generations) and substantially outperforms iHS for recent selection on standing variation: [Well Supported].**  
  Coalescent simulations across continuous sweeps, standing variation sweeps starting 100 generations ago, and historical sweeps stopping >100 generations ago (Figure 2, A–C) confirm that SDS has high temporal specificity to the tip-branch coalescent epoch (~2,000–3,000 years for $N = 3,000$) and follows a standard normal distribution under neutral and admixed demographic models. The empirical biased-subsampling control in UK10K ($N = 1,500$ drawn from $3,195$ to induce artificial 1–10% allele frequency shifts; Figure 2D) provides an elegant internal validation showing a linear response (~0.3–0.4 SD per 1% frequency shift) where iHS has zero power.

* **Strong positive selection acted on lactase persistence (*LCT*), the *MHC* region, and lighter hair/eye pigmentation variants in the ancestors of modern Britons within the past 2,000–3,000 years: [Well Supported].**  
  The single-locus signals at *LCT* ($p = 1 \times 10^{-23}$) and *MHC* ($p = 2 \times 10^{-15}$) exceed genome-wide significance by wide margins, and the genotype-stratified singleton distance distributions at *LCT* (Figure 3B) directly mirror theoretical predictions (Figure 1E). Likewise, the curated set of well-characterized large-effect pigmentation loci (Figure 3C, $p = 3 \times 10^{-9}$) relies on biologically validated functional variants rather than noisy genome-wide background polygenic tails.

* **Polygenic selection for increased height drove directed allele frequency shifts across most of the genome (85% of all SNPs): [Fragile].**  
  While the mean tSDS across the 551 genome-wide significant height SNPs ($0.30, p = 4 \times 10^{-11}$; Figure 3D) is notable, extending the test to rank-correlate tSDS with GWAS $Z$-scores across all 4.5 million SNPs ($\rho = 0.078, p = 9 \times 10^{-74}$; Figure 4A) makes the inference exquisitely sensitive to residual population structure. As shown in Figure 2E, SDS correlates strongly with north-south European allele frequency clines ($\rho = 0.32 \pm 0.005$). Because tSDS polarizes SDS solely by $\text{sign}(Z_{\text{GWAS}})$, even a tiny uncorrected north-south stratification bias in the GIANT meta-analysis aligns $\text{sign}(Z_{\text{GWAS}})$ with the north-south cline across thousands of LD blocks. Although the authors check a smaller family-based GWAS and bivariate LD score regression (Figure 4, A and B), bivariate LD score regression assumes stratification effects are independent of LD score—an assumption that breaks down under background selection, assortative mating, or spatially structured coalescent times where variants in higher-LD regions also exhibit larger clinal variance.

* **Polygenic adaptation has pervasively shaped a broad suite of human complex traits (head circumference, birth weight, female hip size, metabolic traits, and menarche) over the past 2,000 years: [Overstated].**  
  In Figure 4C, the authors screen 43 traits from consortia GWAS summary statistics that lack family-based structure controls, relying on nominal ($p < 0.05$, one-sided) bivariate LD score regression to validate hits. Given that consortium GWAS summary statistics for anthropometric, reproductive, and metabolic traits contain variable degrees of European north-south stratification—and that multiple regression (fig. S28) shows none of the traits uniquely explains the shared covariance—attributing these genome-wide tSDS correlations to pervasive recent natural selection rather than residual stratification or pleiotropic clinal structure overstates the empirical certainty.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Control Directly for North-South European Clinal Allele Frequency Differences in Genome-Wide tSDS Tests
* **WHY:** Figure 2E demonstrates that SDS is strongly correlated ($\rho = 0.32$) with the allele frequency difference between northern (GBR/CEU) and southern (TSI/IBS) European populations ($\Delta f_{\text{N-S}}$). If GWAS summary statistics harbor residual north-south stratification, $\text{sign}(Z_{\text{GWAS}})$ will correlate with $\Delta f_{\text{N-S}}$ and thereby induce a spurious positive correlation with SDS across the genome even if all terminal branch length differences reflect historical migration/admixture rather than recent selection within Britain.
* **WHAT:** Re-evaluate the height and 43-trait polygenic tSDS correlations after conditioning on or residualizing out empirical European clinal frequency differences ($\Delta f_{\text{GBR}-\text{TSI}}$ and $\Delta f_{\text{CEU}-\text{IBS}}$) and UK regional principal components.
* **HOW:** Using the 1000 Genomes European populations already analyzed in Figure 2E (plus UK10K top PCs), compute partial Spearman rank correlations (with block-jackknife standard errors) between $Z_{\text{GWAS}}$ and SDS conditional on $\Delta f_{\text{N-S}}$ and UK10K PC loadings, and verify whether the height signal in Figure 4A–B remains significant when restricted to SNPs with minimal north-south allele frequency divergence ($|\Delta f_{\text{N-S}}| < 0.02$).

#### 2. Audit Singleton Calling Sensitivity to Sequencing Depth, Cohort Batch (TwinsUK vs. ALSPAC), and Fine-Scale UK Geography
* **WHY:** UK10K (~7x whole-genome sequencing) merges two distinct cohorts (TwinsUK and ALSPAC) sequenced across multiple centers and time periods. Because singleton detection at ~7x depth has a non-trivial false-negative and false-positive rate that depends on per-sample coverage and quality, any slight difference in mean singleton count per individual between TwinsUK and ALSPAC—or across geographic regions of the UK—will shorten or lengthen apparent tip distances on haplotypes that differ subtly in frequency between the sub-cohorts.
* **WHAT:** Quantify the correlation between SDS computed separately within TwinsUK and ALSPAC, and test whether individual-level singleton density correlates with UK10K principal components or sequencing depth.
* **HOW:** Split the 3,195 UK10K individuals into the TwinsUK and ALSPAC sub-cohorts, compute SDS independently in each half, and report (1) the genome-wide correlation of SDS between the two cohorts, (2) whether normalizing each individual's singleton distances by their genome-wide singleton count alters the polygenic tSDS correlations in Figure 4, and (3) whether regressing individual singleton density against the top 20 UK10K genetic PCs reveals geographic gradients in tip-branch lengths.

#### 3. Propagate Tip-Length Estimation Uncertainty into Downstream Locus and Polygenic Tests
* **WHY:** At each SNP, SDS replaces the full likelihood profile of the tip-length log-ratio with a single point maximum-likelihood estimate (Figure 1F) standardized within 1% DAF bins. However, SNPs in regions of low recombination or low local singleton density have much wider likelihood surfaces (higher estimation variance) than SNPs in high-density regions, and treating all DAF-standardized point estimates as homoscedastic ignores locus-specific information content.
* **WHAT:** Report the Fisher information / asymptotic standard error of the unstandardized log-ratio MLE at each SNP and weight downstream polygenic regressions accordingly.
* **HOW:** Extract the second derivative of the log-likelihood curve from the existing SDS optimization step at each SNP to obtain its standard error $\text{SE}_i$, verify that weighting SNPs by $\text{SE}_i^{-2}$ (or filtering out the lowest-information quintile of genomic windows) preserves the single-locus and polygenic results, and include $\text{SE}_i$ in the publicly released Dryad table.

#### 4. Temper Abstract and Main-Text Framing of Genome-Wide Polygenic Adaptation
* **WHY:** The Abstract states without qualification that *"recent selection for increased height has driven allele frequency shifts across most of the genome"* and *"polygenic adaptation has played a pervasive role in shaping genotypic and phenotypic variation in modern humans,"* whereas the final sentence of the main text acknowledges that *"the confounding role—if any—of population structure in contributing to these signals remains to be fully determined."*
* **WHAT & HOW:** Revise the Abstract and lines 99–105 to explicitly distinguish between (1) the well-supported single-locus and oligogenic signals (*LCT*, *MHC*, pigmentation, and top GWAS loci) and (2) the whole-genome polygenic rank correlations across all 4.5 million SNPs, noting in the Abstract that whole-genome tSDS correlations can be amplified by subtle alignment between GWAS stratification and European clinal structure.
