### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

Sebastiani et al. perform a genome-wide association study (GWAS) and polygenic classification analysis of exceptional longevity using 243,980 SNPs in a discovery cohort of 801 centenarians from the New England Centenarian Study (NECS; median age 104 years) and 914 PCA-cluster-matched controls (241 NECS controls and 673 Illumina `iControlDB` controls), followed by evaluation in two replication cohorts: Replication Set 1 (`ELIX`; 253 nonagenarians/centenarians, median age 100 years, and 341 matched `iControlDB` controls) and Replication Set 2 (60 unmatched NECS centenarians, median age 107 years, and 2,863 unmatched `iControlDB` controls). In single-SNP analyses, only one locus—`rs2075650` in `TOMM40/APOE`—reaches genome-wide significance ($\text{OR} = 0.49$, $p = 1.03 \times 10^{-8}$ in Table 1) and replicates across cohorts. The authors then rank sub-threshold SNPs by their discovery-set maximum Bayes Factor (MBF), construct an ensemble of 281 nested naïve Bayes classifiers ($\Sigma_1 \subset \Sigma_2 \subset \dots \subset \Sigma_{281}$), and cluster the resulting 281-point cumulative posterior probability curves using `CAGED` into 26 "genetic signatures." While the 281-SNP ensemble achieves 89% sensitivity and 89% specificity in the discovery set, performance drops to 60% sensitivity and 58% specificity ($\text{AUC} = 0.58$) in Replication Set 1, and 78% sensitivity with 61.2% specificity ($\text{AUC} = 0.74$) in Replication Set 2. Summary statistics for the 281 SNPs are provided in Table S1, though individual-level genotypes and analysis scripts are not deposited in a public repository.

While the replication of `TOMM40/APOE` (`rs2075650`) and the observation that centenarians carry a similar burden of common disease-risk alleles as controls (Figure 10) are solid empirical contributions, the multi-SNP risk modeling and "genetic signature" clustering suffer from severe methodological flaws: (1) systematic confounding between case/control status and genotyping platform/laboratory across all three cohorts (Table 2), coupled with permissive quality-control thresholds; (2) substantial overfitting from selecting 280 sub-threshold SNPs on the discovery set, evidenced by near-chance discrimination ($\text{AUC} = 0.58$) in the primary matched replication set; and (3) a mathematical artifact in the `CAGED` signature analysis, which clusters cumulative partial-product trajectories whose shape is dictated by the arbitrary MBF ordering of the first ~20 SNPs rather than distinct biological pathways, followed by unadjusted pairwise log-rank survival tests across 26 clusters. Addressing these issues requires restricting training and validation to platform-matched controls, replacing cumulative trajectory clustering with order-invariant genotype clustering, and applying multiple-testing corrections to all post-hoc subgroup and survival comparisons.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **SNP rs2075650 in TOMM40/APOE is significantly associated with exceptional longevity and replicates across independent cohorts: [Well Supported].**  
  The depletion of the `rs2075650` G allele in centenarians (15% vs. 26% in controls, $\text{OR} = 0.49$) reaches genome-wide significance in the discovery set, replicates in the matched ELIX cohort ($\text{OR} = 0.47$, $p = 4.68 \times 10^{-4}$) and against external Coriell NINDS controlsgenotyped on the same 370k platform ($\text{OR} = 0.55$, $p = 3.86 \times 10^{-6}$; Table 1), and was orthogonally verified via blinded TaqMan genotyping.

* **Centenarians and controls carry comparable aggregate frequencies of known common disease-associated risk variants: [Moderately Supported].**  
  Across 1,214 GWAS Catalog and HGMD disease-associated SNPs present on the arrays (Figure 10, Table S3), centenarians carry nearly the same distribution of risk alleles as controls, with only 13 individual disease SNPs showing significant depletion in centenarians (Table S4). Support is moderated by incomplete coverage of known disease loci on the 243,980-SNP intersection panel.

* **An ensemble of 281 nested naïve Bayes SNP models accurately discriminates exceptional longevity from average longevity: [Overstated].**  
  Of the 281 SNPs in the classifier, 280 fail to reach genome-wide significance in the discovery set. Consequently, the 89% sensitivity and 89% specificity in the discovery set largely reflect in-sample winner's curse from aggregating 280 noisy features. In the primary independent matched replication cohort (`ELIX`, $n=253$ cases, $n=341$ controls), accuracy drops to 60% sensitivity and 58% specificity ($\text{AUC} = 0.58$). More critically, Table 2 reveals pervasive array and laboratory confounding across all cohorts: 100% of centenarians (`NECS` and `ELIXIR`) were genotyped at Boston University on Illumina `370`, `610`, or `1M` arrays, whereas 74% of Discovery controls (`Illumina I`), 100% of Replication Set 1 controls (`Illumina II`), and 100% of Replication Set 2 controls (`Illumina III`) came from `iControlDB` and were genotyped at unknown labs on Illumina `300` and `550` arrays. Because the QC pipeline allowed Hardy–Weinberg $\chi^2$ statistics up to 50 ($p \approx 1.5 \times 10^{-12}$) and pairwise array minor allele frequency differences up to 0.20, even subtle platform-specific allele-frequency biases between `370/610/1M` (cases) and `300/550` (`iControlDB` controls) will artificially inflate discrimination in *both* the discovery and replication sets. Furthermore, 21 of the 60 centenarians in Replication Set 2 were pre-existing NECS samples that had been excluded from the discovery set only due to PCA matching, and a specificity of 58%–61% implies a 39%–42% false-positive rate for a phenotype with a true population prevalence of $0.02\%$ ($1/5,000$).

* **Centenarians partition into 26 reproducible "genetic signatures" that correlate with distinct lifespans and age-related disease sub-phenotypes: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  To construct "genetic signatures," the authors plot each subject's cumulative posterior probability $y_k = P(\text{EL} \mid \Sigma_k)$ as SNPs $k = 1, \dots, 281$ are added one by one in order of their discovery-set MBF, and then cluster these 281-point trajectories using 4th-order polynomials in `CAGED` (Figure 8). Because $\Sigma_k$ is a nested cumulative product, the log-odds $\sum_{i=1}^k \log [P(g_i \mid \text{EL}) / P(g_i \mid \text{AL})]$ forms a sequential random walk that saturates near $P \approx 0$ or $P \approx 1$ once $k \gtrsim 30$. Consequently, the trajectory shapes clustered by `CAGED` (e.g., whether a curve dips at $k=5$ before rising at $k=15$) are an artifact of the arbitrary ranking order of the first ~20 SNPs and the heavy over-weighting of early SNPs in the ensemble average $\frac{1}{281}\sum_{k=1}^{281} P(\text{EL} \mid \Sigma_k)$ (where $\text{SNP}_1$ is weighted $281\times$ more heavily than $\text{SNP}_{281}$). Finally, the lifespan comparisons in Figure 9 report unadjusted log-rank $p$-values ($p = 0.01$ for C1 vs. C2; $p = 0.02$ for C1 vs. C26; $p = 0.10$ for R1 vs. R2) cherry-picked from $\binom{26}{2} = 325$ possible cluster pairs without multiple-testing correction.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Eliminate Array/Laboratory Confounding by Evaluating the Classifier on Platform-Matched Controls
* **WHY:** In Table 2, every centenarian was genotyped at BU on Illumina `370/610/1M` arrays, whereas all `iControlDB` controls (`Illumina I, II, III`) were genotyped on `300/550` arrays at unknown centers, with QC permitting inter-array MAF differences up to 0.20 and HWE $\chi^2 < 50$. Aggregating 281 SNPs can easily accumulate small technical allele-frequency shifts between `370/610/1M` and `300/550` that replicate across Discovery, Replication 1, and Replication 2 simply because all three sets share the same case-versus-control array split.
* **WHAT:** Re-evaluate the 281-SNP classifier (and single-SNP associations) using controls genotyped on the exact same array type (`Illumina 370`) as the cases, and tighten SNP QC filters.
* **HOW:** (i) Restrict Discovery cases ($n=583$ on `370`) and Replication 1 `ELIX` cases ($n=209$ on `370`) to the `370` array and evaluate sensitivity, specificity, and AUC against the `237` NECS `370` controls and the `867` Coriell NINDS `370` controls (completely excluding `300/550` `iControlDB` samples); (ii) tighten HWE QC in controls from $\chi^2 < 50$ to $\chi^2 < 10.83$ ($p > 0.001$) and inter-array MAF difference from $<0.20$ to $<0.02$; and (iii) TaqMan-genotype the remaining 253 model SNPs (or a random subset) in the `iControlDB` or Coriell controls to verify that no `300/550` vs. `370/610/1M` calling artifacts exist among the 281 SNPs.

#### 2. Replace the Nested Cumulative Ensemble with a Single Calibrated Model and Nested Cross-Validation
* **WHY:** Averaging $P(\text{EL} \mid \Sigma_k)$ over $k=1, \dots, 281$ implicitly assigns weight $(282-k)/281$ to the $k$-th SNP, and selecting $K=281$ by inspecting discovery-set sensitivity and specificity curves (or resampling after ranking all 243,980 SNPs on the full discovery set) leaks test-set information into feature selection.
* **WHAT:** Compare the single 281-SNP naïve Bayes model $P(\text{EL} \mid \Sigma_{281})$ and a standard $L_1/L_2$-penalized logistic regression against the nested ensemble using strict outer cross-validation where SNP ranking is performed exclusively inside each training fold.
* **HOW:** In the 1,000 random $2/3$ training / $1/3$ test splits of the discovery set, re-compute the single-SNP MBF across all 243,980 SNPs strictly within the $2/3$ training subset before selecting the top $K$ SNPs, and report the out-of-sample AUC, sensitivity, and specificity on the held-out $1/3$ test set as well as on ELIX. In addition, report positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV) evaluated at the true population prior $\text{Pr}(\text{EL}) = 1/5,000$ alongside $\text{Pr}(\text{EL}) = 0.5$.

#### 3. Replace Order-Dependent Cumulative Curve Clustering (`CAGED`) with Order-Invariant Genotype Clustering
* **WHY:** Clustering cumulative partial-product curves $y_k = P(\text{EL} \mid \Sigma_k)$ in `CAGED` treats an arbitrary SNP ranking index $k \in \{1, \dots, 281\}$ as a time axis, making cluster identity heavily dependent on the ordering of the first few SNPs.
* **WHAT:** Cluster subjects directly on their vector of 281 SNP log-likelihood ratios (which is invariant to SNP ordering) and apply formal multiple-testing correction to all cluster-level survival and phenotype comparisons.
* **HOW:** Represent each subject $i$ by the 281-dimensional vector $z_{ij} = \log [P(g_{ij} \mid \text{EL}) / P(g_{ij} \mid \text{AL})]$ for $j = 1, \dots, 281$ (without taking cumulative sums over $k$). Apply standard order-invariant clustering (e.g., Gaussian mixture modeling with BIC or consensus $k$-means) to $Z$. To test whether clusters differ in survival (Figure 9), perform a single global $K$-group log-rank omnibus test across all clusters first, followed by Holm–Bonferroni or Benjamini–Hochberg adjustment across all pairwise comparisons.

#### 4. Correct Literature Enrichment Analysis for Gene Length and Array SNP Density
* **WHY:** In Figure 5, the authors test whether the 130 genes harboring 137 of the 281 SNPs are enriched for Alzheimer's disease and coronary artery disease annotations using a standard Fisher's exact test in Genomatix. Because brain-expressed and dementia-associated genes (e.g., `SORCS1`, `SORCS2`) are exceptionally large and represented by many more SNPs on Illumina arrays than average genes, they have a much higher prior probability of containing at least one top-281 SNP by chance.
* **WHAT:** Re-compute disease-gene enrichment using a SNP-permutation null that accounts for gene size and SNP density per gene.
* **HOW:** Generate 10,000 null sets of 281 LD-pruned SNPs matched on minor allele frequency (or via label permutation of case/control status), map each null set to genes within 20 kb, and compute the empirical permutation $p$-value for observing $\ge 38$ Alzheimer's-linked and $\ge 24$ CAD-linked genes.

#### 5. Technical, Mathematical, and Editorial Cleanup
* **WHY:** Multiple discrepancies between the main text and tables, mislabeled table headers, and missing inline equations impede verification.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  1. **Discovery Bayes Factor and $p$-value mismatch for `rs2075650` (Text/Figure 3 vs. Table 1):** The Results text and Figure 3 caption state `log10(MBF) = 7.9 and p-value < e-10` for `rs2075650` in the discovery set, whereas Table 1 (row 1) lists `LOG10(BF) = 6.31` and `p-value = 1.03E-08`. Reconcile these values (clarifying if one is the allelic/general model and the other is the dominant model).
  2. **Repeated SNP rsID typo (`rs207650` vs. `rs2075650`):** In the Results text, Table 1 title, and Table 1 footnote, `rs2075650` is misspelled as `rs207650`. Correct all occurrences to `rs2075650`.
  3. **Mislabeled column headers in Tables 6, 7, and 8:**
     - In Table 6 (Allelic model), the two allele columns are both labeled `M` and `M`; change the second column header to `m`.
     - In Table 7 (Dominant model), the second column is labeled `Mm` (while containing `n13`, `n03`, `n.3`); change the header from `Mm` to `mm`.
     - In Table 8 (Recessive model), the two columns are both labeled `M` and `M` (while containing `n11` and `n12+n13`); change the headers to `MM` and `Mm/mm`.
  4. **Birth cohort year discrepancy:** The Results section states NECS discovery cases were born *"between 1890 and 1910"*, whereas Materials and Methods states *"between 1880 and 1910"*. Unify the birth year range (note that a 119-year-old centenarian enrolled in the late 1990s was born in ~1880).
