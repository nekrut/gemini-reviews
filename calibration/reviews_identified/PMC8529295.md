**Manuscript Title:** Unexpected detection of SARS-CoV-2 antibodies in the prepandemic period in Italy.  
**Authors:** Apolone G, Montomoli E, Manenti A, Boeri M, Sabia F, Hyseni I, Mazzini L, Martinuzzi D, Cantone L, Milanese G, Sestini S, Suatoni P, Marchianò A, Bollati V, Sozzi G, Pastorino U.

### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

Apolone et al. screen 959 stored plasma samples collected between September 2019 and February 2020 from asymptomatic heavy smokers (92.9% with $\ge 30$ pack-years; 63.2% aged 55–65 years) enrolled in a national lung cancer screening trial (SMILE; NCT03654105) headquartered in Milan, Italy. Using an in-house, proprietary SARS-CoV-2 receptor-binding domain (RBD) ELISA (`VM-IgG-RBD` and `VM-IgM-RBD`), the authors report RBD-binding antibodies in 111 of 959 participants (11.6%), driven predominantly by IgM (97/959, 10.1%) rather than IgG (16/959, 1.7%, with only 2/959 dual IgM+/IgG+). Monthly positivity rates are reported as 14.2% (23/162) in September 2019, 16.3% (27/166) in October, 9.5% (26/273) in November, 7.5% (11/147) in December, 2.8% (3/106) in January 2020, and 20.0% (21/105) in February 2020. Follow-up testing of the 111 ELISA-positive samples in a qualitative cytopathic effect (CPE) live-virus microneutralization assay yielded 6 positive samples (0.6% of the cohort; 4 from October, 1 from November, 1 from February). Raw optical density (OD) values and neutralization titers are not publicly deposited ("available upon request").

The manuscript's primary conclusion—that SARS-CoV-2 was circulating widely across Italy by early September 2019—is not supported by the presented data and rests on three methodological flaws: (1) complete absence of pre-pandemic negative control sera (e.g., 2015–2018 samples) or cross-reactivity panels against endemic seasonal human coronaviruses (`HCoV-OC43`, `229E`, `NL63`, `HKU1`) to establish the false-positive rate of the proprietary IgM ELISA in heavy smokers; (2) a recruitment base-rate artifact in which the claim that positive cases cluster in Lombardy (59/111, 53.2%) simply reflects the fact that 51.2% (491/959) of all study participants were recruited in Lombardy (giving an identical positivity rate of 12.0% inside Lombardy vs. 11.1% outside Lombardy, $p = 0.69$); and (3) internal immunological contradictions in Table 1, where a purported 14%–16% IgM seroprevalence in September–October 2019 never converts into rising IgG seroprevalence (which remains flat at 0.9%–2.0% through February 2020), declines to 2.8% in January 2020, fails to neutralize virus in 94.6% (105/111) of ELISA-positive cases, and exceeds Italy's post-first-wave national seroprevalence in May–July 2020 (2.5%) by sixfold. Resolving these flaws requires testing blinded pre-2018 negative control sera, orthogonal confirmatory assays against full-length Spike and Nucleocapsid antigens, and correcting the geographic base-rate analysis.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **SARS-CoV-2 RBD-specific antibodies were present in 14.2%–16.3% of screened individuals in Italy in September–October 2019, demonstrating widespread pre-pandemic viral circulation: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  An observed positivity rate of 11.6% (87.4% of which is isolated IgM+ without IgG+) cannot be interpreted as true infection prevalence without estimating and subtracting the assay's false-positive rate ($1 - \text{specificity}$). Even a modest 10% false-positive rate in an unvalidated IgM ELISA applied to plasma from older heavy smokers (a population with elevated rheumatoid factor and cross-reactive polyclonal IgM) fully accounts for the observed 10.1% IgM positivity. Furthermore, Table 1 contradicts basic humoral kinetics: whereas real SARS-CoV-2 infection induces durable IgG seroconversion within 14 days (as cited by the authors in reference 3), IgG positivity in Table 1 remains invariant between 0.9% and 2.4% across all six months, only 2 of 111 positive subjects have both IgM and IgG, and overall seropositivity drops fivefold from October 2019 (16.3%) to January 2020 (2.8%). Finally, a 14%–16% national infection rate in autumn 2019 is incompatible with the Italian Ministry of Health serosurvey cited in the text, which found only 2.5% national seroprevalence (and 7.5% in Lombardy) in May–July 2020 *after* the devastating spring 2020 wave.

* **The geographic distribution of pre-pandemic antibody-positive individuals (53.2% in Lombardy) mirrors the official COVID-19 incidence in Italy: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  This conclusion commits a base-rate fallacy by comparing raw numerator counts (`59/111 = 53.2%` of positives in Lombardy) without normalizing by regional recruitment denominators. As stated in the text, 491 of the 959 SMILE trial participants (`51.2%`) resided in Lombardy. Normalizing by the number of tested individuals reveals that the seropositivity rate in Lombardy is $59/491 = 12.0\%$, which is statistically indistinguishable from the seropositivity rate across the rest of Italy ($52/468 = 11.1\%$; Fisher's exact test $p = 0.69$, odds ratio $= 1.09$, 95% CI $[0.72, 1.65]$). An assay with a uniform ~11.6% background false-positive rate will necessarily yield ~51% of its positive calls in the region contributing 51% of the cohort.

* **The proprietary RBD-based ELISA is a valid surrogate for viral neutralization in this cohort: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In the Discussion (paragraph 15), the authors claim an "excellent correlation" between their RBD-ELISA and neutralization, asserting that *"the RBD-based ELISA can be used as a valid surrogate for neutralization."* This statement is directly contradicted by their own results (paragraph 9), which show that 105 of the 111 RBD-ELISA–positive samples (**94.6%**) tested negative in the live-virus CPE microneutralization assay (positive predictive agreement of only $6/111 = 5.4\%$).

* **Six microneutralization-positive plasma samples from October 2019–February 2020 confirm functional anti-SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing antibodies in autumn 2019: [Fragile].**  
  Only the 111 ELISA-positive samples were assayed by microneutralization, with no blinded ELISA-negative controls or pre-2018 historical negative controls included to determine the false-positive rate of the qualitative CPE assay at the lowest serum dilution tested. Moreover, the manuscript does not report the exact neutralization titer (e.g., $\text{ID}_{50}$ or $\text{MN}_{100}$ dilution: 1:10, 1:20, etc.), whether the 6 neutralizing samples were IgG+ or IgM-only, or whether neutralization was confirmed by plaque reduction or pseudovirus inhibition.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Calibrate Assay Specificity Using Blinded Pre-2018 Negative Controls and Seasonal Coronavirus Panels
* **WHY:** Without measuring the false-positive rate of `VM-IgM-RBD`, `VM-IgG-RBD`, and the qualitative CPE microneutralization assay on true pre-pandemic negative samples matched for age and smoking history, background cross-reactivity cannot be distinguished from true SARS-CoV-2 exposure.
* **WHAT:** Quantify assay specificity and compute Rogan–Gladen specificity-adjusted seroprevalence estimates with 95% confidence intervals.
* **HOW:** Assay a blinded panel of $\ge 300$ archived plasma samples collected from heavy smokers in 2015–2017 (well before SARS-CoV-2 emergence) alongside sera positive for endemic human coronaviruses (`HCoV-OC43`, `HCoV-229E`, `HCoV-NL63`, `HCoV-HKU1`) and rheumatoid factor. Using the empirical false-positive rate $\hat{f}$ from the 2015–2017 panel, compute the Rogan–Gladen adjusted prevalence $\hat{p}_{\text{adj}} = (\hat{p}_{\text{obs}} - \hat{f}) / (\hat{\text{sens}} - \hat{f})$ and exact binomial test $p$-values testing $H_0: \hat{p}_{\text{obs}} \le \hat{f}$ for each month in Table 1.

#### 2. Correct the Geographic Analysis by Reporting Region-Specific Positivity Rates ($n/N$)
* **WHY:** Reporting that 53.2% (59/111) of positive cases come from Lombardy when 51.2% (491/959) of the cohort was sampled in Lombardy misleads readers into perceiving a geographic hotspot where none exists in the prevalence rates (12.0% vs. 11.1%).
* **WHAT:** Replace raw numerator percentages across regions and provinces with denominator-normalized seroprevalence rates ($n_{\text{pos}} / N_{\text{tested}}$) and formal heterogeneity tests.
* **HOW:** In the Abstract, Results, Figure 2, and Supplementary Tables S3–S4, report the regional positivity rate $n/N$ (%) with 95% Clopper–Pearson confidence intervals for Lombardy ($59/491 = 12.0\%$) versus non-Lombardy regions ($52/468 = 11.1\%$) and across the 12 Lombardy provinces, together with a $\chi^2$ or Fisher's exact test for spatial heterogeneity. Remove the claim that the geographic distribution of positive cases mirrors official COVID-19 incidence unless regional prevalence rates ($n/N$) correlate significantly with regional COVID-19 attack rates.

#### 3. Perform Orthogonal Antigen Confirmation (Nucleocapsid and Full-Length Spike IgG) and Longitudinal Follow-Up
* **WHY:** Isolated IgM reactivity against a single antigen (95 of 111 positives are IgM+ / IgG−) with a declining temporal trend from October (16.3%) to January (2.8%) is a classic signature of non-specific IgM binding rather than acute viral infection.
* **WHAT:** Re-test all 111 RBD-reactive samples (and a matched random subset of RBD-negative samples) using orthogonal CE-IVD/FDA-authorized assays targeting SARS-CoV-2 Nucleocapsid (N) and full-length trimeric Spike (S) IgG, and check subsequent blood draws from the SMILE prospective trial for IgG seroconversion.
* **HOW:** Run commercial high-specificity ($\ge 99.5\%$) anti-N and anti-S IgG assays on the 111 plasma aliquots and report the concordance matrix. For SMILE participants who had follow-up blood draws or clinical follow-up in 2020, report whether the 95 IgM+ / IgG− participants from September–December 2019 ever seroconverted to anti-S/anti-N IgG.

#### 4. Provide Quantitative ELISA Optical Densities and Microneutralization Titers
* **WHY:** Treating both ELISA and microneutralization as binary outcomes hides whether positive calls cluster barely above an arbitrary cutoff threshold.
* **WHAT:** Report continuous OD distributions, cutoff definitions, and endpoint neutralization dilutions for all 111 samples.
* **HOW:** Add a supplementary figure plotting the continuous `VM-IgM-RBD` and `VM-IgG-RBD` OD ratios (or arbitrary units) by week of collection, indicating the exact cutoff threshold, mean + SD of negative controls, and highlighting the 6 microneutralization-positive samples labeled with their exact reciprocal endpoint dilution titers (e.g., 1:10, 1:20, 1:40, 1:80).

#### 5. Technical, Mathematical, and Editorial Cleanup
* **WHY:** Internal discrepancies in recruitment dates, national case totals, and unsupported claims weaken technical rigor.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  1. **Recruitment window mismatch (Abstract/Text vs. Table 1):** The Abstract states samples were collected *"between September 2019 and March 2020"* and the text cites *"March 10 (last SMILE study recruitment date)"*, yet the monthly sample sizes in Table 1 from September through February (`162 + 166 + 273 + 147 + 106 + 105`) sum to exactly `959`, leaving 0 samples collected in March 2020. Clarify whether any samples were collected in March 2020.
  2. **National COVID-19 case count discrepancy (Main text vs. Figure 2 caption):** Paragraph 7 states *"5791/10,141 (57.1%) patients with COVID-19"*, whereas the Figure 2A caption states *"10,149 patients with COVID-19"*. Reconcile `10,141` vs. `10,149`.
  3. **Remove contradiction on ELISA-neutralization correlation:** Remove or qualify the statement in paragraph 15 that *"the RBD-based ELISA can be used as a valid surrogate for neutralization"*, since 94.6% (105/111) of RBD-ELISA–positive samples were microneutralization-negative in this study.
