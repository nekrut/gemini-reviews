# Peer Review: Gut Microbiome Signature Are Correlated With Bone Mineral Density Alterations in the Chinese Elders (PMC9008261)

**Overall Score:** **3.4 / 10.0** (Flawed / Overstated)
* **Score Breakdown:** Rigor: 0.8/3.5 | Claims: 0.7/2.5 | Biology: 1.0/2.0 | Reproducibility: 0.9/2.0

---

## 1. Executive Summary & Synthesis

Wang et al. investigate associations between clinical characteristics, serum bone turnover markers, gut microbiome composition (16S rRNA amplicon sequencing and shotgun metagenomics), and bone mineral density (BMD) measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) in elderly Chinese subjects from Wuhan. Starting from an epidemiological cohort of 455 subjects (175 females and 280 males), the authors select 113 elderly individuals (58 postmenopausal females and 55 males aged $\ge 50$ years) for fecal 16S rRNA sequencing, and further subset 57 individuals (29 females and 28 males) for shotgun metagenomic sequencing. Within each sex, participants are stratified into three BMD diagnostic categories: normal BMD (NC, $T\text{-score} \ge -1.0$), osteopenia (ON, $-2.5 < T\text{-score} < -1.0$), and osteoporosis (OP, $T\text{-score} \le -2.5$). The authors report sex-specific differences in microbial alpha/beta diversity, relative abundances of bacterial phyla/genera (e.g., *Firmicutes*, *Bacteroidetes*, *Clostridiales*, *Blautia*, *Ruminococcaceae*), and KEGG functional pathways (e.g., carbohydrate, purine/pyrimidine, and oxidative phosphorylation metabolism), and propose receiver operating characteristic (ROC) biomarker panels (`AUC > 0.7`) to predict osteoporosis.

While the clinical question of sex-specific gut-bone axis remodeling in aging populations is biologically relevant, the study's statistical design and bioinformatic execution suffer from severe methodological flaws that invalidate most of its microbiome and predictive claims: (1) complete absence of multiple-hypothesis testing correction (e.g., Benjamini–Hochberg FDR) across hundreds of bacterial taxa, KEGG pathways, and clinical traits tested via Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and Pearson correlations; (2) severe underpowering due to sex-by-diagnosis subgrouping, which reduces the shotgun metagenomic cohorts to only $n \approx 6\text{--}10$ subjects per subgroup; (3) circular and unvalidated ROC biomarker analysis, exemplified by the authors testing DXA `T-score` as a predictor of osteoporosis (`T-score <= -2.5`) and reporting as a finding that `T-score` achieves `AUC = 1.0, P = 0.000`, alongside unvalidated in-sample microbial ROC models; (4) inappropriate application of standard parametric Pearson correlation and Student's $t$-tests to sparse, zero-inflated, compositional relative abundance data without log-ratio (e.g., CLR) transformation or covariate adjustment for the strong age and BMI confounding present between BMD groups; and (5) internal contradictions between the text and tables regarding statistical tests and sample counts.

## 2. Claim-by-Claim Evaluation

* **Claim 1:** Postmenopausal females exhibit significantly higher prevalence of osteoporosis (32.7%) than males aged $\ge 50$ years (9.8%), and lumbar spine and total hip BMD correlate positively with weight/BMI and negatively with age and years since menopause. **[Well Supported]**
  * **Evidence & Audit:** The baseline clinical and DXA measurements across the 455 subjects (Table 1, Figure 2G–L) reflect well-established human physiology and epidemiology: postmenopausal estrogen decline accelerates bone loss in women, mechanical loading/body mass (BMI) positively correlates with areal BMD, and age/duration of menopause negatively correlates with BMD.

* **Claim 2:** In postmenopausal females, gut microbial alpha diversity (Observed OTUs, Chao1, ACE) is elevated in osteoporosis (OP) compared to normal bone density (NC), whereas in males aged $\ge 50$ years, Observed OTUs and Shannon diversity are reduced in osteopenia/osteoporosis. **[Fragile]**
  * **Evidence & Audit:** In the 16S rRNA cohort ($n = 58$ females and $n = 55$ males split across NC, ON, and OP subgroups, yielding $n \approx 15\text{--}22$ per cell), Table 2 shows that female OP subjects are significantly older ($69.20 \pm 6.62$ years) and have lower BMI ($22.84 \pm 3.55\text{ kg/m}^2$) than female NC subjects ($62.13 \pm 7.77$ years, $P < 0.05$; BMI $25.49 \pm 3.63\text{ kg/m}^2$, $P < 0.05$). Despite this 7-year age gap and $2.65\text{ kg/m}^2$ BMI difference between female OP and NC subgroups, the alpha-diversity comparisons (Figure 3A–F) are unadjusted univariate tests that do not control for age, BMI, diet, or medication history.

* **Claim 3:** In postmenopausal females, BMD correlates negatively with the phylum *Firmicutes* and order *Clostridiales* (`P < 0.05`) and positively with *Bacteroidetes*, whereas in elderly males BMD correlates with *Bacteroidaceae*, *Ruminococcaceae*, and *Faecalibacterium*. **[Overstated]**
  * **Evidence & Audit:** All taxonomic comparisons across phyla, classes, orders, families, and genera (Figure 4, Tables S1–S2) and Pearson correlations with L1–4 BMD, total hip BMD, and T-scores rely on unadjusted nominal $P < 0.05$ thresholds with zero correction for multiple comparisons. Furthermore, applying standard linear Pearson correlation directly to compositional relative abundances (proportions summing to 1) induces spurious negative correlations (the constant-sum constraint on the simplex, especially between the two dominant phyla *Firmicutes* and *Bacteroidetes*) and is highly sensitive to extreme values in small subgroups ($n \approx 15\text{--}20$).

* **Claim 4:** Shotgun metagenomic functional profiling demonstrates that carbohydrate metabolism, purine/pyrimidine metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation pathways are negatively correlated with BMD in females, whereas carbohydrate metabolism, starch/sucrose metabolism, and two-component systems are positively correlated with BMD in males. **[Methodologically Flawed]**
  * **Evidence & Audit:** The shotgun metagenomics subset comprises only 29 females and 28 males divided into three diagnostic categories each (NC, ON, OP), leaving an average of only $n \approx 9\text{--}10$ subjects per subgroup (and likely fewer in male OP given the 9.8% prevalence of OP in males). Performing hundreds of unadjusted Pearson correlation tests and Wilcoxon tests across KEGG Level 1, Level 2, and Level 3 pathways on $N = 28\text{--}29$ subjects without false discovery rate (FDR) correction guarantees dozens of false-positive associations by chance alone.

* **Claim 5:** ROC curve analysis demonstrates that specific clinical factors (`T-score`, `Age`, `Weight`, `ALP`) and gut microbiome signatures (`Firmicutes`, `Clostridiales`, `Blautia`, `Olsenella`, `Bacteroidaceae`, `Alloprevotella`, `Diaister` [sic]) serve as robust predictive biomarkers for osteoporosis with `AUC > 0.7` (and `AUC = 1.0` for `T-score`). **[Methodologically Flawed]**
  * **Evidence & Audit:** This analysis contains two fundamental flaws:
    1. *Circular tautology (`AUC = 1.0` for T-score):* In the Results ("Relationship of Clinicopathological Features and BMD"), the authors state: *"The predictable factors included T-score (AUC = 1, P = 0.000), age (AUC = 0.767, P = 0.008), weight (AUC = 0.728, P = 0.025) and ALP (AUC = 0.742, P = 0.017) in the female (Figure S1A); while in the male, the predictable factors included T-score (AUC = 1, P = 0.000)..."* Because osteoporosis is deterministically defined in the Methods by `T-score <= -2.5` (and Normal by `T-score >= -1.0`), evaluating `T-score` as a "predictive factor" for the `T-score`-defined disease label is a mathematical tautology that must yield $\text{AUC} = 1.000$ by definition.
    2. *Data leakage and overfitting in microbial ROC curves:* The microbial taxa (`Blautia`, `Olsenella`, `Alloprevotella`, etc.) were first selected in the exact same small sample ($n = 58$ females or $n = 55$ males) because they showed nominal univariate differences ($P < 0.05$) between OP and NC, and were then evaluated via in-sample ROC curves on the same discovery subjects without train/test splitting or cross-validation. Post-selection in-sample AUC estimates on $n \approx 15\text{--}20$ cases vs. controls are severely upward-biased.

## 3. Technical, Statistical, and Biological Critique

### 3.1 Major Methodological and Statistical Issues

1. **Absence of Multiple-Testing Correction Across Hundreds of Taxa, Pathways, and Correlations:**
   Nowhere in the Methods ("Statistical Analysis") or Results do the authors apply a false discovery rate (Benjamini–Hochberg FDR) or family-wise error rate correction across the microbiome and metagenomic tests. When testing hundreds of OTUs/genera (Tables S1–S2), dozens of KEGG pathways (Tables S3–S4), and large pairwise correlation matrices linking taxa, KEGG pathways, and BMD parameters (Table S5, Figure 6) at a nominal threshold of $P < 0.05$, the expected number of false positives exceeds the number of reported associations.

2. **Confounding by Age and BMI in Unadjusted Microbiome Comparisons:**
   Table 2 reveals substantial baseline confounding across the BMD subgroups in the 16S sequencing cohort: in females, mean age increases from $62.13 \pm 7.77$ years in NC to $69.20 \pm 6.62$ years in OP ($P < 0.05$), while BMI drops from $25.49 \pm 3.63\text{ kg/m}^2$ in NC to $22.84 \pm 3.55\text{ kg/m}^2$ in OP ($P < 0.05$). In males, weight drops from $72.24 \pm 9.93\text{ kg}$ in NC to $62.71 \pm 8.92\text{ kg}$ in OP ($P < 0.05$) and BMI drops from $25.33 \pm 2.66$ to $23.16 \pm 2.34\text{ kg/m}^2$ ($P < 0.05$). Because both gut microbiota composition (including the *Firmicutes*-to-*Bacteroidetes* ratio) and bone mineral density are strongly driven by host age and adiposity/BMI, unadjusted univariate correlations between bacterial relative abundances and BMD cannot distinguish direct microbiome-bone associations from simple confounding by age and body mass.

3. **Inappropriate Statistical Treatment of Compositional Microbiome Data and Contradictory Test Descriptions:**
   In the Methods ("Statistical Analysis"), the authors state: *"A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or a pearson correlation coaching [sic] test was used for the data with normal distribution; otherwise, a Mann-Whitney U-test or Kruskal-Wallis test was the choice."* Yet in the Results ("Variation of GM Composition and Function in Three Subgroups..."), they state that functional differences between subgroups were tested using *"Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and Student's t-test."* Applying parametric Pearson correlations and Student's $t$-tests directly to relative abundances without centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation violates normality and independence assumptions on the simplex.

4. **Outdated Bioinformatic Pipeline and Unreported Subgroup Sample Sizes:**
   Amplicon sequences were clustered into 97% OTUs using legacy `QIIME v1.8.0` (2014) and annotated against `Greengenes` (`gg_13_8`, last updated in 2013) rather than modern amplicon sequence variant (ASV) denoising (`DADA2`) and updated taxonomic databases (`SILVA` / `GTDB`). Furthermore, Tables 1 and 2 never report the exact sample size ($n$) inside each of the six sex-by-BMD subgroups (Female NC, Female ON, Female OP; Male NC, Male ON, Male OP) for either the 113-sample 16S cohort or the 57-sample shotgun metagenomic cohort, obscuring the exact degrees of freedom in every subgroup comparison.

### 3.2 Minor Issues and Textual Errors

1. **Internal Sample-Count Discrepancies:**
   In the Results ("Epidemiological Investigation of Study Population"), the text states: *"Specifically, in 122 postmenopausal females..."* whereas two sentences earlier and in Table 1 the number of postmenopausal females is given as $119$ ($175\text{ total females} - 56\text{ females } < 50\text{y} = 119$).
2. **Typographical and Taxonomic Spelling Errors:**
   The manuscript contains numerous typographical errors: *"pearson correlation coaching test"* (Methods), *"Dialister"* misspelled as *"Diaister"* in the Results, *"Actinobacteria"* misspelled as *"Acitinobacteria"*, and *"inhibitants"* instead of *"inhabitants"*.
3. **Data Availability:**
   While raw sequencing reads were deposited in the China National GeneBank DataBase (`CNGBdb` project `CNP0002492`), no analysis scripts, metadata tables mapping sample IDs to BMD subgroups, or processed OTU/KEGG tables are provided for computational verification.
