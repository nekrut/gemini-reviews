### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript presents the *Drosophila melanogaster* Genetic Reference Panel (DGRP Freeze 1.0), a community resource of 192 inbred lines derived by 20 generations of full-sib mating from a single natural population in Raleigh, North Carolina, with whole-genome resequencing completed for 168 lines using Illumina (mean 21.4x coverage across 158 lines) and Roche 454 (mean 12.1x coverage across 39 lines; 29 lines sequenced on both platforms with 99.36% SNP concordance). Using the Joint Genotyper for Inbred Lines (JGIL), the authors catalogue 4,672,297 SNPs, 105,799 polymorphic microsatellites, and 36,810 transposable element (TE) insertion sites across 113.5 Mb (94.25%) of the euchromatic genome, alongside *Wolbachia pipientis* infection status (51.2% of lines). Combining DGRP polymorphism with divergence to *D. simulans* and *D. yakuba*, the authors characterize genome-wide nucleotide diversity ($\pi = 0.0056, \theta = 0.0067$), linkage disequilibrium decay ($r^2 < 0.2$ within 10 bp on autosomes and 30 bp on the $X$ chromosome), recombination-diversity correlations (positive below 2 cM/Mb, flat above 2 cM/Mb), and standard/generalized McDonald–Kreitman tests (estimating that 25.2% of fixed differences are adaptive and that the $X$ chromosome experiences >4-fold higher adaptive fixation rates than autosomes). Finally, the authors perform genome-wide association (GWA) mapping across ~2.5 million common variants (minor allele count $\ge 4$) for starvation resistance, startle response, and chill coma recovery time, reporting hundreds of candidate variants at $P < 10^{-5}$ and concluding from multiple regression and partial least squares (PLS) models that 6–10 SNPs (or all $P < 10^{-5}$ SNPs) explain 51–85% of the phenotypic variance. All inbred fly stocks, raw reads, alignments, variant calls, phenotypes, GWA portals, and the `PopDrowser` genome browser are publicly available (Table 1).

As a foundational model-organism community resource and population-genomic survey, the DGRP stock collection, variant catalogue, and molecular evolution analyses are of high technical quality and lasting utility. In accordance with resource-paper evaluation standards, the primary deliverable (the 168-line sequenced reference panel, variant call set, and population-genomic browser) is rigorously validated. However, the quantitative genetic claims in the Abstract, Results, and Discussion regarding the GWA and phenotypic prediction analyses ("6–10 SNPs explain from 51–72% of the phenotypic variance and 65–90% of the genetic variance" and PLS models explaining "72–85% of the phenotypic variance", contrasted directly against human GWAS "missing heritability") suffer from severe in-sample feature-selection overfitting (winner's curse / data leakage). Selecting the top $P < 10^{-5}$ SNPs from a scan of 2.5 million markers in $N = 168$ lines and fitting multiple regression or PLS models on the *exact same* 168 lines without out-of-sample cross-validation guarantees that even pure random noise markers will explain 50–85% of the variance. Additionally, segregating cosmopolitan chromosomal inversions (which are common in *D. melanogaster* and suppress recombination while maintaining residual heterozygosity) are mentioned in passing as a source of unfixed SNPs but are not controlled for in either the GWA models or the centromeric/autosomal diversity comparisons. These issues can be resolved through cross-validated variance estimation and inversion-karyotype adjustment.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Generation and high-concordance variant cataloguing of 168 sequenced inbred *D. melanogaster* lines (4.67M SNPs, 105.8k polymorphic microsatellites, 36.8k TE insertion sites): [Well Supported].**  
  The primary resource deliverable is thoroughly validated: JGIL probabilistic genotyping accounts for 20 generations of full-sib inbreeding ($F = 0.986$), cross-platform concordance across 29 dual-sequenced lines is 99.36% (Supplementary Table 3), euchromatic coverage averages 94.25% at 21.4x Illumina depth, and all lines and data products are openly distributed via Bloomington and public repositories (Table 1).

* **Polymorphism correlates positively with recombination below 2 cM/Mb, and the $X$ chromosome exhibits faster adaptive evolution ("faster $X$") and stronger purifying selection than autosomes: [Well Supported].**  
  Windowed analyses across 50 kb intervals confirm the piecewise relationship between $\pi$ and recombination ($\rho = 0.471$ for $<2\text{ cM/Mb}$ vs. $\rho = -0.0044$ for $\ge 2\text{ cM/Mb}$, avoiding mutation-rate confounding via constant divergence $k$; Fig. 2a–b, Supplementary Table 12). SFS-integrated McDonald–Kreitman and Direction of Selection ($DoS$) analyses consistently demonstrate elevated adaptive fixation ($\alpha$) and a higher fraction of strongly deleterious sites ($d$) on the $X$ chromosome relative to autosomes across coding and non-coding site classes (Figs. 2c, 3).

* **Low-frequency alleles in novel candidate genes show an inverse relationship between minor allele frequency and GWA effect size, consistent with mutation-selection balance: [Fragile].**  
  In a sample of $N = 168$ lines where the minimum minor allele count is 4 (MAF $\approx 2.4\%$), statistical power to reach $P < 10^{-5}$ is strictly bounded by the minimum detectable effect size: a variant present in only 4 out of 168 lines *cannot* achieve $P < 10^{-5}$ unless its sample effect size is extreme (or driven by 1–2 outlier lines in the phenotype tail), whereas a common variant at MAF $= 0.40$ can achieve $P < 10^{-5}$ with a much smaller effect size. Thus, conditioning on $P < 10^{-5}$ automatically induces a steep inverse curve between MAF and estimated effect size via truncation bias (Beavis effect / winner's curse), even under a null model where true effect sizes are independent of allele frequency.

* **Multiple regression (6–10 SNPs) and PLS models of significant GWA SNPs explain 51–85% of the phenotypic variance and 65–90% of the genetic variance, contrasting with human GWAS: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  With $p = 2,490,165$ SNPs tested in $n = 168$ lines ($p \gg n$), roughly $25\text{--}30$ independent false-positive SNPs are expected at $P < 10^{-5}$ under the global null hypothesis (and the reported $90\text{--}235$ hits at $P < 10^{-5}$ barely exceed the family-wise error rate threshold of $0.05 / 2.5 \times 10^6 = 2 \times 10^{-8}$). When markers are pre-selected by scanning all $n = 168$ lines for $P < 10^{-5}$ and then entered into a multiple regression or PLS model evaluated on those *same* 168 lines without out-of-sample cross-validation, high $R^2$ (51–85%) is a mathematical consequence of high-dimensional selection bias rather than true predictive variance explained. Contrasting this unvalidated in-sample $R^2$ with out-of-sample human GWAS heritability is therefore invalid.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Evaluate Phenotypic Variance Explained ($R^2$) Using Nested Out-of-Sample Cross-Validation
* **WHY:** Selecting candidate SNPs at $P < 10^{-5}$ across all 168 lines and fitting multiple regression or PLS models on the same 168 lines leaks test-set information into feature selection, severely inflating $R^2$ (51–85%).
* **WHAT:** Re-estimate the phenotypic variance explained by the multiple regression and PLS models using strict 10-fold (or leave-one-out) nested cross-validation, where the genome-wide association scan ($P < 10^{-5}$ marker selection) and regression weight fitting are performed strictly inside the training folds ($90\%$ of lines) and predictive $R^2$ is evaluated on the held-out test lines ($10\%$).
* **HOW:** Split the 168 lines into 10 folds; in each fold, run the single-marker GWAS on the ~151 training lines, select the top $P < 10^{-5}$ SNPs (or top 6–10 gene-centered SNPs), fit the multiple regression and PLS models on the training lines, and predict the phenotypes of the 17 held-out lines. Report the out-of-sample cross-validated prediction $R^2_{\text{CV}}$ alongside a permutation null distribution (permuting phenotype labels across the 168 lines before running the entire GWAS + regression pipeline) to establish the true predictive accuracy above high-dimensional noise.

#### 2. Control for Segregating Cosmopolitan Chromosomal Inversions in Population Genomics and GWA Scans
* **WHY:** Natural North American *D. melanogaster* populations segregate for multiple large paracentric inversions (e.g., *In(2L)t*, *In(2R)NS*, *In(3R)Payne*, *In(3R)Mo*). As noted on line 27, inversions maintain associative overdominance and residual heterozygosity during inbreeding, create long-range LD blocks ("occasional spurious long range LD", line 83), and alter recombination rates across chromosome arms.
* **WHAT:** Genotype or infer the inversion karyotypes of the 168 DGRP lines and include inversion status (along with *Wolbachia* infection status and residual chromosome-wide heterozygosity) as covariates in the GWA mixed models and in the centromeric vs. non-centromeric nucleotide diversity ($\pi$) comparisons.
* **HOW:** Identify inversion breakpoints or diagnostic LD clusters from the paired-end reads and SNP matrices across arms 2L, 2R, 3L, and 3R; test whether the autosomal centromeric/middle diversity patterns (Fig. 2a) and long-range LD blocks (Fig. 4a) are driven by segregating inversions; and re-run the GWA scans adjusting for inversion karyotypes.

#### 3. Correct the MAF-vs.-Effect-Size Analysis for Truncation (Beavis) Bias
* **WHY:** Plotting $|\text{effect size}|$ against MAF exclusively for SNPs passing a fixed significance threshold ($P < 10^{-5}$, Supplementary Fig. 10) creates an artificial $1/\sqrt{\text{MAF}(1-\text{MAF})}$ curve because low-MAF variants have larger standard errors ($\text{SE} \propto [n \cdot \text{MAF}(1-\text{MAF})]^{-1/2}$) and must exceed a much higher effect-size threshold ($|\hat{\beta}| > z_{10^{-5}} \cdot \text{SE}$) to be included in the plot.
* **WHAT:** Overlay the analytical minimum detectable effect size curve ($|\beta_{\min}(\text{MAF})| = t_{10^{-5}, \text{df}} \cdot \hat{\sigma}_y / \sqrt{2 n \cdot \text{MAF}(1-\text{MAF})}$) on Supplementary Fig. 10 and test whether the observed inverse relationship exceeds the statistical truncation boundary.
* **HOW:** Compute the exact $P = 10^{-5}$ detection boundary as a function of MAF for each of the three traits, plot it on Supplementary Fig. 10, and apply a truncation-corrected maximum likelihood estimator (or permutation test matching the SFS) before attributing the curve to mutation-selection balance.

#### 4. Temper Claims Comparing Unvalidated DGRP In-Sample $R^2$ to Human GWAS Heritability
* **WHY:** In the Abstract ("explain a large fraction of the phenotypic variance") and Discussion (line 101: "Regression models incorporating significant SNPs explain most of the phenotypic variance of the traits, in contrast with human association studies..."), the manuscript attributes the high $R^2$ to biological differences or homozygosity without acknowledging the statistical winner's curse of selecting 6–10 markers out of 2.5 million in $N=168$ lines.
* **WHAT:** Revise the Abstract, "Predicting phenotypes from genotypes" section, and Discussion to clearly distinguish in-sample descriptive $R^2$ of post-hoc selected candidate SNPs from out-of-sample polygenic prediction accuracy.
* **HOW:** Update line 89 and line 101 to report the permutation-adjusted or cross-validated variance explained, and note that with $N=168$ lines, GWA at $P < 10^{-5}$ is a hypothesis-generating screen for candidate validation rather than an unbiased estimator of total narrow-sense heritability.
