### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

Faria et al. investigate the late-2020 resurgence of SARS-CoV-2 in Manaus, Brazil, combining ARTIC V3/MinION viral genome sequencing of 184 clinical samples collected in November–December 2020 (48 complete [$\ge 95\%$], 95 near-complete [$75\%–95\%$], and 41 partial [$25\%–75\%$] genomes) with molecular-clock phylodynamics, codon-level selection analysis, longitudinal RT-PCR cycle threshold (Ct) measurements ($n=942$), national air-travel data, and a two-category Bayesian semi-mechanistic transmission and mortality model. They identify and characterize lineage P.1 (descended from B.1.1.28), defined by 17 nonsynonymous mutations—including the Spike RBD triad K417T, E484K, and N501Y—plus 3 deletions, 4 synonymous substitutions, and a 4-bp insertion. Using a local molecular clock with a nonparametric skygrid tree prior, they estimate the P.1 time to most recent common ancestor (tMRCA) around 15 November 2020 (95% Bayesian credible interval [BCI]: 6 October–24 November 2020) and find positive support ($\text{BF} = 6.04$) for an elevated substitution rate on the stem branch leading to P.1. Integrating genomic variant frequencies with SIVEP-Gripe SARI mortality series, their dynamical model estimates that P.1 is 1.7- to 2.4-fold more transmissible (50% BCI; median 2.0) and retains 54% to 79% cross-immunity (50% BCI; median 68%, i.e., 21%–46% immune evasion) relative to non-P.1 lineages. All sequencing data, epidemiological series, and modeling code are publicly archived on GISAID and GitHub.

This is a rigorous, well-integrated genomic epidemiology study that stands out for its methodological care: the authors explicitly test and control for epidemic growth-phase bias in RT-PCR Ct values (showing that the raw Ct difference vanishes after adjusting for symptom-to-swab delay), visualize the joint posterior identifiability ridge between transmissibility and immune evasion (Figure 4A–B), and caution against attributing increased post-emergence mortality solely to intrinsic viral virulence given healthcare system collapse. The main areas for improvement are: (i) the Abstract and main text report **50% Bayesian credible intervals** (interquartile ranges) rather than standard 95% credible intervals for the headline transmissibility, cross-immunity, and mortality estimates without labeling them as 50% BCIs in the Abstract; (ii) the homogeneous-mixing assumption in the dynamical model ties inferred immune escape to the assumed first-wave attack rate; and (iii) several figure panel cross-references (`Fig. 1C` vs. `1D` and `Fig. 3B` vs. `3C`) are transposed between the main text and captions.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Lineage P.1 emerged in Amazonas around mid-November 2020 following accelerated evolution along its ancestral stem branch and expanded from 0% to 87% of sequenced cases in ~7 weeks: [Well Supported].**  
  Phylogenetic placement within B.1.1.28 sequences from Amazonas (posterior probability $= 1.00$), root-to-tip regression (Figures S6–S8), local molecular clock dating (median tMRCA 15 November 2020, 95% BCI: 6 October–24 November 2020), and binomial logistic growth modeling of P.1 genomic proportions (Figure 3A, Table S4) provide consistent support. The Bayes factor for an elevated stem-branch clock rate ($\text{BF} = 6.04$) represents moderate/positive statistical support under Kass and Raftery criteria, and diversifying selection on 8 of 10 Spike substitutions is independently supported by codon selection models and convergent evolution with B.1.351 and B.1.1.7.

* **Lower RT-PCR Ct values during the P.1 wave are confounded by epidemic growth timing and symptom-to-sampling delay: [Well Supported].**  
  Although unadjusted lognormal regressions on sequenced samples ($n=128–129$) and period-stratified laboratory series ($n=942$) show lower E- and N-gene Ct values for P.1 by 1.43–1.91 cycles ($p = 0.029$ and $p = 0.01$), the authors explicitly condition on days from symptom onset to swab collection ($n=42$) and demonstrate that statistical significance is lost ($p = 0.15$ and $p = 0.12$). Their conclusion that Ct shifts cannot distinguish higher peak viral load from prolonged shedding or epidemic-phase ascertainment is exemplary.

* **P.1 is 1.7- to 2.4-fold more transmissible and evades 21% to 46% of prior non-P.1 immunity: [Moderately Supported].**  
  The two-category Bayesian transmission model captures both SIVEP-Gripe mortality and P.1 genomic replacement across extensive sensitivity analyses varying waning rates and emergence dates (Tables S5–S10). However, two caveats qualify the precision of these estimates: first, `1.7–2.4` and `21%–46%` (54%–79% cross-immunity) are **50% BCIs** rather than 95% BCIs, and because transmissibility and immune escape trade off along a collinear ridge in the joint posterior (Figure 4A), the marginal 95% BCIs are substantially wider (e.g., the 95% one-sided threshold for immune escape extends down to 10%). Second, the model assumes homogeneous mixing; if spatial or socioeconomic contact heterogeneity in Manaus caused pockets of the population to remain unexposed during the first wave (or if blood-donor seroprevalence overestimated whole-city attack rates), less immune escape is required to explain the second wave.

* **Post-P.1 infections carry a 1.2- to 1.9-fold higher relative risk of mortality, which cannot be separated from healthcare system strain: [Well Supported].**  
  The authors report the 50% BCI (`1.2–1.9`, median `1.5`, with 90% posterior probability of $\text{RR} > 1$, meaning the two-sided 95% BCI overlaps 1.0) and explicitly state that hospital saturation and oxygen shortages in Manaus confound intrinsic P.1 severity with healthcare access.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Explicitly Report 95% Bayesian Credible Intervals Alongside 50% BCIs in the Abstract and Main Text
* **WHY:** In the Abstract, the ranges `1.7- to 2.4-fold more transmissible` and `54 to 79% of the protection` are presented without specifying their credible level, leading readers to assume they are standard 95% credible intervals when they are actually 50% BCIs (interquartile ranges).
* **WHAT:** Label the 50% BCIs explicitly in the Abstract and report the corresponding 95% BCIs for transmissibility, cross-immunity, and relative mortality risk in both the Abstract and main text.
* **HOW:** Update the Abstract sentence to stateboth the 50% and 95% BCIs (e.g., *"1.7- to 2.4-fold more transmissible [50% BCI; 95% BCI: X.X–X.X] and that previous (non-P.1) infection provides 54 to 79% [50% BCI; 95% BCI: XX–XX%] of the protection"*), and include the marginal 95% BCIs in the "Mathematical modeling of lineage P.1 epidemiological characteristics" section alongside the 50% BCIs.

#### 2. Add a Sensitivity Analysis for First-Wave Attack Rate and Contact Heterogeneity in the Dynamical Model
* **WHY:** In a homogeneously mixed two-strain model, the inferred trade-off between transmissibility ($\Delta R_0$) and immune escape ($1 - \text{cross-immunity}$) in Figure 4A depends strongly on the fraction of the population assumed to be immune at the start of November 2020 (~67%–76% from blood-donor serosurveillance). Population heterogeneity in contact rates (super-spreading/assortative mixing by neighborhood or age) reduces the herd-immunity threshold and can leave susceptible sub-networks even when blood-donor seroprevalence is high.
* **WHAT:** Quantify how the joint posterior contour in Figure 4A shifts across lower effective first-wave cumulative attack rates or an overdispersion/susceptible-depletion exponent.
* **HOW:** Using the existing Stan/Bayesian transmission code in the GitHub repository, run a sensitivity grid where first-wave infection fatality ratio (IFR) or susceptibility depletion is varied so that pre-November 2020 cumulative attack rates span 45%, 55%, 65%, and 75% (or incorporate a susceptibility-heterogeneity parameter $\gamma$ scaling the effective susceptible fraction as $S_{\text{eff}} = S^{1+\gamma}$), and plot the resulting 50% and 95% joint posterior contours in the Supplementary Materials.

#### 3. Temper the Interpretation of the Local Clock Bayes Factor ($\text{BF} = 6.04$)
* **WHY:** The text states that *"the local clock model statistically confirmed a higher evolutionary rate for the branch immediately ancestral to lineage P.1 compared with lineage B.1.1.28 as a whole [Bayes factor (BF) = 6.04]."* A Bayes factor of 6.04 ($2 \ln \text{BF} = 3.60$) provides moderate/positive support rather than definitive confirmation, especially since only a single ancestral branch is being parameterized.
* **WHAT:** Describe $\text{BF} = 6.04$ accurately as moderate positive evidence and report the posterior rate estimate (in substitutions/site/year) for the P.1 stem branch versus the background B.1.1.28 clock rate.
* **HOW:** Replace *"statistically confirmed"* with *"provided moderate statistical support for"* in the "Dating the emergence of the P.1 lineage" section, and add the median and 95% BCI of the background B.1.1.28 substitution rate and the P.1 stem-branch rate (or inferred excess mutation count).

* **WHY:** Several swapped figure panel references and minor sequence-count discrepancies between text and captions create confusion when cross-checking results.
* **WHAT & HOW:**

     - In "Infection with P.1 and sample viral loads" (paragraph 2, line 288), *"quantifying the trend shown in Fig. 1C"* should refer to **`Fig. 1D`** (which plots genome counts by lineage over time), not `Fig. 1C`.
  2. **Reversed descriptions of `Fig. 3B` and `Fig. 3C` between main text and caption:**
     - In the main text (lines 288 and 296), `Fig. 3B` is cited for the genomically sequenced subsample ($n=128/129$) and `Fig. 3C` is cited for the full $n=942$ sample stratified by the three time periods. However, in the **Fig. 3 caption** (line 294), label `(B)` describes the time-period stratification of all symptomatic cases and label `(C)` describes the sequenced subsample. Swap the `(B)` and `(C)` text in the Figure 3 caption (or in the main text) so they match the plotted panels.
