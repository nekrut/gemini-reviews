### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript introduces the Singleton Density Score (SDS), a coalescent-based statistical method to detect very recent natural selection (over the timescale of terminal genealogical branch lengths, $\sim 75$ generations or $\sim 2,000\text{–}3,000$ years in a sample of $\sim 3,000$ individuals) from contemporary whole-genome sequences. By measuring the physical distance between the nearest singleton mutations flanking a test SNP across each diploid genotype and computing a maximum-likelihood estimate of the log-ratio of mean tip-branch lengths for the derived versus ancestral alleles (standardized within derived allele frequency bins), SDS uses the two alleles at each locus as internal controls for local mutation rate, recombination rate, and singleton ascertainment. Applying SDS to 3,195 whole genomes from the UK10K Project across 4.5 million autosomal SNPs ($\text{MAF} > 5\%$), the authors identify genome-wide significant single-locus sweeps at *LCT* ($P = 1 \times 10^{-23}$), the MHC region ($P = 2 \times 10^{-15}$), and *WDFY4* ($P = 3 \times 10^{-11}$), enrichment for derived alleles associated with lighter hair and eye pigmentation ($P = 3 \times 10^{-9}$), and genome-wide correlations between trait-polarized SDS (tSDS) and GWAS effect sizes for height ($\rho = 0.078, P = 9 \times 10^{-74}$ in meta-analysis; $\rho = 0.094, P = 9 \times 10^{-163}$ in a family-based study) and several other complex traits among 43 tested phenotypes. Precomputed SDS scores and source code are publicly available on Dryad and GitHub.

The SDS estimator itself is an elegant, theoretically grounded methodological advance for population genetics, supported by coalescent simulations under demographic expansion and admixture, comparisons showing an order-of-magnitude sharper recent-time specificity than iHS, and an empirical biased-subsampling validation on UK10K genomes. However, while the single-locus and oligogenic pigmentation findings are robust, the claims of pervasive genome-wide polygenic adaptation across height and other complex traits are vulnerable to residual population stratification. Specifically, the authors show in Figure 2E that SDS correlates strongly with the European North–South allele frequency gradient ($\rho = 0.32$), which means that even minute residual geographic stratification in GWAS summary statistics will align with SDS across millions of non-significant SNPs. Moreover, bivariate LD score regression (LDSC) does not cleanly separate true polygenic selection from clinal stratification when one of the traits is a window-based coalescent statistic (SDS) whose variance and clinal covariance naturally scale with local LD, and the 43-trait screen relies on a nominal one-sided $P < 0.05$ LDSC filter without multiple-testing correction. Strengthening the manuscript requires explicitly modeling how shared North–South European drift affects bivariate LDSC slopes, testing tSDS within strictly sibling-pair (within-family) GWAS effect estimates, and bringing the key likelihood equations and UK10K singleton quality-control metrics into the main text.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **SDS accurately infers allele frequency changes over the terminal-branch timescale ($\sim 75$ generations for $n \approx 3,000$) and outperforms iHS for recent selection on standing variation: [Well Supported].**  
  Coalescent simulations (Figure 2A–C) and empirical biased subsampling of 1,500 individuals from the 3,195 UK10K genomes (Figure 2D) demonstrate that each 1% artificial shift in allele frequency shifts mean SDS by $0.3\text{–}0.4$ SD, whereas iHS has negligible power for instantaneous or standing-variation shifts and retains historical signal from sweeps that ended $> 1,000$ generations ago. Using the ancestral and derived alleles at the same test SNP as internal controls effectively cancels local heterogeneity in mutation and recombination rates.

* **Strong recent selection has acted on the lactase (*LCT*) locus, the MHC region, and lighter hair/eye pigmentation loci in the ancestors of modern Britons over the past 2,000–3,000 years: [Well Supported].**  
  The single-locus signals at *LCT* ($P = 1 \times 10^{-23}$) and *MHC* ($P = 2 \times 10^{-15}$; maximum $\text{SDS} = 7.9$) far exceed the genome-wide significance threshold ($P < 5 \times 10^{-8}$; Figure 3A–B), and curated large-effect pigmentation variants (e.g., *OCA2/HERC2*, *SLC45A2*, *IRF4*, *TYR*, *SLC24A4*) show a clear directional shift toward derived alleles ($P = 3 \times 10^{-9}$; Figure 3C). Because these variants have large, well-replicated effects established across independent cohorts and functional assays, they are insensitive to subtle genome-wide stratification.

* **Polygenic selection for increased height has driven allele frequency shifts across most of the genome (85% of all SNPs): [Fragile].**  
  While the mean tSDS across the 551 genome-wide significant height SNPs is positive ($\text{mean} = 0.30, P = 4 \times 10^{-11}$; Figure 3D), extending the rank correlation to all 4.5 million genome-wide SNPs ($\rho = 0.078, P = 9 \times 10^{-74}$; Figure 4A) is highly sensitive to population structure. Because SDS in UK10K is strongly correlated with the allele frequency difference between northern and southern Europe ($\rho = 0.32 \pm 0.005$; Figure 2E), any uncorrected North–South European stratification in GWAS $Z$-scores produces a spurious genome-wide correlation with tSDS. In Figure 4A, even completely non-significant SNPs ($P \in [0.5, 0.99]$) exhibit a positive mean tSDS of $\sim 0.05\text{–}0.10$. Although the authors rely on a family-based height study (reference 20) and bivariate LD score regression (Figure 4B) to argue against stratification, reference 20's public summary statistics still incorporate population-level information/imputation across European cohorts, and bivariate LDSC assumes that stratification effects are independent of LD score—an assumption that breaks down when clinal allele frequency differences and haplotype-window singleton densities covary with local LD.

* **Polygenic adaptation has pervasively shaped numerous other complex human traits (infant head circumference, birth weight, female hip size, metabolic traits, male BMI, menarche): [Overstated].**  
  In Figure 4C, the 43 complex traits are screened using the all-SNP Spearman correlation with tSDS, and traits are highlighted as validated if bivariate LDSC achieves a nominal **one-sided $P < 0.05$** without multiple-hypothesis testing correction across the 43 traits. Given the strong correlation between SDS and European latitudinal structure (Figure 2E) and the authors' own acknowledgement in the final paragraph that *"the confounding role—if any—of population structure in contributing to these signals remains to be fully determined,"* framing these multi-trait correlations in the Abstract as showing that *"polygenic adaptation has played a pervasive role in shaping genotypic and phenotypic variation in modern humans"* overstates the evidence.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Control Directly for the European North–South Allele Frequency Gradient in tSDS Analyses
* **WHY:** Figure 2E establishes that SDS correlates strongly ($\rho = 0.32$) with the allele frequency difference between northern and southern European populations ($\text{GBR/CEU}$ vs. $\text{TSI/IBS}$). Consequently, any residual North–South European stratification in GWAS summary statistics will induce a false-positive correlation between $Z_{\text{GWAS}}$ and $\text{SDS}$ across millions of SNPs.
* **WHAT:** Residualize SDS (or conduct partial correlation and multivariable block-jackknife regression) conditioning on 1000 Genomes / European geographic allele frequency differences ($\Delta p_{\text{GBR}-\text{TSI}}$, $\Delta p_{\text{CEU}-\text{IBS}}$, and principal components of European allele frequencies) before testing for correlation with GWAS effect sizes.
* **HOW:** For each of the 4.5 million SNPs, fit the linear model $\text{SDS}_j = \beta_0 + \beta_1 (p_{\text{GBR},j} - p_{\text{TSI},j}) + \beta_2 (p_{\text{CEU},j} - p_{\text{IBS},j}) + \epsilon_j$, and re-compute the genome-wide Spearman rank correlation $\rho(\text{tSDS}_{\text{resid}}, Z_{\text{GWAS}})$ and block-jackknife $P$-values for height and the 43 traits in Figure 4A–C using the residualized scores $\epsilon_j$.

#### 2. Re-evaluate Bivariate LD Score Regression Under Clinal Stratification and Apply Multiple-Testing Correction
* **WHY:** Standard bivariate LDSC assumes that environmental/ancestry stratification produces covariance that is flat with respect to LD score $\ell_j$. However, under recent clinal divergence or admixture across Europe, SNPs with higher LD scores tag more linked variants and share longer coalescent haplotype blocks, causing both clinal allele frequency shifts and SDS to scale with $\ell_j$. Furthermore, using an unadjusted one-sided $P < 0.05$ threshold for LDSC across 43 traits inflates false discoveries.
* **WHAT:** Simulate clinal population stratification between two diverging European demes in the coalescent framework used for Figure 2, test whether bivariate LDSC between a stratified non-selected GWAS phenotype and SDS yields a positive slope with LD score, and apply Benjamini-Hochberg FDR (or Bonferroni) correction to the LDSC $P$-values across all 43 traits in Figure 4C.
* **HOW:** Using the authors' existing coalescent simulator, simulate a neutral trait with an environmental mean difference between a southern deme (TSI-like) and northern deme (GBR-like) without natural selection, compute GWAS $Z$-scores in a weakly stratified sample alongside UK10K-like SDS scores, and run bivariate LDSC. Report both the LDSC intercept (which explicitly measures shared population stratification/cryptic relatedness) and the FDR-adjusted two-sided LDSC slope $P$-values for all 43 traits in Figure 4C.

#### 3. Quantify Sensitivity to Low-Coverage Sequencing Errors and Sub-Cohort Batch Effects in UK10K
* **WHY:** The UK10K dataset ($n = 3,195$) merges two distinct cohorts (ALSPAC and TwinsUK) sequenced at relatively low average depth ($\sim 7\times$). Because singletons are the variant class most heavily affected by base-calling errors, coverage fluctuations, and batch differences between cohorts, differential singleton false-positive or false-negative rates between sub-cohorts or across GC-content/coverage strata could bias tip-length estimates for alleles with slight frequency differences between ALSPAC and TwinsUK.
* **WHAT:** Report the singleton concordance/error rate in UK10K and verify that SDS scores computed separately within ALSPAC and within TwinsUK are concordant and unconfounded by sequencing depth.
* **HOW:** Compute SDS independently within the ALSPAC subset and the TwinsUK subset, report the genome-wide correlation between $\text{SDS}_{\text{ALSPAC}}$ and $\text{SDS}_{\text{TwinsUK}}$, and verify that excluding transition singletons (or CpG dinucleotides and low-mappability regions) leaves the top single-locus hits (*LCT*, *MHC*, *WDFY4*) and polygenic correlations unchanged.

#### 4. Temper Claims of Pervasive Genome-Wide Polygenic Selection in the Abstract and Main Text
* **WHY:** The Abstract states without qualification that *"recent selection for increased height has driven allele frequency shifts across most of the genome"* and *"polygenic adaptation has played a pervasive role,"* whereas the final paragraph of the main text notes that *"the confounding role—if any—of population structure in contributing to these signals remains to be fully determined."*
* **WHAT:** Bring the Abstract and Discussion into alignment with the stratification caveat at the end of the paper.
* **HOW:** Revise the last two sentences of the Abstract to state that genome-wide correlations between SDS and GWAS summary statistics are consistent with polygenic selection on height and other complex traits, while noting that subtle uncorrected population structure in large meta-analyses could also contribute to all-SNP correlations.

#### 5. Include Core Mathematical Formulations in the Main Text
* **WHY:** The main text currently contains zero mathematical equations, deferring the entire likelihood model for singleton distances, the handling of censored intervals at chromosome boundaries, and the standardization formula to the external Supplementary Materials.
* **WHAT & HOW:** Add a concise 1-paragraph mathematical summary (or Box) to the main text stating: (1) how the left and right singleton distances $d_{i,L}, d_{i,R}$ for diploid individual $i$ with genotype $g_i \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ are modeled as a function of the unknown allele-specific mean tip lengths $\hat{t}_A, \hat{t}_D$ and local mutation/recombination parameters; (2) the explicit definition of the raw score $\text{rSDS} = \log(\hat{t}_A / \hat{t}_D)$; and (3) the block-jackknife block size (in Mb or cM) used to compute standard errors in Figures 2E and 4A.
