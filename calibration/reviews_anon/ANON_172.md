## 1. Executive Summary & Synthesis

This manuscript introduces `DiffFormer`, a conditional denoising diffusion probabilistic model (DDPM) with a Transformer denoising backbone designed to estimate cell-type proportions from bulk RNA-seq profiles. The authors evaluate `DiffFormer` against six baseline methods (`MuSiC`, `CPM`, `SCDC`, `DecOT`, `Add_GP`, and an `ElasticNetCV` proxy labeled `ADAPTS`) across four simulated pseudo-bulk datasets generated from scRNA-seq (`pbmc3k`, `pbmc68k`, `liver`, `pancreas`) and one real bulk RNA-seq dataset of 12 human PBMC samples with flow cytometry proportions (`GSE107011`).

While conditional generative modeling for bulk deconvolution is conceptually interesting, the manuscript suffers from severe methodological and mathematical flaws that invalidate its benchmark comparisons:
1. **Direct numerical contradiction on the real-world `GSE107011` benchmark:** Table 2 shows that `CPM` achieves a substantially *lower* (better) Overall RMSE (`0.1207`) than `DiffFormer` (`0.1752`) on `GSE107011`. Yet Section 3.5, Section 3.6, and Table 3 claim that `DiffFormer` significantly outperformed `CPM` in RMSE with $p = 2.8 \times 10^{-5}$ and Cohen's $d = 1.67$.
2. **Simpson's paradox in flattened "Overall PCC" vs. cell-type-level failure:** The authors compute "Overall PCC" (`0.5419` on `GSE107011`) by flattening the $12 \times 7$ sample-by-cell-type matrix into an 84-element vector, which conflates between-cell-type mean abundance differences with cross-sample accuracy. In Section 4.3, the authors admit that for individual cell types such as MAIT cells, `DiffFormer`'s cross-sample correlation is strongly negative (`PCC = -0.59`), directly contradicting their claim in Section 3.5 that `DiffFormer` provides *"stable, reliable predictions for every single cell type."*
3. **In-sample cell-pool leakage in pseudo-bulk simulations:** Both the 5,000 training pseudo-bulks and 500 test pseudo-bulks are drawn with replacement from the *exact same scRNA-seq cell pool* using the same $\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha = \mathbf{1})$ distribution without holding out cells or donors for testing.
4. **Mathematical and architectural inconsistencies:** Equations (1)–(2) define an unconstrained Gaussian DDPM on $\mathbb{R}^K$ without specifying how the compositional simplex constraint ($\hat{y}_k \ge 0, \sum_{k=1}^K \hat{y}_k = 1$) is enforced, and Section 2.3 hardcodes input/output dimensions (`7` cell types and `5000` genes) that only match `GSE107011` and contradict the four pseudo-bulk datasets (`5, 6, 8, 9` cell types and `2,000` genes).

## 2. Claim-by-Claim Evaluation

* **Claim 1:** *On simulated pseudo-bulk datasets (`pbmc3k`, `pbmc68k`, `liver`, `pancreas`), DiffFormer achieves ~10-fold lower RMSE (~0.012–0.018) and higher PCC (>0.99) than all existing deconvolution methods.* — **[Methodologically Flawed]**
  * **Evidence & Critique:** In Section 2.2 (`Simulation Data Generation`), for each scRNA-seq dataset, 5,500 pseudo-bulk mixtures are generated by sampling 500 single cells with replacement from the *same single-cell dataset* according to $\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha = \mathbf{1})$ proportions, and split into 5,000 training and 500 test samples. There is no cell-level or donor-level split between the scRNA-seq pool used to synthesize the 5,000 training mixtures and the 500 test mixtures. Furthermore, `DiffFormer` is trained directly on 5,000 supervised pairs $(X_{\text{sim}}, y_{\text{sim}})$ drawn from the target $\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha = \mathbf{1})$ prior and target cell pool, whereas reference-based baselines (`MuSiC`, `SCDC`, `CPM`) only receive summary cell-type expression signatures without supervised training on the simulated mixtures or knowledge of the symmetric Dirichlet prior. Testing on pseudo-bulks aggregated from the identical cells used during training measures in-sample memorization rather than out-of-sample deconvolution generalization.

* **Claim 2:** *On the real-world `GSE107011` PBMC benchmark, DiffFormer significantly outperforms all competing methods in both RMSE and PCC (Table 2, Table 3, Section 3.5–3.6).* — **[Methodologically Flawed]**
  * **Evidence & Critique:** Look closely at Table 2 (`Deconvolution performance comparison on the real-world GSE107011 dataset`, line 226):
    * `DiffFormer`: **Overall RMSE = 0.1752**, Overall PCC = 0.5419
    * `CPM`: **Overall RMSE = 0.1207**, Overall PCC = 0.1190
    * `ADAPTS`: **Overall RMSE = 0.1721**, Overall PCC = 0.5067
    Both `CPM` (`0.1207`) and `ADAPTS` (`0.1721`) have *lower* (better) Overall RMSE in Table 2 than `DiffFormer` (`0.1752`). Yet Section 3.5 (line 232) claims that `DiffFormer` *"achieved the lowest overall RMSE (0.1752)"*, and Table 3 (line 263) claims a paired $t$-test comparing per-sample RMSE of `DiffFormer` vs. `CPM` on `GSE107011` yields $p = 2.8 \times 10^{-5}$ and Cohen's $d = 1.67$ in favor of `DiffFormer`! Because Overall RMSE and per-sample RMSE across the $N=12$ samples of `GSE107011` satisfy $\text{RMSE}_{\text{overall}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{12}\sum_{i=1}^{12} \text{RMSE}_i^2}$, it is mathematically impossible for `CPM` to have an Overall RMSE of `0.1207` vs. `DiffFormer`'s `0.1752` while `DiffFormer` beats `CPM` across those same 12 samples with $d = 1.67, p = 2.8 \times 10^{-5}$ unless either Table 2 or Table 3 is fabricated or computed on completely different numbers.

* **Claim 3:** *DiffFormer provides "stable, reliable predictions for every single cell type" on `GSE107011`, avoiding the catastrophic failures of traditional methods (Section 3.5, line 236).* — **[Overstated]**
  * **Evidence & Critique:** In Section 3.5 (line 232), the reported "Overall PCC" (`0.5419`) is computed by flattening all 12 samples $\times$ 7 cell types into an 84-element vector. When a matrix with large differences in mean abundance across cell types (e.g., abundant T/monocyte populations vs. rare plasmablasts/DCs) is flattened, the overall correlation is dominated by between-cell-type mean differences rather than within-cell-type accuracy across patients (Simpson's paradox). In Section 4.3 (`Limitations and Future Directions`, offset 46080, line 33), the authors themselves admit that on individual cell types in `GSE107011`, `DiffFormer` fails: *"for certain cell types, such as MAIT cells (PCC = -0.59), performance remained poor."* A negative correlation of $-0.59$ refutes the claim in Section 3.5 that the model provides reliable predictions for every single cell type.

* **Claim 4:** *The ablation study confirms that both the diffusion process and the Transformer architecture are essential to DiffFormer's performance (Section 3.7, Table 4).* — **[Fragile]**
  * **Evidence & Critique:** Table 4 reports ablation metrics (`DiffFormer (Full)`, `w/o Diffusion`, `w/o Transformer`) exclusively on the `pbmc3k` in-sample pseudo-bulk dataset (`RMSE = 0.0124` vs. `0.0289` and `0.0351`), where training and test pseudo-bulks share the same single cells, and omits any ablation on the real-world `GSE107011` dataset.

## 3. Technical & Methodological Critique

### Major Flaws

1. **Mathematical Incompleteness of Simplex Enforcement in Gaussian DDPM:**
   * Cell-type proportions lie on the probability simplex $\Delta^{K-1} = \{y \in \mathbb{R}^K : y_k \ge 0, \sum_{k=1}^K y_k = 1\}$. However, Equations (1)–(3) define a standard unconstrained Gaussian forward and reverse diffusion process in $\mathbb{R}^K$ ($x_t = \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_t} x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_t}\epsilon, \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$). Starting from $x_T \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$ and iterating the reverse Gaussian transitions produces unconstrained real vectors that can have negative entries and do not sum to 1. Nowhere in Section 2.3 or Section 2.4 do the authors specify whether outputs are clipped at zero and renormalized, projected onto the simplex, or modeled in log-ratio (CLR/ILR) coordinates.

2. **Hardcoded Architecture Dimensions Contradicting the Datasets:**
   * Section 2.3 (`Model Architecture`, line 91) states: *"Initially, two separate linear layers project the input proportions (7 to 128) and the conditional bulk expression (5000 to 128) into a shared 128-dimensional embedding space... and a final linear projection layer (128 to 7) maps the processed features to the predicted noise."* However, according to Section 2.1 (`Datasets and Preprocessing`, lines 37–54), only `GSE107011` has 7 cell types and 5,000 HVGs; the four simulated datasets have `8` (`pbmc3k`), `5` (`pbmc68k`), `6` (`liver`), and `9` (`pancreas`) cell types and `2,000` HVGs!

3. **Contradictory Definitions and Improper Implementations of Baselines:**
   * In the Introduction (line 17), `CPM` is defined as *"the non-negative least squares-based Cistrome-based aPproach to Multigene profiling (CPM) [5]"*, whereas Reference [5] is Frishberg et al. (2019) *Cellular Population Mapping*, which uses support vector regression—as stated later in Section 2.5 (line 108: *"CPM: A popular method based on support vector regression [5]"*). Defining the same acronym (`CPM`) as two completely different names and algorithms within the same paper is a hallmark of unverified text generation.
   * Similarly, `ADAPTS` (Section 2.5, line 116) is not run using the published `ADAPTS` R package (Danziger et al. 2019, Ref. [17]), but is instead replaced by a custom `scikit-learn` `ElasticNetCV` regression fit directly on mean expression profiles.
   * For `GSE107011` (`Section 2.1`, line 53), the authors state that they used `pbmc3k` (`2,700` cells from a single healthy donor on 10x Chromium v1) as the single-cell reference to deconvolve 12 bulk RNA-seq PBMC samples profiled across 7 flow-sorted populations (`MAIT`, `Plasmablasts`, `T_gd`, `T_CD8_Memory`, `Monocytes_C`, `DC_Myeloid`, `B_Naive`), even though `pbmc3k` standard annotations (`Naive CD4 T`, `Memory CD4`, `CD14+ Mono`, `B`, `CD8 T`, `FCGR3A+ Mono`, `NK`, `DC`, `Platelet`) do not even match those 7 flow-cytometry populations (`MAIT`, `T_gd`, `Plasmablasts`)! The manuscript never explains how 8 standard `pbmc3k` clusters were mapped to the 7 `GSE107011` populations during training.

4. **Internal Numerical Inconsistencies Between Text and Table 1:**
   * For `pbmc68k`, Table 1 (line 165) reports `DiffFormer` RMSE = `0.0124`, whereas Section 3.3 (line 187) states `"achieving a remarkably low RMSE of 0.0122"`.
   * For `liver`, Table 1 (line 165) reports `DiffFormer` RMSE = `0.0149`, whereas Section 3.4 (line 206) states `"achieved an exceptionally low RMSE of 0.0150"`.

## 4. Constructive Recommendations

1. **Resolve the `GSE107011` Table 2 vs. Table 3 Contradiction:** Explain or correct the mathematical impossibility whereby `CPM` has a lower Overall RMSE (`0.1207`) than `DiffFormer` (`0.1752`) in Table 2 while `DiffFormer` is claimed to outperform `CPM` in RMSE with $p = 2.8 \times 10^{-5}$ and $d = 1.67$ in Table 3.
2. **Eliminate Single-Cell Pool Leakage in Pseudo-Bulk Benchmarks:** Split single-cell datasets by *donor* (or at minimum into disjoint train and test cell pools) *before* generating training and testing pseudo-bulks, and evaluate out-of-distribution generalization when test proportions are not drawn from the exact same symmetric $\text{Dirichlet}(\alpha = \mathbf{1})$ distribution used for training.
3. **Report Per-Cell-Type Cross-Sample Correlations:** Replace the flattened 84-element "Overall PCC" with the mean and individual per-cell-type Pearson and Spearman correlations across the 12 samples of `GSE107011`, and clearly document how the cell types in the single-cell training reference were harmonized with the 7 `GSE107011` flow cytometry populations.
4. **Specify Simplex Constraint Handling and Fix Baseline Descriptions:** Explicitly state how non-negativity and sum-to-one constraints are enforced during DDPM sampling, parameterize the architecture dimensions $(G, K)$ generically in Section 2.3, and correct the contradictory definition of `CPM` in the Introduction.
