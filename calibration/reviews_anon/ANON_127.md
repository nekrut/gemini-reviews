# Critical Peer Review (`ANON_127`)

## Section 1: Overall Assessment & Quantitative Scorecard

**Overall Score:** **8.1 / 10.0** (Strong / Sound)
* **Score Breakdown:** Rigor: 2.7/3.5 | Claims: 2.0/2.5 | Biology: 1.7/2.0 | Reproducibility: 1.7/2.0

**Executive Verdict:**
This evolutionary genomics study evaluates the prevalence and correlates of positive selection across 977 immune genes (classified into six functional categories: pattern recognition receptors [PRRs], cytokines and their receptors, other cell surface proteins, intracellular signaling proteins, extracellular proteases, and effectors) and 1,005 non-immune control genes across 30 rodent species (Muridae and Cricetidae). The authors combine TOGA ortholog projection, PRANK codon alignments, PAML `codeml` (M1a vs. M2a), and HyPhy BUSTED (explicitly testing the impact of synonymous rate variation $+S$ and multi-nucleotide substitutions $+MH$ via model averaging), and generate a novel 10-organ wild bank vole (*Myodes glareolus*) RNA-seq dataset ($N = 19$ animals across two seasons) alongside house mouse polymorphism ($p_N/p_S$) and ENCODE expression data to separate pathogen-driven positive selection from relaxed selective constraint. While the phylogenetic and bioinformatic execution is thorough and fully reproducible via an archived GitHub repository, the study's central functional comparison is weakened by severe power loss under the conservative BUSTED $+S+MH$ model—forcing the exclusion of three of the six immune categories (PRRs, effectors, and proteases) from the constraint-adjusted GLM—and by the conceptual ambiguity of using intraspecific $p_N/p_S$ as a proxy for neutral/purifying constraint in immune loci subject to balancing or recurrent selection.

## Section 2: Audit of Key Claims

1. **Claim 1:** *"Instead, regardless of how we controlled for constraint, we consistently found highest prevalence of positive selection in the category cytokines and their receptors, and the category 'other cell surface proteins'. These are categories where few genes are involved in direct interactions with pathogens."* (Section: Abstract, p. 1; Results, "Differences between functional categories in the prevalence of positive selection", pp. 6–8)
   * **Tag:** `[Moderately Supported]`
   * **Evidence & Audit:** Across all five selection pipelines in Table 2, "cytokines and their receptors" ($n = 213$) and "other cell surface proteins" ($n = 214$) exhibit higher point estimates of positive selection than intracellular signaling ($n = 411$). However, the claim that these categories have higher positive selection than direct pathogen-interacting categories (PRRs, $n = 56$; effectors, $n = 47$) *after controlling for selective constraint* is only partially tested: under the preferred conservative model (`BUSTED model-average q < 0.2`), only 3 PRRs ($5.4\%$), 2 effectors ($4.3\%$), and 2 proteases ($5.6\%$) are significant (Table 2), and the authors explicitly excluded PRRs, effectors, and proteases from the $p_N/p_S$-adjusted binomial GLMs (Results, p. 7: *"excluding categories with 3 or fewer positively selected genes, i.e., PRRs, effectors, and extracellular proteases"*). Furthermore, in the less conservative `BUSTED (-S -MH) q < 0.2` model where all six categories are retained, effector proteins actually have the highest raw proportion of positive selection ($68.1\%$ vs. $59.2\%$ in cytokines, Table 2) and do not differ significantly from cytokines or cell surface proteins after controlling for $p_N/p_S$ (Fig. 3, left column).

2. **Claim 2:** *"Accounting for S and MH makes tests for positive selection much more conservative... Using this approach [model averaging] and correcting for multiple testing (q < 0.2), the number of positively selected genes dropped an order of magnitude compared with when not accounting for S and MH: 37 (3.8%) immune genes and 20 (2.0%) control genes were found to be positively selected (Table 2; χ² = 4.91, P = 0.027)."* (Section: Results, "Tests for positive selection", p. 5; Table 2)
   * **Tag:** `[Well Supported]`
   * **Evidence & Audit:** Table 2 clearly quantifies the drop in called genes from $363$ ($37.2\%$) immune and $218$ ($21.7\%$) control genes under standard `BUSTED (-S -MH)` ($q < 0.2$) down to $37$ ($3.8\%$) immune and $20$ ($2.0\%$) control genes under `BUSTED model-average` ($q < 0.2$). This aligns with recent molecular evolution benchmarks showing that unmodeled synonymous rate variation and multi-hit substitutions inflate false positives in branch-site likelihood ratio tests.

3. **Claim 3:** *"Importantly, this suggests that the varying proportions of genes under positive selection among different functional categories of immune genes is not simply due to different levels of selective constraint (Supporting Information: Fig. S3)..."* (Section: Results, p. 7; Fig. 3; Table S2)
   * **Tag:** `[Moderately Supported]`
   * **Evidence & Audit:** When conditioning on intraspecific $p_N/p_S$ (from *Mus musculus* or *Myodes glareolus*) in binomial GLMs (Fig. 3) or when conditioning on tissue specificity ($\tau$) and expression level (Fig. 5, Table S2), cytokines and cell surface proteins remain significantly elevated over intracellular signaling and non-immune control genes. However, when protein–protein interaction degree (number of interacting proteins from STRING) and coding sequence (CDS) length are simultaneously included in the multivariable GLM (Table S2), the effect of `Category` is no longer statistically significant in three of the four models (`codeml` mouse: $P = 0.28$; `BUSTED` mouse: $P = 0.34$; `BUSTED` vole: $P = 0.097$), indicating that network centrality, gene length, and expression breadth account for much of the variance between categories.

## Section 3: Technical Critique & Actionable Recommendations

### Major Methodological & Statistical Issues

1. **Exclusion of Direct Pathogen-Interacting Categories (PRRs and Effectors) from Primary Constraint Models Due to Low Power (Pillar 4 & Pillar 5):**
   * **Issue:** The central biological hypothesis introduced in the Abstract and Introduction contrasts proteins that directly recognize or destroy pathogens (PRRs, $n = 56$; effectors, $n = 47$) against cytokines ($n = 213$) and cell surface receptors ($n = 214$). Because sample sizes in PRRs and effectors are 4-fold smaller than in cytokines/cell surface proteins—and effector peptides (such as defensins and antimicrobial peptides) have short coding sequences with lower per-gene statistical power in codon models—only 3 PRRs and 2 effectors pass $q < 0.2$ under BUSTED model-averaging, leading to their omission from the primary constraint-adjusted GLMs (Fig. 3 right column, Fig. 5 right column, Table S2).
   * **Remediation:** (i) Explicitly temper the Abstract and Discussion to clarify that under the conservative BUSTED $+S+MH$ model, the study lacks statistical power to compare PRRs and effectors against cytokines after adjusting for constraint, and that under the standard models (`codeml` and `BUSTED -S -MH`), effectors have a positive selection prevalence comparable to cytokines once $p_N/p_S$ and CDS length are controlled for; (ii) test a pooled "direct pathogen interaction" category ($\text{PRRs} + \text{Effectors}$, $n = 103$) or use Firth's penalized-likelihood logistic regression (`logistf`) so that categories with small event counts are not dropped from the GLMs.

2. **Conceptual Ambiguity and Length-Dependent Pseudocount Bias in Intraspecific $p_N/p_S$ (`pN/p(S + 1)`) (Pillar 3 & Pillar 4):**
   * **Issue:** First, the authors use intraspecific $p_N/p_S$ (`log10((pN/p(S + 1)) + 0.001)`) as a measure of pure "selective constraint" (relaxed purifying selection). In immune genes, however, intraspecific nonsynonymous polymorphism ($p_N$) is frequently elevated by balancing selection, frequency-dependent selection, or incomplete selective sweeps within populations; conditioning on $p_N/p_S$ therefore partially regresses out ongoing pathogen-mediated selection rather than strictly neutral relaxation. Second, adding a $+1$ pseudocount to the raw count or rate of synonymous polymorphisms (`p(S + 1)`, Materials and Methods, p. 14) introduces a systematic gene-length bias: in short genes (such as cytokines and antimicrobial effectors) with very few synonymous segregating sites ($S \in \{0, 1, 2\}$), adding $+1$ to $S$ inflates the denominator by $50\text{--}100\%$, artificially depressing estimated $p_N/p_S$ in short genes relative to long genes.
   * **Remediation:** Verify whether the results in Figure 3 hold when using an unbiased estimator of selective constraint (such as the Direction of Selection statistic $\text{DoS} = D_n/(D_n + D_s) - P_n/(P_n + P_s)$ or $\omega_a$, the rate of adaptive substitution relative to neutral divergence) and when including $\log(\text{CDS length})$ alongside $p_N/p_S$ in the Figure 3 GLMs.

### Minor Presentation & Numerical Issues

1. **Counterintuitive Relationship Between Nominal $P < 0.05$ and FDR $q < 0.2$ in Table 2 (Pillar 4 & Pillar 7):**
   * In Table 2 (`BUSTED (-S -MH)`), the number of significant genes at FDR $q < 0.2$ ($218$ control, $363$ immune; total $581/1,982 = 29.3\%$) is *higher* than at unadjusted nominal $P < 0.05$ ($201$ control, $356$ immune; total $557/1,982 = 28.1\%$). While this is mathematically consistent under the Benjamini–Hochberg step-up procedure when the discovery fraction exceeds $25\%$ and the chosen FDR threshold is permissive ($q = 0.20 \implies P_{\text{cutoff}} \approx 0.293 \times 0.20 = 0.0586 > 0.05$), choosing $q < 0.20$ means up to $20\%$ of selected genes are expected false positives and creates a visual paradox in Table 2 where "multiple-testing correction" increases the number of hits. Adding a brief footnote to Table 2 explaining why $q < 0.20$ corresponds to $P < 0.0586$ in row 4—or reporting standard $q < 0.05$ and $q < 0.10$ thresholds—would prevent reader confusion.
