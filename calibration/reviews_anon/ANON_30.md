### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript benchmarks six differential gene expression (DEG) methods—three parametric methods (`DESeq2`, `edgeR` quasi-likelihood F-test, and `limma-voom`) and three non-parametric methods (`NOISeq`, `dearseq`, and the two-sample Wilcoxon rank-sum test on TMM-normalized counts-per-million, CPM)—across 13 large-sample human population RNA-seq datasets ($N = 100\text{--}1,376$), comprising one melanoma anti-PD-1 immunotherapy cohort (`GSE91061`, 51 pre-nivolumab vs. 58 on-nivolumab samples), six GTEx tissue-pair comparisons, and six TCGA tumor-vs.-normal comparisons. Using 1,000 condition-label permutations per dataset to evaluate false discovery rate (FDR) control and 50 row-permuted semi-synthetic datasets per cohort to evaluate power and FDR across sample sizes ($n = 2\text{--}100$ per group), the authors report that `DESeq2` and `edgeR` exhibit severely inflated false positives in large cohorts due to negative binomial model violations and expression outliers, whereas the Wilcoxon rank-sum test consistently controls the FDR and achieves comparable or superior power whenever per-condition sample size exceeds $n = 8$. All analyzed datasets are publicly accessible from GEO (`GSE91061`), GTEx v8, and TCGA GDC Xena Hub.

Demonstrating that simple non-parametric rank tests are competitive and robust against heavy-tailed outliers in large population-scale transcriptomic cohorts ($n \ge 50$) is a practical and valuable message for the bioinformatics community. However, several striking anomalies emphasized in the paper—most notably that `DESeq2` and `edgeR` identify *more* DEGs in 84.9% and 78.9% of permuted immunotherapy datasets than in the unpermuted data, and that these permuted hits are strongly enriched for immune GO terms (Fig. 1A–E)—stem from unacknowledged methodological flaws in the authors' benchmark design: (1) ignoring the longitudinal paired-patient structure of the `GSE91061` pre- vs. on-nivolumab biopsies and the matched tumor-normal patient structure of the TCGA datasets during both model fitting and unrestricted label permutation; (2) permuting raw read counts independently across rows ($X'_i, Y'_i$) in the semi-synthetic benchmark, which decouples gene counts from sample sequencing depths ($L_j$) and corrupts library-size normalization; and (3) applying `vcd::goodfit` to rounded normalized counts (`round(normalized_counts)`) rather than raw counts with a size offset, which invalidates the negative binomial goodness-of-fit test. Re-running the permutations with within-patient block/pair preservation, permuting size-factor-normalized values (or scaling by recipient library size) in the semi-synthetic generator, and correcting the goodness-of-fit formulation are straightforward computational fixes that will put the benchmark on solid statistical ground.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Claim 1: In the anti-PD-1 immunotherapy dataset (`GSE91061`), `DESeq2` and `edgeR` identify more DEGs in ~80–85% of randomly permuted datasets than in the original dataset, and these spurious permuted DEGs are enriched for immune response GO terms because of negative binomial model violations. [Methodologically Flawed]**  
  In `GSE91061` (Riaz et al., 51 pre-nivolumab and 58 on-nivolumab melanoma biopsies), the majority of samples are **paired longitudinal biopsies (`pre` and `on` therapy) collected from the same patients** (with substantial inter-patient heterogeneity in baseline T-cell infiltration and prior ipilimumab exposure). However, in *Methods* (lines 77 and 96), the authors fit an **unpaired** two-group design (`~ condition`, omitting patient ID) and generated 1,000 negative controls by **unrestricted random permutation** of the 109 labels across all samples. In the original dataset, each paired patient contributes one `pre` and one `on` biopsy, so large patient-to-patient differences in baseline tumor immune infiltration cancel out between the two treatment groups (yielding few marginal DEGs). When labels are permuted completely at random without keeping each patient's pair split across the two groups, ~50% of paired patients have *both* of their biopsies assigned to the same permuted group. Because baseline immune infiltration (`cytolytic/T-cell activation` genes) varies dramatically between "hot" and "cold" melanoma patients, randomly clustering both biopsies of several "hot" tumors into one permuted group generates strong false-positive immune GO enrichments (Fig. 1E) and causes more DEGs to be called in the unblocked permutations than in the patient-balanced original data (Fig. 1A). The same unmodeled pairing issue applies to the six TCGA datasets, which consist of matched tumor and normal tissues from the same patients (line 64).

* **Claim 2: Goodness-of-fit testing (`vcd::goodfit`) demonstrates that spurious DEGs fail the negative binomial distributional assumption (Fig. 1F). [Methodologically Flawed]**  
  In *Methods* (lines 107–111), the authors test goodness-of-fit using:
  `summary(goodfit(round(normalized_counts), type = "nbinomial", par = list(size = 1/dispersion)))`.
  This test is mathematically invalid for two reasons. First, in `DESeq2` and `edgeR`, the negative binomial distribution models the **raw integer read counts** $K_{ij} \sim \text{NB}(\mu_{ij} = s_j q_i, \text{size} = 1/\alpha_i)$, *not* the normalized counts $\text{round}(K_{ij} / s_j)$. Scaling a negative binomial random variable by $1/s_j$ multiplies its variance by $1/s_j^2$, changing the mean–variance relationship from $\mu_{ij} + \alpha_i \mu_{ij}^2$ to $q_i / s_j + \alpha_i q_i^2$; whenever library size factors $s_j \ne 1$, $\text{round}(K_{ij} / s_j)$ does not follow a negative binomial distribution with dispersion $\alpha_i$ even under the exact null model. Second, `vcd::goodfit` performs a Pearson $\chi^2$ test across discrete integer bins without binning continuous/large counts; for expressed genes where rounded counts span hundreds of unique integers across $n=51\text{--}58$ samples, almost every bin has expected count $\ll 1$, violating the asymptotic $\chi^2$ approximation.

* **Claim 3: On semi-synthetic GTEx and TCGA datasets, only the Wilcoxon rank-sum test controls the FDR while achieving superior power for $n > 8$ per condition (Fig. 2). [Fragile]**  
  To construct the semi-synthetic matrices $(X', Y')$ (Methods, line 98), the authors keep raw read counts unpermuted ($X'_i = X_i, Y'_i = Y_i$) for "true DEGs" (defined as the intersection of all 6 methods at $\text{FDR} < 10^{-6}$), and for all remaining genes ("true non-DEGs"), they randomly permute the raw read counts $\sigma_i(X_i, Y_i)$ **independently for each gene $i$ across samples**. Because sample library sizes (sequencing depths $L_j = \sum_i K_{ij}$) vary several-fold across GTEx and TCGA samples, permuting raw counts independently row-by-row moves a raw count $K_{i, \sigma_i(j)}$ generated in sample $\sigma_i(j)$ (with sequencing depth $L_{\sigma_i(j)}$) into column $j$ (which has sequencing depth $L_j$). When `DESeq2`, `edgeR`, `limma-voom`, or `Wilcoxon` subsequently normalizes column $j$, the unpermuted true DEGs still reflect column $j$'s original depth $L_j$, whereas the row-permuted non-DEGs carry the depths $L_{\sigma_i(j)}$ of random donor samples. This row-wise scrambling of library depths injects synthetic overdispersion and group-correlated depth artifacts into the non-DEGs, distorting parametric dispersion/mean fits and biasing the semi-synthetic FDR benchmark.

* **Claim 4: `DESeq2` and `edgeR` show large call discrepancies (e.g., 8% overlap on `GSE91061`) under default settings. [Moderately Supported]**  
  While the discrepancy on `GSE91061` is real, part of the divergence across the 13 datasets stems from an asymmetric pre-filtering protocol in *Methods* (lines 79–89): the authors applied `edgeR::filterByExpr` before running `edgeR`, `limma-voom`, `dearseq`, and `Wilcoxon`, but did **not** apply `filterByExpr` before running `DESeq2` (relying instead on `DESeq2`'s internal `results()` independent filtering, which optimizes the filter threshold for the target $\alpha$ and tests a different universe of genes).

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Account for Paired Patient Structure in `GSE91061` and TCGA During Model Fitting and Permutation
* **WHY:** Treating longitudinal paired patient biopsies (`GSE91061` pre- vs. on-nivolumab) and matched tumor-normal samples (TCGA) as independent observations and permuting condition labels freely across patients breaks the within-patient pairing, converting inter-patient heterogeneity (such as baseline tumor immune infiltration) into artificial group differences in the permuted datasets.
* **WHAT:** Re-analyze `GSE91061` and the paired TCGA cohorts (1) restricting to unique patients (one sample per patient) when testing unpaired methods, and (2) using paired designs (`~ patient + condition` in `DESeq2`/`edgeR`/`limma-voom` and `wilcox.test(..., paired = TRUE)`) with **within-patient sign-flipping permutations** (randomly swapping the `pre` and `on` labels within each patient pair).
* **HOW:** Parse the patient identifiers from `GSE91061` (`GEO` sample metadata) and TCGA barcodes (`TCGA-XX-XXXX`). Generate 1,000 paired permutations where for each paired patient, the `pre` and `on` labels are swapped with probability $0.5$ (and any unpaired samples are permuted separately among unpaired samples). Re-generate Figure 1A–E to verify whether blocking by patient eliminates the paradoxical excess of permuted DEGs and immune GO terms.

#### 2. Preserve Sample Library Sizes When Generating Semi-Synthetic Datasets
* **WHY:** Permuting raw integer counts $K_{ij}$ independently for each gene $i$ across columns destroys the column-wise library size structure $L_j$, causing a count drawn from a high-depth sample to be normalized by the size factor of a low-depth sample.
* **WHAT:** Modify the semi-synthetic data generator so that row-wise permutations preserve each recipient sample's library size $L_j$.
* **HOW:** Before permuting non-DEG gene $i$ across samples, divide the raw counts by the original sample library size factors $s_j$ (producing normalized rates $q_{ij} = K_{ij} / s_j$), apply the random permutation $\sigma_i$ to the normalized rates $q_{ij}$, and multiply by the recipient column's size factor $s_j$ (rounding or Poisson-sampling to integer counts: $K'_{ij} = \text{round}(s_j \cdot q_{i, \sigma_i(j)})$). Re-run Figure 2A–B on these depth-consistent semi-synthetic matrices.

#### 3. Replace the Invalid `vcd::goodfit` Call on Normalized Counts with a Proper Offset-Aware Goodness-of-Fit Diagnostic
* **WHY:** Rounding normalized counts (`round(K_ij / s_j)`) alters the negative binomial variance function, and `vcd::goodfit` fails when counts span many unique integers with expected bin counts $<1$.
* **WHAT:** Evaluate negative binomial goodness-of-fit directly on the raw counts $K_{ij}$ using the fitted mean $\hat{\mu}_{ij} = \hat{s}_j \hat{q}_{ic}$ and dispersion $\hat{\alpha}_i$ via randomized quantile residuals (Dunn–Smyth residuals) or the deviance/Pearson goodness-of-fit statistic.
* **HOW:** For each gene $i$, compute the negative binomial deviance statistic $D_i = 2 \sum_j \left[ K_{ij} \log\frac{K_{ij}}{\hat{\mu}_{ij}} - (K_{ij} + \hat{\alpha}_i^{-1}) \log\frac{1 + \hat{\alpha}_i K_{ij}}{1 + \hat{\alpha}_i \hat{\mu}_{ij}} \right]$ (or convert each raw count $K_{ij}$ to its exact NB cumulative distribution percentile using `pnbinom(K_ij, mu = mu_ij, size = 1/alpha_i)` and test uniformity via a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test) and update Figure 1F.

#### 4. Standardize Pre-Filtering Across All Six Methods and Benchmark `Clipper` If Recommended
* **WHY:** Applying `edgeR::filterByExpr` to five methods while leaving `DESeq2` unfiltered confounds method comparisons in Figure S1, and recommending the authors' own `Clipper` method in the *Conclusions* (line 51) without evaluating it in the paper is unsupported.
* **WHAT:** Apply the exact same `filterByExpr` gene filter to the input count matrix of all six methods (including `DESeq2`), and either include `Clipper` in the small-sample benchmark (Fig. 2B for $n < 8$) or remove the unbenchmarked recommendation from the Conclusions.
* **HOW:** Pass the `filterByExpr`-filtered count matrix into `DESeqDataSetFromMatrix` with `results(dds, independentFiltering = FALSE)` so all six tools are evaluated on the identical set of tested genes.
