### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript develops an eight-gene prognostic risk score—termed the consensus machine learning-related signature (MLRS: *BIRC5, TPX2, AGER, CLIC5, EMCN, MAMDC2, SEMA3G,* and *TEK*)—for clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC). Using bulk transcriptomic data from TCGA ($n = 529$) as a discovery cohort and E-MTAB-1980 ($n = 101$), ICGC ($n = 91$), and GSE22541 as validation cohorts, the authors intersect weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) module candidates with differentially expressed genes and univariate Cox regression hits, then evaluate 101 combinations of 10 machine learning algorithms. The selected CoxBoost + Ridge model is subsequently correlated with clinicopathological stage, bulk immune deconvolution (ssGSEA, CIBERSORT, TIDE), non-renal immunotherapy cohorts (IMvigor210, GSE78220, GSE135222, GSE91061), cell-line drug sensitivity predictions (`oncoPredict` on CTRP/PRISM), single-cell RNA-seq from 7 ccRCC tumors (GSE210042; 30,663 cells), and in vitro *EMCN* perturbation assays in ccRCC cell lines (786-O, Caki-1) and HUVECs. Raw bulk and single-cell datasets are publicly available via TCGA, GEO, ICGC, and ArrayExpress, but no custom analysis code or trained model weights are provided.

While the integration of multi-cohort transcriptomics with a Transwell endothelial-tumor co-culture assay addresses a clinically relevant question in ccRCC risk stratification, the manuscript suffers from severe methodological and internal consistency flaws. First, the model selection procedure leaks validation data by ranking the 101 candidate models according to their average C-index across the external validation cohorts, invalidating those cohorts as independent tests of generalizability and biasing comparisons against 101 published signatures. Second, the Abstract directly contradicts the Results on two core findings—stating that the high-MLRS group has a favorable prognosis (whereas the Results show high-MLRS has poor survival) and that *EMCN* knockdown inhibited cell proliferation (whereas the Results show *EMCN* overexpression inhibited proliferation and *EMCN* knockdown in HUVECs promoted proliferation). Third, the immunotherapy validation relies entirely on urothelial carcinoma, melanoma, and lung cancer cohorts (while misidentifying IMvigor210 patients as ccRCC in Section 3.6), and Section 3.5 vs. Section 4 make mutually contradictory statements about whether CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, B cells, and M1 macrophages are enriched in the high-MLRS or low-MLRS group. Resolving these issues requires strictly separating training and validation cohorts during hyperparameter and model selection, re-evaluating immunotherapy response in actual ccRCC cohorts, reporting the exact mathematical formula of the MLRS, and reconciling the pervasive contradictions across the text.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **The 8-gene CoxBoost + Ridge MLRS generalizes across four independent cohorts and outperforms 101 published ccRCC signatures (Sections 3.2–3.3, Figures 2–3): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In Section 2.3 (line 37) and Section 4 (line 176), the authors state that the final MLRS model was chosen from 101 algorithm combinations based on the highest average C-index across the validation datasets. Using the validation cohorts (E-MTAB-1980, ICGC, GSE22541) to select the winning model constitutes direct test-set leakage. Consequently, the C-indices in Figures 2A and 2D–G reflect post-hoc selection bias rather than independent out-of-sample generalization, whereas the 101 published comparator signatures were not afforded model-selection tuning on these validation cohorts. Furthermore, differential expression (`limma`, $p < 0.05$) and univariate Cox filtering ($p < 0.05$, without false discovery rate correction) were performed on the entire TCGA dataset prior to 10-fold cross-validation, leaking survival labels across folds.

* **High- vs. low-MLRS groups exhibit distinct "cold" and "hot" tumor immune microenvironments (Section 3.5, Figure 5): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  The manuscript contradicts itself regarding the direction of immune infiltration. In Section 3.5 (line 136), the authors report that infiltration levels of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells (TIMER), B cells (CIBERSORT), and M1 macrophages ( quanTIseq) were *significantly greater in patients with high MLRS than in those with low MLRS* (Figure 5A), yet immediately conclude that low-MLRS tumors are "hot" and high-MLRS tumors are "cold." In the Discussion (line 180), the authors assert the exact opposite: that patients in the *low-risk (low-MLRS)* group had a highly infiltrated microenvironment characterized by the presence of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells (TIMER), B cells (CIBERSORT), and M1 macrophages (quanTIseq), whereas high-MLRS tumors were "cold" with "minimal immune cell infiltration." In addition, the Abstract (line 7) states that "the high-MLRS group had a favorable outcome in four independent datasets," directly contradicting Section 3.2 (line 107) and Figure 2B, where high-MLRS patients suffer worse overall survival.

* **MLRS predicts immunotherapy clinical benefit in ccRCC patients and identifies small-molecule drug sensitivities (Section 3.6, Figure 6): [Overstated].**  
  Section 3.6 claims to "evaluate the performance of the MLRS for predicting immunotherapeutic efficacy in ccRCC patients" and states that the authors "compared differences in long-term survival among patients with ccRCC after 3 months of treatment... in the IMvigor210 cohort" (line 147). However, IMvigor210 consists of metastatic urothelial carcinoma patients (line 59), GSE78220 and GSE91061 are melanoma cohorts, and GSE135222 is a non-small cell lung cancer cohort. None of the four immunotherapy cohorts contain ccRCC patients, and in GSE78220 the response difference is not statistically significant ($p = 0.058$, line 147). Because ccRCC exhibits distinct immunobiology compared to urothelial cancer and melanoma—and because renal-specific genes in the signature (such as *AGER*, noted in line 120 as highly specific to ccRCC) have different baseline expression in non-renal tissues—extrapolating from non-renal cohorts does not establish predictive utility for ccRCC immunotherapy.

* **MLRS is enriched in endothelial cells via Notch signaling, and endothelial *EMCN* suppresses ccRCC proliferation (Sections 3.7–3.8, Figures 7–8): [Fragile].**  
  scRNA-seq analysis of 7 ccRCC samples (GSE210042) shows that *EMCN* expression is restricted to endothelial cells and virtually absent from epithelial/tumor cells (Figures 8A–B). However, the authors first test *EMCN* function by ectopically overexpressing *EMCN* directly inside epithelial ccRCC tumor cell lines (786-O and Caki-1; Figures 8M–N), which does not match its endogenous cellular compartment. While the subsequent Transwell co-culture of *EMCN*-silenced HUVECs with 786-O and Caki-1 cells (Figures 8O–P) is biologically more appropriate, the Abstract (line 7) misstates the result as "*EMCN* knockdown inhibited cell proliferation" (in fact, *EMCN* knockdown in HUVECs *enhanced* ccRCC proliferation in Figure 8P), and the co-culture duration is inconsistently reported as 48 hours in the text (lines 73, 169) versus 4 days in the Figure 8P legend (lines 167, 203).

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Eliminate Validation Data Leakage in Machine Learning Model Selection
* **WHY:** Selecting the winning algorithm combination (CoxBoost + Ridge) by maximizing the average C-index across the external validation cohorts (E-MTAB-1980, ICGC, GSE22541) invalidates those cohorts as independent validation sets and inflates performance relative to published signatures.
* **WHAT:** Restrict all feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, and model selection strictly to the TCGA discovery cohort using nested cross-validation, then lock the model weights before evaluating once on the external cohorts.
* **HOW:** Within each outer fold of a 10-fold cross-validation on TCGA ($n = 529$), run `limma` differential expression (with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR $< 0.05$), WGCNA module filtering, and univariate Cox selection, followed by inner-loop hyperparameter tuning for the 101 algorithm combinations. Select the single model family with the highest mean out-of-fold C-index within TCGA alone, fit it on TCGA, explicitly report the linear predictor formula $\text{MLRS} = \sum_{i=1}^{8} \beta_i x_i$ with exact numerical coefficients $\beta_i$, and recompute Figures 2A–G on E-MTAB-1980, ICGC, and GSE22541 without post-hoc model filtering.

#### 2. Validate Immunotherapy Predictions in Actual ccRCC Clinical Cohorts
* **WHY:** None of the four immunotherapy cohorts analyzed in Section 3.6 (IMvigor210, GSE78220, GSE135222, GSE91061) include ccRCC patients, and line 147 erroneously refers to IMvigor210 urothelial cancer patients as "patients with ccRCC."
* **WHAT:** Evaluate the locked MLRS in publicly available transcriptomic cohorts of ccRCC patients treated with immune checkpoint blockade, and correct the cohort descriptions in Section 3.6.
* **HOW:** Download the bulk RNA-seq and clinical survival/response data from the CheckMate-025, CheckMate-009, and CheckMate-010 nivolumab trials in advanced ccRCC (Braun et al., *Nature Medicine* 2020) and/or the JAVELIN Renal 101 avelumab + axitinib trial (Motzer et al., *Nature Medicine* 2020). Compute MLRS using the locked TCGA weights, test the association with overall survival, progression-free survival, and objective response rate (CR/PR vs. SD/PD), and replace or contextualize the non-renal cohorts in Figure 6.

#### 3. Reconcile Contradictory Statements on Prognosis, Immune Infiltration, Drug Sensitivity, and *EMCN* Function
* **WHY:** Multiple sections of the manuscript state opposite directions for the same biological results, preventing readers from interpreting the data.
* **WHAT & HOW:** Audit and correct the following internal contradictions against the underlying R outputs:
  1. **Abstract vs. Results (Prognosis):** In the Abstract (line 7), change *"the high-MLRS group had a favorable outcome in four independent datasets"* to reflect that the **low-MLRS** group had a favorable outcome and the **high-MLRS** group had poor survival (matching Section 3.2, line 107, and Figure 2B).
  2. **Abstract vs. Results (*EMCN* Assay):** In the Abstract (line 7), change *"EMCN knockdown inhibited cell proliferation"* to accurately state that *EMCN* overexpression in ccRCC cells inhibited proliferation while *EMCN* knockdown in co-cultured HUVECs promoted ccRCC cell proliferation (matching Section 3.8, line 169, and Figures 8N, 8P).
  3. **Section 3.5 vs. Discussion (Immune Infiltration):** Reconcile line 136 (which states CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, B cells, and M1 macrophages are significantly higher in the **high-MLRS** group) with line 180 (which states those exact same four cell types characterize the **low-MLRS** group). Also reconcile line 136 (which places M1 macrophages higher in high-MLRS via quanTIseq) with line 140 (which places M1 macrophages higher in low-MLRS via CIBERSORT).
  4. **Section 2.7 vs. Section 3.6 (`oncoPredict` Direction):** Clarify whether the y-axis in Figures 6F–G represents predicted $\text{IC}_{50}$ / AUC (where lower values mean higher sensitivity, as defined in line 61) or an inverted sensitivity score. If Figure 6G plots predicted $\text{IC}_{50}$ or AUC, a positive correlation with MLRS (line 151) indicates *resistance* rather than sensitivity in the high-MLRS group.

* **WHAT & HOW:**

  2. **Section 3.1 (line 98 vs. line 102):** Line 98 states *"a total of 122 genes from those two modules (turquoise: 22 and blue: 12) were screened."* Since $22 + 12 = 34 \neq 122$, correct the module gene counts so they sum to 122 (or correct the total to 34 and explain how 33 genes remained after intersecting with DEGs in line 102).
