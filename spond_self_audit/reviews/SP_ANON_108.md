### 1. Executive Summary
This meta-analysis evaluates 855 Illumina HIV-1 *pol* (PR, RT, IN) amplicon next-generation sequencing (NGS) datasets across eight published studies (821 patients) to quantify how lowering minority variant calling thresholds ($20\%, 10\%, 5\%, 2\%, 1\%, 0.5\%, 0.2\%, \text{ and } 0.1\%$) inflates artifactual mutation calls arising from RT-PCR error and APOBEC3F/3G-mediated $G \to A$ hypermutation. Filtering bases at Phred $Q \ge 30$ and positions with coverage $\ge 1,000$ reads in an extended `HYDRA`/`Bowtie2` pipeline, the authors classify all 19,887 possible amino acid substitutions across 947 *pol* positions into "usual" ($\ge 0.01\%$ prevalence in Stanford HIVDB group M population sequences, $n = 4,651$), "unusual" ($< 0.01\%$ prevalence and non-DRM, $n = 15,236$), and "signature APOBEC" mutations ($n = 296$ low-prevalence `GA`/`GG`-context substitutions associated with stop codons or active-site knockouts). They show that while the median proportion of positions with unusual mutations increases slowly from $0\%$ (at the $20\%$ threshold) to $0.3\%$ (at the $1\%$ threshold), it explodes exponentially below $1\%$—reaching $1.3\%$ at $0.5\%$, $6.9\%$ at $0.2\%$, and $23.2\%$ at $0.1\%$ (where $33.9\%$ of all detected mutations are unusual). Moreover, in two of three studies with viral load metadata, unusual mutation rates at $0.5\%\text{--}5\%$ thresholds correlate inversely with plasma HIV-1 RNA levels, confirming template scarcity / PCR error propagation, while signature APOBEC mutations outnumber non-APOBEC unusual mutations in $13.2\%\text{--}17.3\%$ of samples at the $0.5\%\text{--}2\%$ thresholds.

This is a practical, well-executed bioinformatic audit of clinical HIV-1 deep sequencing data that provides a clear empirical rationale against naive $<1\%$ variant calling in UMI-free amplicon NGS and delivers a publicly accessible diagnostic web tool (`hivdb.stanford.edu/hivdb/by-reads/`). A few methodological points regarding subtype reference bias in the definition of "usual" mutations, non-independence of repeated samples, and parametric correlation on skewed proportions merit minor clarification.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
* **Empirical demarcation of the $<1\%$ PCR/sequencing error regime ([Well Supported]):** The sharp inflection point below the $1\%$ allele frequency threshold—where the median fraction of all called mutations that are unusual (`<0.01%` in group M Sanger sequences) jumps from $1.1\%$ (at $2\%$ threshold) to $4.2\%$ (at $1\%$), $12.0\%$ (at $0.5\%$), $25.1\%$ (at $0.2\%$), and $33.9\%$ (at $0.1\%$)—combined with the external baseline from single-genome sequencing (SGS, line 147) provides compelling quantitative proof that UMI-free amplicon NGS calls below $1\%$ are dominated by RT-PCR and sequencing artifacts.
* **Subtype B reference asymmetry in the definition of "usual" mutations ([Moderately Supported]):** On line 59, the authors state: *"Usual mutations were defined as differences from the subtype B consensus sequence that were not unusual."* However, $29.1\%$ of the 855 samples are non-B subtypes (including Telele 2019, which is $99\%$ subtype C, $N = 109$, and Nguyen 2018 / Ode 2015 / Moscona 2017 with substantial subtype A, C, CRF01_AE, and CRF02_AG fractions). Measuring "usual mutations" as differences from the *subtype B* consensus (rather than each sample's own subtype consensus or sample consensus) automatically inflates the baseline count of "usual mutations" at $10\%\text{--}20\%$ thresholds in non-B samples due to fixed inter-subtype polymorphisms, which in turn depresses the denominator of `unusual / (usual + unusual)` at higher thresholds for non-B studies like Telele (2019). Defining mutations relative to each sample's own majority consensus sequence when profiling sub-consensus thresholds ($0.1\%\text{--}20\%$) would separate sub-consensus intra-host variation from fixed inter-host/inter-subtype divergence.
* **Use of Pearson $r$ on skewed mutation proportions and clustered samples ([Moderately Supported]):** The authors use Pearson's linear correlation $r$ (line 61, Fig. 4, Figs. S1–S2) to test associations between $\log_{10}$ read depth or viral load and the proportion of positions with unusual mutations, despite noting on line 122 that the distribution of unusual mutations within each study is heavily right-skewed (maximum $\approx 5\times$ the median). Reporting Spearman's rank correlation $\rho$ alongside Pearson's $r$ would ensure that correlations are not driven by extreme low-VL outliers. Additionally, Ode (2015) contributed 92 longitudinal/replicate samples from 58 persons (Table 1 footnote 1), which should be accounted for when computing study-level $p$-values in Fig. 4.
* **Residual Q30 Illumina substitution error at $0.1\%\text{--}0.2\%$ thresholds ([Moderately Supported]):** In the Discussion (line 143), the authors argue that because 95% of positions had $\ge 3,000$ reads, random machine error would only need to occur 3 times to reach $0.1\%$, concluding that *"most unusual mutations did not result from machine error"*. In fact, a Phred score threshold of $Q \ge 30$ corresponds to a nominal base-call error rate of $10^{-3} = 0.1\%$ per base (and systematic sequence-specific Illumina error motifs often occur at $0.1\%\text{--}0.5\%$ independently of read depth). Thus, at $0.1\%$ and $0.2\%$ thresholds, coverage of $3,000\text{--}30,000\times$ does *not* average out systematic base-calling errors; rather, the Law of Large Numbers guarantees that any systematic error motif with true error rate $\ge 0.1\%$ will consistently exceed a $0.1\%$ read fraction regardless of coverage depth, explaining why read coverage did not correlate with unusual mutations at $0.1\%\text{--}0.2\%$.

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
* **Inverse relationship with plasma viral load ([Well Supported]):** Showing that lower plasma HIV-1 RNA copy numbers significantly increase the rate of unusual mutations at $0.5\%\text{--}5\%$ thresholds in Avila-Rios (2016) and Huber (2016) directly links template input bottlenecks during RNA extraction/cDNA synthesis to early-cycle PCR error jackpot events.
* **Clinical flagging of the 17 APOBEC-context drug resistance mutations ([Well Supported]):** Identifying the 17 canonical DRMs across PR (`D30N`, `M46I`, `G48S`, `G73S`), RT (`D67N`, `E138K`, `M184I`, `G190E/S`, `M230I`), and IN (`G118R`, `E138K`, `G140S`, `G163K/R`, `D232N`, `R263K`) that sit in APOBEC3F/3G dinucleotide contexts and demonstrating that $\ge 3$ signature APOBEC mutations co-occur at $0.5\%\text{--}2\%$ thresholds in ~1/6 of samples provides actionable clinical guidance for resistance interpretation.

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
* **Open data and web tool availability:** All 8 datasets are publicly available in NCBI SRA / Zenodo, the group M prevalence tables are documented on GitHub (`hivdb/hivfacts`), and the resulting threshold-profiling tool is deployed online (`hivdb.stanford.edu/hivdb/by-reads/`).
