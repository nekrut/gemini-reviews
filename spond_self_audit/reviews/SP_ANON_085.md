### 1. Executive Summary
This manuscript introduces a fast, alignment- and pairwise-distance-based transmission network reconstruction pipeline (`HIVClustering` / `TN93`) and applies it to all 84,527 de-duplicated, contaminant-screened HIV-1 group M *pol* sequences (and 9,426 *gag* sequences) in the Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) database across 141 countries. Defining potential transmission partners as sequences sharing $\ge 500\text{ nt}$ overlap with Tamura–Nei 93 (TN93) genetic distance $\le 1.0\%$, the authors identify 4,342 connected components comprising 13,295 individuals and 51,182 undirected edges. They show that the empirical degree distribution is best fit by a preferential-attachment Waring distribution (characteristic exponent $\rho = 1.74$), that $68\%$ of countries with clustered sequences share cross-border links (including a 333-node CRF01_AE Southeast Asian cluster across 17 countries and a 674-node subtype A1 Former Soviet Union cluster across 18 countries), and that node degree correlates inversely with the count of drug resistance-associated mutations (DRAMs; ordinal logistic regression $p < 0.0001$, and $p < 0.01$ after stripping 36 DRAM codons).

The computational method is well-engineered, scalable, and thoughtfully validated with contamination screening (identifying and removing 8 HXB2/1980s lab-control clusters totaling 230 sequences), codon-bootstrap edge stability ($>99\%$), distance-threshold sensitivity ($0.2\%\text{--}2.0\%$), and DRAM-codon jackknifing. The main methodological limitation lies in interpreting the aggregate degree distribution of a heterogeneous public sequence repository (LANL) as a preferential-attachment "scale-free" contact process ($\rho = 1.74$), when database representation is heavily confounded by outbreak-investigation sampling density and regional epidemic age (Table 1).

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
* **Confounding of Scale-Free Degree Distribution by Outbreak Ascertainment and Epidemic Dynamics (Pillars 2 & 3):** Under a fixed genetic distance threshold ($d_{\text{TN93}} \le 0.01$), an undirected genetic similarity graph is a geometric random graph over the viral genealogy rather than a direct record of who-infected-whom contact degree. A single point-source outbreak (e.g., an acute injection drug use outbreak or nosocomial cluster densely sampled within 6–12 months of transmission) forms a near-complete clique where every sequence is within $1\%$ of every other sequence, assigning degree $k \approx N_{\text{cluster}} - 1$ to all members even if the underlying transmission chain was strictly linear ($k \le 2$). Consequently, fitting Pareto, Yule, and Waring distributions to the pooled global LANL degree distribution (lines 121–122) conflates contact-network preferential attachment with clique formation in densely sampled young outbreaks (such as the Former Soviet Union IDU and East/Central Asian epidemics, which are $2.0\text{--}5.4\times$ over-represented in clusters in Table 1).
* **Node Non-Independence in the DRAM vs. Degree Regression (Pillars 3 & 4):** In the Drug Resistance section (lines 170–172), the authors use ordinal logistic regression across nodes to show an inverse association between node degree and DRAM count ($p < 0.0001$ on the full alignment, $p < 0.01$ after stripping 36 DRAM codons and adjusting for sampling year and geographic region). However, nodes within the same connected component share both viral ancestry (and thus transmitted DRAMs) and correlated node degrees (especially within dense cliques). Treating connected nodes as independent observations in an ordinal logistic regression without cluster-robust standard errors or phylogenetic/network random effects inflates Type I error. By contrast, the 100-replicate random 36-codon deletion control ($\rho = 1.75$, 95% CI $1.72\text{--}1.78$ vs. $\rho = 1.84$ when removing DRAMs) is a well-designed non-parametric permutation control.
* **Contamination Screening and Ambiguity Handling (Pillar 2):** Averaging nucleotide ambiguities during TN93 calculation (so `R:A` is scored as $0.5$ rather than $0$) and explicitly screening out heterochronous clusters containing 1980s lab strains (removing 230 sequences including 188 linked to HXB2 across 25 countries) are exemplary data-hygiene practices that prevent classic database-artifact super-clusters.

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
* **Necessity of Global Reference Context for Local Cluster Inference [Well Supported]:** The recovery and cross-border expansion of previously published local clusters (Table 2, Figures 2–3) convincingly demonstrate that country-restricted subsampling misses international transmission links.
* **Fitness/Transmission Trade-off of Drug Resistance Mutations [Moderately Supported]:** The shift in network exponent ($\rho$ increasing from $1.74$ to $1.84$ upon removing 36 DRAM codons, outside the 95% null interval $1.72\text{--}1.78$) and the negative association between DRAM count and node degree are consistent with reduced transmission fitness of drug-resistant strains, though partially confounded by treatment-status ascertainment (sequences with multiple DRAMs often derive from longitudinal salvage-therapy studies of chronically infected patients rather than acute transmission cohorts).
* **Preferential Attachment Mechanism from Global LANL Degree Distribution [Overstated]:** While the empirical genetic similarity graph has a heavy-tailed degree distribution well approximated by a Waring distribution, attributing this tail to preferential attachment in the underlying transmission network overstates what pairwise genetic thresholding on uncalibrated public database submissions can resolve.

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
* **Data & Code Availability:** Open-source C++ and Python implementations (`HIVClustering`, `TN93`, `BioExt`) are publicly archived on GitHub, and all sequence data derive from public LANL repositories with clear coordinate and filtering specifications.
