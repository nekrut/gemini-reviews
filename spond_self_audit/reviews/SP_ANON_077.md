## 1. Executive Summary
This concise correspondence ("Letter to the Editor") re-evaluates the sequence datasets and evolutionary conclusions of Redd et al., who had reported that HIV-1 transmission in heterosexual discordant couples in Rakai, Uganda preferentially involved "ancestral" donor viral strains (sampled $\ge 2$ years prior to transmission) rather than "contemporary" donor strains (sampled shortly after transmission). Through a rigorous phylogenetic and statistical audit, the authors demonstrate that Redd et al.'s conclusion rests on two fundamental methodological errors:
1. **Conflation of Phenetic Genetic Distance with Cladistic Evolutionary Relatedness:** Using the classic hominoid mitochondrial phylogeny as an pedagogical illustration, the authors explain that evolutionary relatedness is determined strictly by topological common ancestry (most recent common ancestor, MRCA), not raw branch-length distance. Re-analyzing the 9 Rakai couples who underwent 454 deep sequencing using Bayesian MCMC molecular-clock phylogenetics (BEAST), the authors show that in 7 of 9 couples, recipient viruses share a statistically supported MRCA with **contemporary** donor viruses (posterior probability $\ge 0.98$ in 4 monophyletic couples and $\ge 0.99$ in 3 intermixed couples), 2 of 9 couples have unresolved topologies due to low donor divergence, and **0 of 9 couples** show recipient viruses sharing a closer common ancestor with ancestral donor viruses.
2. **Neglect of Statistical Estimation Variance on Short Sanger Amplicons:** Re-analyzing the 22 couples with bulk/Sanger sequences from Figure 1 of Redd et al., the authors show that comparing point estimates of Tamura-Nei 93 (TN93) pairwise distances on short sequences without confidence intervals mistakes binomial counting noise for biological signal (e.g., 1 vs. 3 nucleotide differences corresponds to $0.25\%$ [95% CI: $0.0\%\text{–}1.25\%$] vs. $0.76\%$ [95% CI: $0.23\%\text{–}1.8\%$], likelihood ratio test posterior probability $= 0.51$). Across all 22 couples, only 1 couple exhibits a nominally significant ($p \le 0.05$, uncorrected) reduction in genetic distance to the ancestral donor sequence—well within the expected Type I error rate under the null ($22 \times 0.05 = 1.1$ expected false positives).

This is a textbook example of rigorous forensic peer review in molecular evolution. Every mathematical, phylogenetic, and population-genetic argument is exact, proportionate, and directly supported by re-analysis of the primary data.

## 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit

### 2.1 Cladistic Common Ancestry vs. Phenetic Distance
- Redd et al. inferred "preferential transmission of ancestral strains" by observing that recipient sequences sometimes had a slightly shorter raw genetic distance to earlier donor timepoints than to later donor timepoints.
- The authors correctly point out that once a phylogeny is inferred, lineage origins are determined by the branching order (which donor population shares an MRCA with the recipient clade; Figure 1B–D). If a recipient lineage branches from within or sister to the contemporary donor population (as confirmed with posterior probability $\ge 0.98\text{–}0.99$ in all 7 resolved 454-sequenced couples), the transmitted virus physically originated from the contemporary circulating donor pool, regardless of whether subsequent evolutionary rate slowdowns in the recipient, rate accelerations in the donor, or post-transmission reversion to consensus (convergent evolution) result in shorter tip-to-tip distances to ancestral nodes.

### 2.2 Uncertainty Quantification for Pairwise Genetic Distances on Short Sequences
- On short sub-genomic HIV-1 amplicons (~400 bp), a difference of 1 vs. 3 nucleotide substitutions represents only 2 mutational events. The authors compute 95% bootstrap confidence intervals and apply a formal phylogenetic likelihood ratio test / posterior probability comparison to test the null hypothesis of equal genetic distance ($d_{\text{recipient, ancestral}} = d_{\text{recipient, contemporary}}$).
- Demonstrating that only 1 out of 22 couples achieves nominal significance ($p \le 0.05$ before multiple-testing correction, where $E[\text{false positives}] = 22 \times 0.05 = 1.1$) completely dismantles the claim of a systematic distance anomaly in the Sanger cohort.

## 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality

### Claim Audit Summary
- **Claim 1:** *"In 4 couples (couples 8–11), viruses from the recipient were monophyletic and shared a most recent common ancestor with contemporary viruses from the donor (posterior probability, >= .98)... In 3 couples (couples 1, 12, and 14) viruses from the recipient were intermixed with contemporary donor viruses (posterior probability, >= .99)... In no couple was there phylogenetic evidence to support recipient viruses being the closest relatives of ancestral donor viruses."* — **[Well Supported]**: Directly established via Bayesian MCMC molecular-clock phylogenetic re-analysis of the 9 deep-sequenced transmission pairs.
- **Claim 2:** *"We found sufficient signal to assert that the recipient sequence is genetically more similar to the ancestral donor sequence in only 1 of 22 total couples (posterior probability, <= .05; no correction for multiple testing)."* — **[Well Supported]**: Properly accounts for estimation variance in TN93 distances across short alignments and highlights the expected false-positive rate across 22 unadjusted tests.
- **Claim 3:** *"An increase in the evolutionary rate in donor virus later in infection or a slowdown in the rate of viral evolution in the recipient... or convergent evolution (ie, reversion to an ancestral genotype/phenotype) is a possible explanation for decreased genetic distance separating recipient sequences from the ancestral donor viruses. Nevertheless, none of these scenarios point to the preferential transmission of ancestral viruses."* — **[Well Supported]**: Mechanistically sound synthesis of how branch-length heterogeneity and consensus reversion affect tip-to-root distances without altering the source population of transmission.

## 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
- All re-analyses were conducted on the publicly available sequences from Redd et al. using standard, cited methods (BEAST MCMC molecular clock, TN93 distance with bootstrap CIs, and likelihood ratio testing). Within the strict length limits of a 52-line journal correspondence, the authors provide specific couple identifiers (`couples 8–11`, `couples 1, 12, and 14`, `couples 6 and 15`, and couple `F04331 / H50053`) so that every topological and distance claim can be cross-checked against the original publication.
