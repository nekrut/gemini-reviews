## 1. Executive Summary
This study presents a comprehensive epidemiological and molecular surveillance investigation comparing contact-tracing partner naming networks against HIV-1 *pol* genetic distance networks (`HIV-TRACE`) across $N = 1,342$ HIV-infected individuals (756 newly diagnosed index cases and 586 unique genotyped HIV-positive named partners, connected by 651 naming links) collected by the New York City Department of Health and Mental Hygiene (DOHMH) between 2006 and 2012. By fitting a Bayesian MCMC Gamma–Gaussian mixture model to the empirical distribution of pairwise Tamura-Nei 93 (TN93) genetic distances between named partners, the authors demonstrate a sharp bimodal separation between plausible transmission partners (Gamma component: mean $0.008$, SD $0.006$ substitutions/site) and unrelated within-subtype B pairings (Gaussian component: mean $0.058$, SD $0.012$ substitutions/site, with $99.9\%$ of probability mass $> 0.02$ substitutions/site). Across the empirically validated threshold range of $0.01\text{–}0.02$ substitutions/site (with $0.0175$ maximizing cluster resolution), $48\%$ ($310/651$) of named partner links are corroborated by viral genetic similarity, whereas $52\%$ ($341/651$) involve viruses no more related than random subtype draws ($0.03\text{–}0.08$ substitutions/site), ruling out direct transmission. Conversely, $53\%$ ($388/736$) of genetic links at $\le 0.0175$ are supported by partner naming, and reciprocal partner naming increases the odds of genetic linkage nearly four-fold ($\text{aOR} = 3.72$, 95% CI: $2.43\text{–}5.69$, with $79\%$ [$149/189$] genetically linked).

This is a landmark study in quantitative molecular epidemiology. It provides the first large-scale empirical calibration of HIV-1 genetic distance thresholds against ground-truth public health partner tracing data, rigorously tests robustness to convergent drug-resistance codons (95%–98% concordance) and threshold sensitivity ($0.01\text{–}0.02$ substitutions/site), and accounts for partner-count confounding via multivariable logistic regression and single-partner restriction sensitivity analyses. Only minor technical and editorial points warrant attention.

## 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit

### 2.1 Empirical Calibration of Genetic Distance Thresholds via Mixture Modeling
- Rather than relying on arbitrary *ad hoc* distance cutoffs, the authors model the observed pairwise TN93 distance distribution across all 651 named partner pairs as a two-component mixture of a Gamma distribution (recent direct/indirect transmission links) and a Gaussian distribution (background within-subtype divergence), estimated via 200,000 Metropolis–Hastings MCMC iterations.
- Showing that $99.9\%$ of the background within-subtype Gaussian distribution lies above $0.02$ substitutions/site, and that the total number of distinct network clusters peaks at $0.0175$ substitutions/site before coalescing into giant accreted components above $0.02$ (Figure 2), provides a rigorous, dual-criteria justification (statistical mixture separation + network percolation resolution) for the $0.01\text{–}0.02$ substitutions/site surveillance window.

### 2.2 Robustness to Drug Resistance Convergence and Differential Partner Naming Rates
- The authors thoroughly evaluate potential confounders:
  1. **Convergent Drug Resistance Evolution:** Repeating all pairwise TN93 calculations after stripping 48 surveillance drug-resistance codon positions yields 95%–98% classification agreement across the $0.01\text{–}0.02$ threshold range (98% at $0.0175$, where only 8 borderline pairs out of 651 change status; Figure 3 and Table 2).
  2. **Differential Partner Elicitation and Genotyping Coverage Across Risk Groups:** Because MSM index cases named more partners on average than heterosexuals (mean $2.9$ vs. $1.6$, Table 1), a lower per-partner linkage rate or incomplete genotyping of anonymous partners could bias risk-group comparisons. The authors address this via three complementary analyses: (a) multivariable logistic regression adjusting for total named partners, genotyped partners, race/ethnicity, country of birth, subtype, infection stage (BED assay), AIDS status, and age (Table 3); (b) comparing the per-index-case percentage of genetically linked partners (`S3 Table`); and (c) restricting the regression strictly to index cases who named only a single partner (`S4 Table`), confirming that MSM remained significantly less likely than heterosexuals to be genetically linked to their named partner ($p < 0.001$).

### 2.3 Minor Caveat on Optimistic IUPAC Ambiguity Resolution and Analytic Cohort Selection
- **Optimistic Ambiguity Resolution (`resolve` mode):** Following HIV-TRACE defaults, IUPAC ambiguous nucleotides (e.g., `Y`, `R`) are resolved to zero distance if they match the corresponding nucleotide in the compared sequence, after excluding 13 sequences with $\ge 5\%$ ambiguities. While appropriate for capturing mixed alleles during early/chronic co-transmission, zero-distance ambiguity resolution slightly lowers pairwise distances for sequences with $2\%\text{–}4.9\%$ mixed bases compared to fractional-averaging distance calculation.
- **Conditioning on Genotyped HIV-Positive Partners:** Inclusion in the analytic dataset required that a named partner was located, tested HIV-positive, linked to care, and had a resistance genotype reported to DOHMH. Thus, the $48\%$ genetic corroboration rate applies to *genotyped HIV-positive* named partners rather than all elicited contacts (many of whom test HIV-negative or remain ungenotyped), a scope distinction the authors clearly explain in the Discussion.

### 2.4 Typographical Error in Table 3 (`IDU (M)` Percentage)
- **Table 3, Line 103 (`IDU (M)` row):** Out of $N = 35$ male IDU index cases, $15$ ($43\%$) were genetically linked to $\ge 1$ named partner, and $20$ were not genetically linked. However, the table prints `20 (23%)` instead of `20 (57%)` in the `"Not genetically linked to a named partner, n (%)"` column ($20 / 35 = 57.1\%$; the `23%` value was inadvertently duplicated from the adjacent `Hetero (F)`, `Hetero (M)`, and `Other/Unknown` rows).

## 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality

### Claim Audit Summary
- **Claim 1:** *"In 48% of cases, named partners were infected with genetically closely related viruses, compatible with but not necessarily representing or implying, direct transmission. Partner pairs linked through the genetic similarity of their HIV sequences were also linked by naming in 53% of cases."* — **[Well Supported]**: Directly established from 651 partner naming edges and 736 genetic edges at the empirically calibrated $0.0175$ substitutions/site threshold, with full sensitivity curves reported across $0.01\text{–}0.02$ in Table 2 and Figure 5.
- **Claim 2:** *"Persons who reported high-risk heterosexual contact were more likely to name at least one partner with a genetically similar virus (77%) than those reporting their risk as injection drug use (43%–53%) or men who have sex with men (42%; aOR = 0.22, p < 0.001)."* — **[Well Supported]**: Robust across univariate models, multivariable models adjusted for partner counts and demographics, race-stratified sub-analyses, and single-partner restriction analyses.
- **Claim 3:** *"Reciprocal naming increased the odds that named partners would be genetically linked compared to unidirectionally named partners: model adjusted odds ratio = 3.72 (95% CI: 2.43–5.69), with 79% (149/189) genetically linked."* — **[Well Supported]**: Compelling, policy-relevant finding demonstrated consistently across all transmission risk groups and distance thresholds.

## 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
- **Software & Statistical Transparency:** The genetic network construction software (`HIV-TRACE`, `www.hivtrace.org`), sequence alignment parameters (HXB2 coordinates 2253–3869), mixture model parameter estimates (`S1 Table`), and regression tables (`S2–S4 Tables`) are fully documented.
- **Privacy Constraints on Surveillance Data:** As mandated by New York State Public Health Law for identifiable partner-tracing and clinical HIV genotypes, raw sequences and contact network links cannot be publicly posted, which is appropriately documented in the Ethics/IRB statement.
