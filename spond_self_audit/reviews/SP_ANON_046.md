### 1. Executive Summary
This study analyzes 22 years of prospective observational data (1996–2018) from $N=1,119$ ART-naive persons with HIV (PWH) in San Diego (655 with incident infection $\le 1$ year from estimated date of infection [EDI] in the PIRC cohort; 464 with prevalent or presumed prevalent infection $>1$ year in PIRC and CNICS). Using HIV-TRACE ($<1.5\%$ *pol* genetic distance), the authors trace 752 network "seeds" over a median follow-up of 8.8 years, observing 218 incident linkage events ("incident cluster growth"). Fitting an inverse-sampling-density-weighted Andersen-Gill recurrent-event Cox proportional hazards model, the authors report that the time-varying proportion of cluster members with incident infection is the dominant predictor of subsequent incident cluster growth ($\text{HR} = 43.09$, 95% CI $16.90\text{--}109.86$ in Table 2; reported as $44.09$ in the Abstract). They further show that CDC Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) priority cluster criteria ($\ge 5$ diagnoses in 12 months at $0.5\%$ distance) capture only 11/218 (5.0%) incident linkages (or 24/218 [11.0%] at $1.5\%$ distance), and use the Cox model coefficients to project that reducing the proportion of incident infections by 50–75% would reduce 3-year cluster growth by 56–63%.

The empirical evaluation of the CDC EHE cluster-trigger criteria is valuable and demonstrates that in an established, predominantly dyadic epidemic (where 58.9% of clusters with incident linkages never exceed size 2 and the median inter-EDI interval of linked incident pairs is 2.6 months), high-threshold cluster triggers miss the vast majority of recent transmissions. However, the statistical modeling of cluster growth and the counterfactual prevention projections suffer from three methodological problems: (1) endogenous time-window and evolutionary-distance confounding in the time-varying `proportion of incident` covariate, (2) invalid causal counterfactual extrapolation from scaling a cluster-composition regression variable in an exponential hazard model, and (3) internal numerical inconsistencies between the Abstract, main text, and tables.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
* **Endogenous Time-Window and Genetic-Distance Confounding of the Primary Predictor (`Proportion of incident`):**
  1. *Temporal window overlap:* A seed entering the network as an incident case starts with `proportion of incident` $= 1.0$, which automatically decays to $0.0$ exactly 1 year after their EDI unless another incident node links to them (Fig. 1 caption, line 151). Because the median time between the EDIs of linked incident pairs is only 2.6 months (IQR 1.4–4.9 months; line 170), the vast majority of incident linkages to an incident seed occur during that initial 12-month window—while `proportion of incident` is still $1.0$ simply because the seed recently entered the cohort. Conversely, once a cluster is $>1$ year old, its `proportion of incident` is non-zero *only if it recently acquired a new incident node in the past 12 months*, turning the predictor into a lagged indicator of the outcome itself (recent incident growth). Without explicitly controlling for time since the most recent node addition (or time since seed EDI) in the baseline hazard, the $\text{HR} = 43.09$ conflates recent cluster activity with the biological infectiousness of acute/early HIV.
  2. *Evolutionary divergence bias:* Because HIV-1 *pol* diverges within-host over years of chronic infection, two individuals sampled within $\le 1$ year of their respective transmission events have near-zero within-host divergence from the transmitted strain and are far more likely to fall below a fixed $<1.5\%$ genetic distance threshold than individuals sampled after years of chronic infection (such as the 184 CNICS clinic patients who lacked EDIs and were all assigned "presumed prevalent" status).
* **Invalid Counterfactual Intervention Projections (lines 157 & 227, Fig. 2B–D):** To predict the impact of an intervention that reduces onward transmission from incident or unsuppressed cases by 50% or 75%, the authors simply multiplied the observed cluster covariate (`proportion of incident` or `proportion of unsuppressed`) by $0.5$ or $0.25$ inside the fitted Cox model $\exp(\beta X)$. Scaling a compositional proportion $X \in [0, 1]$ inside a nonlinear exponential hazard function ($\text{HR}(0.5 X) = [\text{HR}(X)]^{0.5}$) does not correspond mechanistically to blocking 50% or 75% of transmissions from incident individuals, nor can a descriptive regression coefficient on cluster composition be interpreted as a causal treatment effect. Moreover, because `proportion of unsuppressed` was collinear with `proportion of incident` (newly diagnosed incident cases are untreated and have 0.6 $\log_{10}$ higher viral load; line 165), conditioning on `proportion of incident` in the multivariable model absorbed the viral load effect ($\text{HR}$ dropping from $1.69$ to $1.35$, $P = .069$), leading to the biologically implausible projection in Fig. 2C that reducing unsuppressed viremia by 75% across the network would reduce 3-year cluster growth by only 11%.
* **Internal Numerical Discrepancies:**
  1. **Abstract vs. Table 2 / Text Hazard Ratio:** The Abstract (line 57) reports the multivariable hazard ratio for `proportion of incident` as **44.09 (95% CI, 17.09–113.78)**, whereas the Results text (line 180) and Table 2 (line 186) report **43.09 (95% CI, 16.90–109.86)**.
  2. **Reversed Intervention Percentages in Results Text (line 227):** Line 227 states that reducing onward transmission from unsuppressed persons *"by 50% or 75% would predict reductions by only 11% and 7% at year 3 (Figure 2C)"*—inverting the order so that a 50% reduction appears more effective than a 75% reduction (the Fig. 2C caption correctly states 7% and 11%, respectively).
  3. **Inconsistent Covariate Timeframes:** Methods (line 155) and Table 3 footnote c (line 220) define the partner covariate as the median number of sex partners in the **3 months** preceding enrollment, whereas Table 1 (line 103), Results (line 180), and Table 2 footnote c (line 199) state **1 month** prior to enrollment.

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
* **Ascertainment Skew Between Sub-Cohorts:** The study merges an active acute/early infection research cohort (PIRC, where 88.4% of newly diagnosed participants had incident infection $\le 1$ year) with a routine clinical care cohort (CNICS, where 100% of 184 participants had no EDI and were categorized as "presumed prevalent"). Furthermore, baseline behavioral and STI variables had high missingness (illicit drug use missing in 100% of CNICS and 55% of PIRC; STI screening missing in 27%).
* **Pragmatic Public Health Insight:** Despite the survival-modeling overreach, the authors' empirical observation in the Discussion (line 238)—that 58.9% of clusters with incident transmissions never grew beyond size 2 and that the median 2.6-month inter-infection window is too narrow for retrospective sequence-based cluster investigation to interrupt transmission—is a candid, well-grounded critique of molecular "detect and respond" paradigms.

---

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
* **Data & Code Availability:** No GitHub/Zenodo repository or GenBank accession list is provided in the manuscript text, and the statistical methodology relies heavily on unshown Supplementary Materials for the inverse sampling density estimator, multiple imputation model, and Andersen-Gill variance specification. Reconciling the Abstract vs. Table 2 HR discrepancy and providing the R analysis scripts are necessary for reproducibility.
