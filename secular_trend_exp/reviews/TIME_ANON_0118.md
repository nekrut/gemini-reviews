### 1. Executive Summary
This evolutionary and statistical genetics study analyzes 1,143 common disease- and trait-associated SNPs from the NHGRI Catalog of Published Genome-Wide Association Studies ($p < 10^{-5}$, across 486 papers) and compares their risk-allele frequencies, ancestral vs. derived allele states (polarized via chimpanzee outgroup/dbSNP), estimated allele ages, and odds ratios against 1,000 unlinked CEU HapMap Phase II/III SNPs ($\ge 200\text{ kb}$ apart) and analytical neutral-theory expectations weighted by GWAS statistical power (`QUANTO`, approximated as $2x(1-x)$). The author reports that disease-associated alleles have a much lower mean frequency than randomly sampled HapMap alleles ($0.394$ vs. $0.610$ after power weighting; $0.721$ unweighted) and are less frequently ancestral ($43.7\%$ vs. $56.8\%$ weighted HapMap and $65.0\%$ weighted neutral theory), concluding that GWAS risk alleles are significantly enriched for young, low-frequency derived alleles.

While the empirical curation of the 1,143 NHGRI GWAS loci (including stratification across 7 phenotypic classes, 142 replicated loci, genotyping array platforms, and publication epochs) is careful and well-documented, the central comparison against the HapMap and neutral "null expectations" in Table 1 and Figure 2 contains a fundamental sampling-weighting asymmetry that artificially inflates the apparent difference between GWAS risk alleles and the null genome.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
- **Size-Biased Allele Sampling Asymmetry in the Null Model (The Core Statistical Flaw):**
  - Look closely at how the author defines a "randomly chosen allele" at each diallelic HapMap or neutral locus in the Methods (line 30): *"For each SNP, alleles were chosen at random after weighting by allele frequency. Thus, a SNP with allele frequencies of 0.80 and 0.20 would have an 80% chance of yielding the major allele. Because high frequency alleles are more likely to be ancestral, randomly chosen alleles from the HapMap dataset are more likely to be ancestral than derived."*
  - Under this frequency-weighted sampling rule, for any SNP with major allele frequency $p > 0.5$ and minor allele frequency $q = 1-p$, the expected frequency of the chosen allele is $E[X] = p^2 + (1-p)^2 = 1 - 2p(1-p)$, which is strictly $\ge 0.50$ for every polymorphic site in the genome! Consequently, the author's unweighted HapMap null mean allele frequency is $0.721$ and the power-weighted HapMap null mean is $0.610$ (Table 1), with Figure 2A–D skewed toward frequencies $>0.50$.
  - However, at a GWAS hit in the NHGRI Catalog, the reported allele is not a random gene copy sampled from a chromosome in the population (which would pick the major allele with probability $p$); it is whichever of the two segregating alleles at the locus is associated with *increased* trait value or disease risk ($\text{OR} > 1.0$). Under the true null hypothesis where risk orientation is independent of allele frequency or ancestral state (e.g., for symmetric quantitative traits like height/morphological traits or neutral markers), each of the two alleles at a polymorphic locus has a $50\%$ probability (not $p$ vs. $1-p$) of being the risk-increasing allele, yielding a null mean risk-allele frequency of exactly $0.5 \cdot p + 0.5 \cdot (1-p) = 0.500$ and a null proportion ancestral of exactly $50.0\%$!
  - Relative to the symmetric $0.500$ null, the observed GWAS risk-allele mean frequency of $0.394$ and ancestral proportion of $43.7\%$ still show a genuine, biologically meaningful enrichment of minor ($<0.50$) and derived ($56.3\%$) alleles among risk-increasing alleles—roughly half the magnitude claimed against the $0.610 / 65.0\%$ size-biased null.
- **Internal Proof of the Locus- vs. Allele-Polarization Discrepancy (Line 135 & Figure 4A):**
  - Remarkably, in the Results (line 135), when the author examines the locus-level derived allele frequency (DAF) distribution directly (which does not suffer from the $p$-weighted allele-picking bias), the author notes: *"In addition, disease-associated SNPs and weighted HapMap SNPs had similar mean derived frequencies (0.426 vs. 0.432). This suggests that much of the difference between disease-associated alleles and the rest of the genome was due to properties of alleles rather than loci."*
  - Yet in the very next section (lines 165–166, Figure 4B), the author plugs the allele-frequency histograms into the neutral Kimura-Ohta allele-age formula (Equations 11–12), reporting mean ages of $2.78\,N_e$ generations for HapMap loci vs. $2.74\,N_e$ generations for GWAS loci (a negligible $1.4\%$ difference in mean age, driven by the $0.432$ vs. $0.426$ mean DAF), while claiming in the Abstract that *"SNPs implicated in genome-wide association studies were enriched for young SNPs compared to randomly selected HapMap loci."*
- **Missing Equations in Formatted Text:** In the manuscript text (lines 38, 45, 49, 56, 63, 67, 86, 88, 221), the display equations (Equations 1–12 and A1) were stripped during HTML/XML-to-text conversion, leaving blank lines after phrases like `"After integration and extensive algebra:"` and `"is:"`. Nevertheless, the verbal definitions in the surrounding sentences (e.g., Equation 6 coinciding with expected heterozygosity $2x(1-x)$, Equation 11 giving Kimura-Ohta neutral age $\tau = \frac{-2x}{1-x}\ln(x)$) allow the mathematical derivations to be reconstructed and verified.

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
- **Claim 1:** "Disease-associated alleles were more likely to be low frequency derived alleles relative to null expectations. 43.7% of disease-associated alleles were ancestral alleles. The mean frequency of disease-associated alleles was less than randomly chosen CEU HapMap alleles (0.394 vs. 0.610, after accounting for probability of detection)." `[Moderately Supported]` — The empirical observation that $56.3\%$ of GWAS risk-increasing alleles are derived ($p < 0.0001$ vs. $50\%$) and have a mean risk-allele frequency of $0.394$ ($<0.50$) is real and consistent with purifying selection keeping deleterious derived risk alleles below frequency $0.50$. However, comparing $0.394$ against a frequency-weighted null of $0.610$ (where alleles were sampled with probability proportional to their frequency $p$) exaggerates the discrepancy by conflating gene-copy sampling with allele-state labeling.
- **Claim 2:** "SNPs implicated in genome-wide association studies were enriched for young SNPs compared to randomly selected HapMap loci." `[Overstated]` — As shown in the author's own Results, the mean derived allele frequency of GWAS SNPs ($0.426$) and power-weighted HapMap SNPs ($0.432$) is nearly identical, yielding virtually indistinguishable mean neutral age estimates ($2.74\,N_e$ vs. $2.78\,N_e$ generations).
- **Claim 3:** "26.8% of disease-associated SNPs with a minor allele frequency $\le 0.2$ had an odds ratio $> 2$, while only 8.0% of SNPs with a minor allele frequency $> 0.2$ had an odds ratio $> 2$ ($p < 0.0001$, Fisher's exact test)." `[Well Supported]` — Directly supported by the 530 SNPs with OR data (Figure 5), reflecting the joint action of statistical power thresholds ($2x(1-x)$) and the Winner's Curse at low MAF, as well as purifying selection against common large-effect alleles.
