# Blinded Review: TIME_ANON_60
**Composite Score:** 4.3 / 10.0 (Rigor: 1.2/3.5 | Claims: 0.9/2.5 | Biology: 1.0/2.0 | Reproducibility: 1.2/2.0)
**Bias Category:** Phylogenetic Taxon-Sampling/Rooting Artifact & Unsubstantiated Horizontal Gene Transfer Claim
**Verdict:** Reject
**Study Modality:** Hybrid Computational + Wet-Lab Functional Validation
**Technology Maturity:** Mid-Stage High-Throughput
**Epistemic Stage:** Basic/Mechanistic Discovery

### 1. Executive Summary
This study proposes that a natterin-4-like gene (`XP_015754265`) in the genome of the reef-building coral *Acropora digitifera* was acquired via horizontal gene transfer (HGT) from fungi, and pairs phylogenetic and BLAST-network analyses of natterin-like sequences with fungal ITS amplicon sequencing of *A. digitifera* tissue from the Great Barrier Reef. While the authors correctly verify that the *A. digitifera* locus resides on a 1.04 Mb host genomic scaffold flanked by stony coral genes—ruling out a simple sequencing-contaminant assembly artifact—the core claim of fungal-to-coral HGT rests on flawed phylogenetic reasoning, biased taxon sampling, and an uninformative microbiome assay. Specifically, the authors infer HGT because coral sequences cluster closer to fungi and teleost fish than to highly divergent insect/flatworm DM9-domain proteins in unrooted trees, discard a structure-based alignment when it groups corals with invertebrates, offer contradictory evolutionary scenarios in the Discussion, and claim support from a coral-associated fungus (*Phoma*) in which no natterin-like gene has ever been identified.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
* **Taxon-Sampling Bias and Misinterpretation of Unrooted Topologies:** To reconstruct the phylogeny of natterin-4 homologs (Fig. 1) and the CLANS network (Fig. 2), the authors selected a small, discontinuous set of BLASTp hits (`e-value < 1e-5`) comprising bacteria, fungi, two corals (*A. digitifera* and *Orbicella faveolata*), teleost fish, and five highly divergent non-coral invertebrate sequences (butterflies and flatworms). As the authors themselves note (line 40), the insect and flatworm sequences lack homology across the C-terminal pore-forming/aerolysin-like domain shared by coral and fish natterins, whereas later in the Discussion (line 52) they acknowledge that other metazoans such as the mollusk *Crassostrea gigas* (misspelled `Crossostrea gigas`) encode natterin-like proteins (`CGL1` / natterin-3) that were omitted from the phylogenetic tree. In an unrooted five-clade tree where the only non-cnidarian invertebrate sequences included are highly divergent paralogs lacking the C-terminal domain, long-branch attraction and domain architecture mismatch inevitably pull the insect/flatworm branch away from corals and fish. This does not constitute evidence of HGT from fungi into corals.
* **Absence of Formal HGT vs. Vertical Inheritance / Gene-Loss Testing:** Establishing eukaryotic HGT requires testing whether the observed topology is significantly better than a vertically constrained metazoan topology (e.g., via Approximately Unbiased [AU] or Shimodaira-Hasegawa [SH] topology tests), evaluating gene-species tree reconciliation (duplication + differential loss across Metazoa vs. lateral transfer), and inspecting gene structure (e.g., conservation of spliceosomal introns between coral and fish natterin genes vs. fungal genes). None of these analyses were performed.
* **Post-Hoc Rejection of Discrepant Alignment Methods:** In Results (line 45), the authors report that the T-Coffee Expresso structure-based alignment grouped coral natterin-like sequences with invertebrates rather than microorganisms (Figures S5–S6), contradicting their HGT hypothesis. They then discard the Expresso alignment on the grounds that the two PDB templates they themselves selected (`1W3GA` and `5MH1A`, misspelled `5H1A` on line 45) had $<30\%$ coverage and BLASTp `e-value = 1.3`. Supplying inappropriate structural templates to Expresso and then dismissing the resulting metazoan grouping without testing domain-specific alignments (e.g., aligning the conserved C-terminal aerolysin-like domain separately from the N-terminal lectin/DM9 repeats) is methodologically unsound.
* **Self-Contradictory HGT Scenarios in the Discussion:** On line 52, the authors write: "Phylogenetic analysis in Figure 1 indicated that the *A. digitifera* naterin-4 homologue formed a cluster with microorganisms rather than animals, which implied that a fungal to coral HGT event potentially occurred prior to cnidaria – bilateria split. The *A. digitifera* natterin-4 homologue grouped closer to fish sequences rather than to the cluster composed of arthropod sequences, which again is contrary to passage down standard evolutionary lines following vertical transmission, but consistent with both coral and ancestral fish acquiring the gene possibly via a second HGT event." If a transfer occurred *prior* to the Cnidaria–Bilateria split (i.e., in the common ancestor of Cnidaria and Bilateria), then its presence in both corals (Cnidaria) and fish (Bilateria) is simple vertical inheritance followed by divergence or loss in arthropods—not a fungal-to-coral HGT or two independent HGT events.

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
* **Uninformative Fungal ITS Microbiome Profiling:** To support a fungal donor for the *A. digitifera* gene, the authors amplified fungal ITS barcodes from coral tissue and identified an abundant sequence (`fungal_sv_5`) matching the genus *Phoma*. However, they immediately concede (line 50): "A genome sequence for Phoma is not presently available to mine for natterin-like homologues and, no natterin-like gene sequences from Phoma species have been deposited in any publically available database." Finding a common marine/endophytic ascomycete in coral mucus whose genome is unsequenced and unknown to harbor the gene provides no evidence for an evolutionary HGT event.
* **Unsubstantiated Functional Claims Regarding Toxicity Repurposing:** Neither the *A. digitifera* protein nor the non-venomous teleost fish homologs were functionally assayed for pore-forming, kininogen-cleaving, or lectin activity. The claim that natterin-4 was acquired as a fungal toxin, horizontally transferred to fish, and subsequently "repositioned to non-toxic functions in non-venomous teleost fish" (Abstract, line 7; Discussion, line 52) is pure speculation; the reverse polarity (an ancestral metazoan lectin/pore-forming immune effector co-opted into venom in *Thalassophryne nattereri*) is far more parsimonious.

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
* **Missing Raw Sequencing Data Accession for ITS Amplicon Dataset:** Although the phylogenetic alignments and MrBayes batch files are included in Supplementary Figures S1–S4 and the *A. digitifera* scaffold accession (`NW_015441109.1`) is provided, the Illumina MiSeq fungal ITS amplicon sequencing dataset generated in this study has no NCBI SRA, ENA, or GenBank accession numbers reported.
* **Inconsistent Software Versioning and Typos:** Methods (line 24) states ML trees were built in MEGA X, whereas line 33 states fungal ITS ML/NJ trees were built in MEGA 7; PDB ID `5MH1A` (line 24) is written as `5H1A` (line 45); *Crassostrea gigas* is misspelled `Crossostrea gigas` (line 52); and `natterin-4` is misspelled `naterin-4` (line 52).
