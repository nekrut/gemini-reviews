### 1. Executive Summary
This brief opinion/hypothesis piece ("short excursus") proposes combining spironolactone (SP) and licorice root extract (glycyrrhizin, GI) as a potential treatment for COVID-19. The authors synthesize disparate literature arguing that SP increases soluble circulating ACE2 (hypothesized to act as a decoy receptor), antagonizes androgen receptors (potentially down-regulating TMPRSS2) and mineralocorticoid receptors (reducing inflammation), while glycyrrhizin exhibits in vitro antiviral activity against SARS-CoV in Vero cells, stabilizes erythrocyte band 3 membrane proteins against oxidative/proteolytic damage, and offsets the hyperkalemic and hypotensive side effects of spironolactone (as previously tested by the authors in polycystic ovary syndrome). The manuscript contains no primary experimental, computational, or clinical data, relies on tenuous mechanistic extrapolations across unrelated cell systems, and contains invalid 9-digit ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
- **No Primary Data or Quantitative Analysis:** The article is a purely narrative hypothesis piece with zero experimental measurements, pharmacokinetic calculations, or quantitative models.
- **Unexamined Pharmacokinetic Disconnects:** The in vitro anti-SARS-CoV activity of glycyrrhizin in Vero cells cited by the authors (Cinatl et al.) required millimolar or high-micromolar concentrations ($\text{EC}_{50} \approx 300\text{--}600\text{ mg/L}$), which are orders of magnitude above the plasma concentrations achievable via safe oral dosing of licorice extract (e.g., $3.5\text{ g/day}$ oral licorice root extract used in PCOS studies). No dose-concentration feasibility or bioavailability analysis is performed.
- **Erroneous ClinicalTrials.gov Identifiers:** In both the Abstract (line 7) and main text (line 23), two of the three cited trial registration numbers contain 9 digits instead of the standard 8-digit NCT format (`NCT044241349` and `NCT043465887`), indicating transcription errors that were not verified prior to publication.

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
- **Claim 1:** *"We propose that the possible association of GI and SP may be considered for Covid-19 infections, taking account of the different mechanism of action of the two substances and their synergism."* — `[Fragile]`. While the opposing electrolyte effects of spironolactone (potassium-sparing mineralocorticoid antagonist) and glycyrrhizin (11$\beta$-HSD2 inhibitor causing pseudohyperaldosteronism and hypokalemia) have a known physiological basis, combining two agents that compete along the mineralocorticoid pathway simultaneously risks cancelling out the desired anti-mineralocorticoid/anti-inflammatory actions of spironolactone while introducing unpredictable blood pressure and potassium fluctuations in acutely ill COVID-19 patients.
- **Claim 2:** *"A direct effect of GI on the cell membrane has been reported in human erythrocytes... GI could be evaluated for... protection of the plasma membrane from virus penetration."* — `[Overstated]`. Extrapolating stabilization of erythrocyte band 3 protein against in vitro oxidative stress to inhibition of spike/ACE2/TMPRSS2-mediated coronavirus fusion in respiratory epithelium lacks direct mechanistic grounding.
- **Claim 3:** *"The additive activity of SP and licorice combination should be presented as a potential mechanism, not currently supported by evidence derived from clinical trials."* — `[Well Supported]`. In the concluding paragraph, the authors appropriately caveat that their proposal is an untested hypothesis requiring quantitative dose-response assessment.
