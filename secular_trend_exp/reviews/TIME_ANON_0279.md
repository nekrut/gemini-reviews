### 1. Executive Summary
This study investigates the association of coding single-nucleotide polymorphisms in *CYP1B1* (`R48G`, `A119S`, `L432V`, `D449D`, `N453S`) with primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) in an Eastern Indian clinic cohort ($N = 264$ POAG patients without known *MYOC* or *CYP1B1* causal mutations vs. $N = 95$ ethnically matched controls from West Bengal), surveys baseline allele and haplotype frequencies across $N = 542$ healthy individuals from 24 diverse Indian ethnic subgroups (spanning Indo-European, Austro-Asiatic, Tibeto-Burman, and Dravidian linguistic families), and tests the functional impact of the `p.Leu432Val` (`c.1666C>G`) variant on $17\beta$-estradiol-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation in transfected human retinal pigment epithelial (`RPE8319`) cells. The authors report a striking enrichment of the `Val432` (`c.1666G`) allele in POAG patients (49.4% vs. 14.2% in controls; allelic $\text{OR} = 6.03$; `GG` vs. `CC+CG` $\text{OR} = 15.51$) and show that `Val432`-transfected RPE cells generate higher `CM-H2DCFDA` fluorescence after 400 nM $17\beta$-estradiol treatment. However, a forensic comparison between the clinic control group ($N = 95$) and the authors' own population genetics survey ($N = 542$) reveals a severe control-group allele frequency anomaly and Hardy-Weinberg departure that substantially inflates the reported odds ratio.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
- **Forensic Audit of the Clinic Control Group ($N = 95$) vs. Population Survey ($N = 542$):**
  - In Table 2 (`c.1666C>G`, `p.Leu432Val`), the 264 POAG patients have a `G` (`Val432`) allele frequency of $261 / 528 = 49.4\%$ (and `C` = $50.6\%$), while the $N = 95$ West Bengal controls have a `G` (`Val432`) allele frequency of only **$27 / 190 = 14.2\%$** (and `C` = $85.8\%$).
  - From the genotype odds ratio reported in the text (line 75: $\text{OR} = 15.505, 95\%\text{ CI}: 5.529\text{--}43.474$ for `GG` vs. `CG+CC`), we can reconstruct the exact $2 \times 2$ genotype table: among the 95 controls (with 27 `G` alleles), there must be **4 `GG` homozygotes**, **19 `CG` heterozygotes**, and **72 `CC` homozygotes** ($2 \times 4 + 19 = 27$ `G` alleles), and among the 264 cases (with 261 `G` alleles), **109 `GG` homozygotes**, **43 `CG` heterozygotes**, and **112 `CC` homozygotes** ($2 \times 109 + 43 = 261$ `G` alleles; $\text{OR} = \frac{109 \times 91}{155 \times 4} = 15.99 \approx 15.51$).
  - Notice two critical statistical issues that the authors never mention or test:
    1. **Severe Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) Violation in Cases (and Borderline in Controls):** In the 264 POAG cases ($p_G = 0.494, q_C = 0.506$), HWE predicts $264 \times 2(0.494)(0.506) = 132.0$ `CG` heterozygotes, yet only $\sim 43$ heterozygotes are present ($\chi^2 \approx 120, P < 10^{-25}$)! Even in the 95 controls, 4 `GG`, 19 `CG`, 72 `CC` shows an excess of `GG` homozygotes over HWE ($95 \times 0.142^2 = 1.92$ expected; $\chi^2 = 3.09$). An extreme heterozygote deficit in RFLP/sequencing data (`BsrI` digest for `c.1666C>G` in Table S1) is a classic signature of partial restriction digestion or allelic dropout.
    2. **Control Group Discrepancy with Eastern Indian Reference Populations:** In Figure 3, the authors' own survey of 24 normal Indian ethnic populations (including four Indo-European Eastern Indian groups from the exact same geographic region, `IE-E-IP1`, `IE-E-LP2`, `IE-E-LP4`) shows that the `C` (`Leu432`) allele frequency is generally $65\%$–$80\%$ (meaning `G` (`Val432`) is $20\%$–$35\%$), and the `C-G-G-T-A` (`Val432`) haplotype is a major haplotype across India (Figure 2). The unusually low `G` frequency (14.2%) in the small $N = 95$ clinic control sample drives much of the apparent $\text{OR} = 6.03$ signal.
- **In Vitro RPE8319 ROS Assay Audit:**
  - The authors transiently transfect `RPE8319` cells with `Leu432` or `Val432` *CYP1B1* cDNA plasmids, treat with 400 nM $17\beta$-estradiol, and measure `CM-H2DCFDA` fluorescence ($n = 3$ experiments in duplicate, paired two-tailed $t$-test $p = 0.0245$ and $p = 0.0197$).
  - However, **no Western blot or qRT-PCR control for transfection efficiency or CYP1B1 protein expression level** comparing the `Leu432` and `Val432` constructs is presented. Without verifying equal protein expression of the two plasmid preps in RPE8319 cells, differences in ROS generation cannot be unambiguously attributed to the single amino acid substitution versus plasmid quality or expression level.

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
- **Claim 1:** Coding SNPs in *CYP1B1* display structured allele and haplotype frequency variation across 24 Indian ethnic subgroups representing four major linguistic families, with `C-G-C-C-A` and `C-G-G-T-A` as the predominant haplotypes. `[Well Supported]` — Grounded in genotyping 542 individuals across 24 well-defined anthropological cohorts (Table 1, Figures 1–3).
- **Claim 2:** The `c.1666G` (`p.Val432`) allele and `GG` genotype confer a 6- to 15-fold increased risk of POAG (`OR = 6.03` and `OR = 15.51`). `[Fragile]` — Compromised by the unexamined heterozygote deficit / HWE departure in the `BsrI` RFLP genotyping data, small control sample size ($N = 95$), and discrepancy between the clinic control allele frequency (14.2%) and background Eastern Indian populations.
- **Claim 3:** The `Val432` variant of CYP1B1 generates significantly higher $17\beta$-estradiol-induced ROS in human RPE cells than `Leu432`, linking 4-hydroxyestradiol oxidative stress to retinal ganglion cell death in POAG. `[Moderately Supported]` — The biochemical rationale (higher 4-hydroxylation activity of `Val432` CYP1B1 toward $17\beta$-estradiol) aligns with prior enzymology literature, and the NAC rescue control works as expected, but the lack of a Western blot normalizing CYP1B1 protein expression between the two transiently transfected constructs weakens the experimental proof.

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
- **Strengths:** Complete primer sequences, annealing temperatures, and RFLP enzymes (`AvaII`, `NaeI`, `BsrI`, `BseGI`, `MwoI`) are provided in Supplemental Table S1, and detailed anthropological metadata for the 24 Indian cohorts are provided in Table 1.
- **Limitations:** Exact genotype counts (`CC`, `CG`, `GG`) and HWE $P$-values are omitted from Table 2 (which reports only allele counts and percentages).
