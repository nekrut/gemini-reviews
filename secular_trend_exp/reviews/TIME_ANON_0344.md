### 1. Executive Summary
This clinical and functional molecular genetics study investigates why routine exon sequencing of the tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase gene (*ALPL*) detects two mutated alleles in 94.9% (131/138) of patients with severe (perinatal and infantile) hypophosphatasia (HPP), but detects only a single heterozygous mutation in 47.6% (49/103) of patients with mild HPP (childhood, adult, odontohypophosphatasia, and perinatal benign forms). To distinguish true dominant-negative heterozygosity from missed compound heterozygosity (e.g., an undetected intronic or regulatory mutation in *trans*), the authors perform site-directed mutagenesis and transient transfection/co-transfection (50% WT : 50% mutant cDNA, $n \ge 4$ independent replicates) in COS-7 cells for 35 mutations carried by 47 mild HPP patients with a single detected mutation. Using a conservative threshold of $\le 40\%$ WT alkaline phosphatase (AP) activity in 50:50 co-transfections, they find that 24 of the 35 mutations (representing 36/47 patients, 76.6%) exert a dominant-negative effect and cluster within the active site, crown domain, and homodimer interface on a 3D homology model of TNSALP. In addition, genotyping 8 intragenic *ALPL* SNPs across single-mutation mild HPP patients ($n=47\text{--}48$) vs. two-mutation HPP controls ($n=74$) reveals a 3-fold enrichment of a specific 8-SNP haplotype (`Haplotype E`: `C-C-T-A-T-G-C-G`, $20\%$ vs. $7\%$, $\chi^2 p = 1.4 \times 10^{-3}$).

The functional co-transfection screening of 35 clinical *ALPL* missense variants in COS-7 cells and their structural mapping onto the TNSALP homodimer provide valuable diagnostic insight into dominant vs. recessive mild HPP. However, the manuscript suffers from multiple internal numerical inconsistencies across Tables 1–4, missing rows for 13 patients in Table 3, and contradictory phasing data for Haplotype E.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
- **Truncation of Table 3 and Missing Data for 13 Patients (Patients 35–47):** The text (line 126) and Table 3 caption state that Table 3 summarizes site-directed mutagenesis results for "35 mutations found in 47 patients with mild HPP and carrying a single heterozygous mutation," and the Discussion (line 163) explicitly refers to "patients 37, 39 and 41" and to mutations `p.A176T`, `p.E191K`, `p.G249V`, `p.I490F`, and `p.R136H` among the 13 patients without dominant-negative inhibition. However, Table 3 in the manuscript ends at **Patient 34** (`p.I72T`, 40.2% WT), listing only 23 unique mutations across 34 patients! All rows for Patients 35 through 47 (the remaining 12 tested mutations with $>40\%$ WT co-transfection activity) were inadvertently cut off from the bottom of Table 3.
- **Internal Sample Size and Chromosome Count Discrepancies Across Tables 1, 2, 3, and 4:**
  1. In Table 1 and Results line 62, the number of mild HPP patients carrying two mutated alleles is $26 \text{ (Childhood)} + 18 \text{ (Adult)} + 10 \text{ (Odonto)} = 54$ (and $8 + 12 + 29 = 49$ with one mutation; total $= 103$). Yet the title of Table 2 states "found in the **53** patients affected with mild HPP and carrying two mutated alleles."
  2. In Table 1, $8 + 12 + 29 = 49$ mild HPP patients carry one mutation. In Results line 62 and Table 3, this is `47` patients (2 not tested by mutagenesis). Then in Table 4, the header states `Patients with 1 mutation (n = 48)`, which corresponds to $2 \times 48 = 96$ chromosomes—yet the haplotype counts in that column sum to $54 + 12 + 7 + 1 + 1 + 19 = \mathbf{94}$ chromosomes (i.e., $n = 47$ patients, not 48!). Furthermore, `73 (0.79)` + `19 (0.21)` sums to $92$ (not 94), because the two singleton haplotypes (`D` and `J`, 1 chromosome each) were omitted from the grouped sub-total.
  3. In Table 4, `Haplotype D` (`T-C-T-A-T-C-T-A`) and `Haplotype F` (`T-C-T-A-T-C-T-A`) are printed with the exact same 8-SNP allele sequence.
- **Missing Variance Estimates in Functional Assays:** Although each of the 35 site-directed mutants was assayed in "at least four" independent COS-7 transfections (line 50), Tables 2 and 3 report only point estimates of `% WT` residual and 50:50 co-transfection activity without SD or SEM values.
- **Contradictory *Cis*/*Trans* Distribution of Haplotype E:** As the authors candidly acknowledge in the Results (line 126), if Haplotype E harbored a recurrent undetected recessive *ALPL* mutation explaining the missing second allele, Haplotype E should be enriched specifically in the 11 patients carrying *recessive* (non-dominant-negative) mutations and should occur in *trans* to the known coding mutation. Instead, Haplotype E occurs at the same frequency in patients with dominant-negative mutations and is found just as often in *cis* as in *trans* (Table 3), which is also consistent with population stratification within the multi-country European-descent cohort or a mild *cis*/*trans* modifier effect.

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
- **[Well Supported]** A majority (~77%) of mild HPP patients in whom only a single heterozygous *ALPL* coding mutation is found carry severe missense alleles (residual activity $0\text{--}5\%$ WT) that exert a dominant-negative inhibitory effect on wild-type TNSALP monomers in 50:50 co-transfection assays ($\le 40\%$ WT activity) and localize to the active site, crown domain, or homodimer interface.
- **[Well Supported]** Mild HPP patients carrying two detected *ALPL* mutations are predominantly compound heterozygotes combining a severe loss-of-function allele with a recurrent moderate allele retaining substantial residual activity (most commonly `c.571G>A` / `p.E191K`, present in 55% of such patients).
- **[Moderately Supported]** Intragenic *ALPL* Haplotype E (`C-C-T-A-T-G-C-G`) is enriched in single-mutation mild HPP cases ($p = 1.4 \times 10^{-3}$), potentially acting as a hypomorphic modifier allele or tagging a regulatory variant, though population substructure cannot be fully excluded without genome-wide ancestry markers.

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
- **Strengths:** dbSNP `rs` identifiers and HGVS coding/intronic coordinates for all 8 SNPs and all missense variants are clearly specified, and transfection normalization (`pcDNA3.1/His/lacZ` $\beta$-galactosidase weighting on a Roche COBAS Integra 800 analyzer) is well described.
- **Deficiencies:** Truncation of Table 3 after Patient 34 omits primary functional data for 13 patients (12 mutations); standard deviations for the $n \ge 4$ transfection replicates are omitted; and the duplicate allele string for Haplotypes D and F in Table 4 requires correction.
