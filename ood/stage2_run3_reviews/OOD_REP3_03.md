### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This cross-sectional neuroimaging study examines whether resting-state fMRI network segregation and hemispheric lateralization differ across 21 young adults (Y; $23.1 \pm 3.4$ years), 27 high-functioning older adults (OA HF; $73.5 \pm 5.0$ years, Short Physical Performance Battery $[\text{SPPB}] > 10$), and 54 low-functioning older adults (OA LF; $76.0 \pm 7.2$ years, $\text{SPPB} \le 10$), and whether network segregation relates to 400 m self-selected walking speed and NIH Toolbox list-sorting working memory. Using 10 functional networks (279 nodes across cerebral cortex and SUIT-normalized cerebellum) defined a priori in an independent dataset (WashU-120), the authors compute node-to-node positive Fisher $z$-transformed correlations on 8-minute multiband rs-fMRI scans ($\text{TR} = 1500\text{ ms}$) and evaluate group differences via ANCOVA (controlling for sex), multivariate associations via regularized canonical correlation analysis (CCA), and post-hoc univariate-screened backward stepwise regressions. They report that sensorimotor and vestibular network segregation are higher in OA HF than OA LF (with sensorimotor segregation not significantly different between Y and OA HF), that Group $\times$ Laterality interactions are null for segregation despite significant main effects of hemisphere and significant Group $\times$ Laterality interactions for within/between-network connectivity, and that network segregation exhibits multivariate and regression associations with walking speed and working memory. Node MNI coordinates are provided in Supplementary Table 1, while participant-level data and analysis scripts are available only upon request.

The study has notable design strengths, including defining the 279 network nodes in an external dataset (WashU-120) to avoid circular ROI selection, dedicated cerebellar normalization via CERES/SUIT, and low head motion verified across groups. However, the statistical analysis and resulting behavioral interpretations are weakened by four methodological issues that can and should be resolved through computational re-analysis. First, the CCA and multiple regression models predicting 400 m walking speed simultaneously include the group indicator (Y vs. OA, or OA HF vs. OA LF) alongside walking speed; because OA HF vs. OA LF was defined using the SPPB—which directly incorporates gait speed—group membership and walking speed are circularly coupled. Second, 35.2% (19/54) of the OA LF group is missing working memory data, yet the CCA appears to include all $N=102$ (or $N=81$ OA) participants via an unspecified missing-data regularization routine in the R `CCA` package, producing an artifactual counter-signed loading between walking speed and working memory in older adults that contradicts the group means in Table 1. Third, all repeated-measures ANCOVAs testing Laterality ($F(1, 98)$) and Group $\times$ Laterality ($F(2, 98)$) report between-subject residual degrees of freedom ($df = 98$) rather than within-subject error degrees of freedom, and no multiple-comparison correction is applied across the 6 networks $\times$ 3 connectivity metrics (18 omnibus models). Fourth, two-stage univariate p-value screening followed by backward stepwise selection inflates Type I error in the post-hoc regressions.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Sensorimotor and vestibular network segregation are significantly higher in high-functioning than low-functioning older adults, driven primarily by preserved within-network connectivity: [Moderately Supported].**  
  The pairwise contrasts between OA HF and OA LF for sensorimotor ($t = 2.686$, Holm-adjusted $p = 0.017$) and vestibular ($t = 2.020$, Holm-adjusted $p = 0.046$) segregation (Table 2) and within-network connectivity (Table 3: sensorimotor $p = 0.004$, vestibular $p = 0.028$) are consistent and biologically plausible. However, omnibus $p$-values across the 6 networks were not corrected for multiple testing (where DLPFC [$p = 0.036$], ACC [$p = 0.024$], and Visual [$p = 0.038$] omnibus group effects are marginal), and the small older-adult age difference between OA HF ($73.5 \pm 5.0$ years) and OA LF ($76.0 \pm 7.2$ years) was not included as a continuous covariate alongside sex.

* **There are no Group $\times$ Laterality interaction effects on network segregation, indicating preserved resting-state lateralization of segregation across age and physical function groups: [Fragile].**  
  First, as the authors acknowledge in the Discussion (Section 4.2, line 370), the "left" and "right" networks were defined by whole-brain seed-to-voxel thresholded maps ($T > 12$) from unilateral seeds, meaning each "hemisphere-specific" network actually contains nodes spanning both hemispheres and the cerebellum. Second, every Laterality main effect ($F(1, 98)$) and Group $\times$ Laterality interaction ($F(2, 98)$) in Sections 3.1 and 3.2 reports 98 denominator degrees of freedom—the exact between-subject residual $df$ for $N = 102$ subjects with 3 groups and 1 sex covariate ($102 - 3 - 1 = 98$)—indicating that model specification or degrees-of-freedom extraction in the repeated-measures ANCOVA did not properly account for the within-subject pairing of Left and Right networks.

* **Canonical correlation analysis demonstrates a dissociation in older adults where slower walking speed is associated with higher working memory and higher DLPFC/visual segregation: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In Section 3.4 (Table 8, Fig. 6) and Section 4.3 (line 383), the authors interpret opposite canonical weights on walking speed ($-0.698$) and working memory ($+0.451$) in the older-adult CCA as evidence that slower-walking older adults have higher working memory. However, Table 1 shows the exact opposite in the raw data: OA LF has both slower walking speed ($0.9 \pm 0.2$ vs. $1.1 \pm 0.2\text{ m/s}$) and lower working memory ($15.5 \pm 3.3$ vs. $16.7 \pm 1.7$) than OA HF, and univariate regressions between working memory and all 10 networks in older adults were completely null (Section 3.4, line 332). This CCA sign artifact arises from (a) including both `physical function group` (SPPB, which contains gait speed) and `400 m walking speed` in the same canonical variate alongside 10 correlated network predictors ($p + q = 14$ variables on only $62$ complete older-adult cases), and (b) handling 23 missing working memory values (35.2% missing in OA LF) via an undocumented missing-data procedure in the `CCA` package while reporting Wilks' $\Lambda$ denominator degrees of freedom ($df_2 = 255.91$ and $70.00$ in Table 8) that assume $N = 81$ complete observations without imputation variance adjustment.

* **Post-hoc multiple linear regressions show that left ACC $\times$ age group interaction predicts walking speed and left sensorimotor $\times$ age group interaction predicts working memory: [Fragile].**  
  Screening 20 candidate predictors (10 networks + 10 network $\times$ group interactions) via marginal univariate regressions ($p < 0.05$) and feeding the survivors into backward stepwise elimination on the same dataset violates valid post-selection inference. In Table 7 (working memory, $n = 76$), the interaction term ($p = 0.045$) would not survive any correction for screening 20 candidates, and biological sex was dropped from the model ($df = 3, 72$ for $n = 76$ leaves only `Left Sensorimotor`, `Age group`, and their interaction, despite the Table 7 caption stating "controlled for biological sex"). Moreover, between Table 6 (all subjects) and Table 9 (older adults only), the significant sensorimotor predictor of walking speed switches hemispheres from Left Sensorimotor (Table 6, $p < 0.001$) to Right Sensorimotor (Table 9, $p = 0.009$), which is not reconciled in the Abstract.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Remove Group Indicator Variables from the Behavioral Side of the CCA and Walking-Speed Regressions
* **WHY:** Because OA HF vs. OA LF is defined by the SPPB (which includes short-distance gait speed), placing both `physical function group` (or `age group`) and `400 m walking speed` inside the "Function variable dataset" of the CCA (and as a predictor in Table 9) creates circular collinearity where the canonical variate simply recaptures the between-group split already tested in Aim 1.
* **WHAT:** Restrict the behavioral variable set $Y$ in the CCA to continuous behavioral outcomes (`walking speed` and `working memory`), residualized for sex (and continuous age within the older adult cohort).
* **HOW:** Residualize the 10 network segregation scores and the 2 behavioral outcomes (`walking speed`, `working memory`) on biological sex (and on continuous age in years for the older-adult-only analysis). Run the CCA between the 2 continuous behavioral outcomes and the 10 network scores, and verify statistical significance using a non-parametric permutation test (1,000 random shuffles of participant rows) rather than asymptotic Wilks' $\Lambda$ approximations, which are anti-conservative at $N = 62\text{–}76$.

#### 2. Properly Handle the 35% Missing Working Memory Data in Older Adults
* **WHY:** Nineteen of 54 OA LF participants (35.2%) and 4 of 27 OA HF participants (14.8%) lack working memory scores (Table 1). Standard ridge-regularized CCA (`CCA::rcc`) regularizes covariance matrices for multicollinearity, not non-random missing outcome data, and Table 8's degrees of freedom ($df_2 = 70$ for Dimension 4) incorrectly treat all 81 older adults as having complete data.
* **WHAT:** Perform complete-case sensitivity analysis ($n = 76$ total; $n = 58$ older adults) alongside multiple imputation (MICE) with Rubin's rules, and test whether the 23 older adults missing working memory differ systematically in SPPB, walking speed, age, or scan-to-behavior time lag.
* **HOW:** Compare demographic, gait, and segregation metrics between older adults with ($n = 58$) and without ($n = 23$) working memory data. Re-run the CCA strictly on complete cases ($n = 74\text{–}76$ across all groups; $n = 57\text{–}58$ in older adults) and compare the canonical loadings against 20 multiply imputed datasets generated via `mice` in R.

#### 3. Correct the Linear Mixed-Effects / Repeated-Measures Specification for Laterality Analyses
* **WHY:** Reporting $F(1, 98)$ for Laterality and $F(2, 98)$ for Group $\times$ Laterality across $N = 102$ participants indicates that within-subject error degrees of freedom were miscalculated or that hemispheres were not modeled with subject-level random intercepts.Furthermore, seeds generated whole-brain clusters containing bilateral nodes.
* **WHAT:** Fit a linear mixed-effects model with a random intercept per participant for all lateralized networks, and verify true anatomical laterality by restricting Left and Right network nodes to their respective ipsilateral cerebral hemispheres (and contralateral cerebellar hemispheres).
* **HOW:** Using `lme4::lmer(metric ~ Group * Laterality + Sex + (1 | Subject_ID))` with Satterthwaite or Kenward–Roger degrees of freedom (`lmerTest`), recompute all Laterality and Group $\times$ Laterality $F$-statistics, and apply Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction across the 6 networks. As a sensitivity check, recompute network segregation after masking out cross-hemisphere cortical nodes from Supplementary Table 1 so that "Left" and "Right" networks are strictly lateralized.

#### 4. Replace Univariate Screening + Stepwise Regression with Penalized or Pre-Specified Models
* **WHY:** Screening 20 marginal terms at $p < 0.05$ and applying backward elimination biases regression coefficients upward and invalidates nominal $p$-values (such as $p = 0.045$ in Table 7, where Sex was also inadvertently dropped).
* **WHAT:** Test the pre-registered/a priori anatomical hypotheses directly in a single multiple regression model (or elastic-net model with cross-validated tuning) while retaining the Sex covariate in all models.
* **HOW:** In line with Hypothesis 2 (Section 1, line 25), fit a single hypothesis-driven model for walking speed using the mobility networks (Sensorimotor, Vestibular, Visual) and a single model for working memory using the cognitive networks (DLPFC, ACC), keeping `Sex` forced into every model (fixing Table 7 so $df$ reflects `Sex` inclusion) and controlling for scan-to-behavior interval ($81.5 \pm 94.8\text{ days}$) as a covariate.

#### 5. Technical and Editorial Corrections
* **WHY:** Minor numerical typos and discrepancies between tables and text reduce clarity.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  1. In Section 3.3 (line 269), correct "$r^2 = 360$" to "$R^2 = 0.360$".
  2. In Table 6 (line 285) vs. text (line 269), reconcile the Age group $p$-value ($p = 0.283$ in Table 6 vs. $p = 0.286$ in text).
