### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript models the current and future potential geographic distributions of three medicinal thyme species (*Thymus fedtschenkoi*, *T. pubescens*, and *T. transcaucasicus*) across Iran using Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt) modeling. Drawing on herbarium records (HSBU, IRAN, TARI), literature, GBIF, and field verification, the authors combine occurrence records with 9 environmental predictors (5 WorldClim bioclimatic variables, 1 SoilGrids edaphic variable [soil organic carbon, `Ocr`], and 3 topographic variables [altitude `Alt`, `Slope`, and `Solar` radiation]) at 30 arc-second ($\sim 1\text{ km}^2$) resolution. Using 10-fold cross-validation and projecting onto a 16-GCM ensemble under two CMIP5 Representative Concentration Pathways (RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5) for the 2050s and 2070s, the authors report AUC values of 0.940–0.975, identify altitude (`Alt`) as the top predictor for *T. fedtschenkoi* (33.8%) and *T. pubescens* (47.9%) and soil organic carbon (`Ocr`) for *T. transcaucasicus* (34.6%), and project net habitat contractions of 8.71% to 29.89% across all species and scenarios. Neither the occurrence coordinates nor the R modeling scripts are publicly deposited ("available with the corresponding author on reasonable request").

Although assessing climate vulnerability for endemic Irano-Turanian and Hyrcanian montane plants is a worthwhile conservation objective, the modeling framework and manuscript execution exhibit critical flaws. First, by including static `Altitude` (along with static `Slope`, `Solar`, and `Ocr`) and excluding primary temperature variables ($\text{Bio1}, \text{Bio5}, \text{Bio6}, \text{Bio10}, \text{Bio11}$) during collinearity filtering, the models assign **68.3% to 81.2% of total percent contribution** (and up to **88.7% of permutation importance** in *T. fedtschenkoi*) to static topographic/edaphic layers that remain completely invariant between 2020, 2050, and 2070. Because `Altitude` serves as a static proxy for present-day mountain temperature in the fitted model, holding `Altitude` fixed under future warming mathematically prevents the modeled species from shifting upward in elevation to track their thermal niche while leaving almost no thermal covariates ($\text{bio9}$ contributes only 1.9%–2.4%, or 0% in *T. transcaucasicus*) to register warming. Second, basic dataset parameters—most notably the exact number of occurrence records ($N$) per species and whether spatial thinning was performed—are completely omitted from the manuscript. Third, the text contains direct self-contradictions (e.g., claiming the modeled 1,400–3,500 m elevation range of *T. fedtschenkoi* is "in line with" a cited study reporting 0–1,400 m; stating 9 variables were used for each species while Table 2 shows only 7), arithmetic errors in Table 3, and systematic figure/table misreferences.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Altitude is the primary environmental determinant of *T. fedtschenkoi* (33.8% PC) and *T. pubescens* (47.9% PC), and all three species will suffer 8.71%–29.89% net habitat loss by 2050–2070 without upward elevational expansion: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  Plants do not respond physiologically to meters above sea level (`Alt`) per se, but to temperature, partial pressure, and radiation that covary with elevation. Because the authors included `Alt` in the Pearson correlation filter ($|r| > 0.70$, line 49) and dropped the collinear temperature variables, *T. fedtschenkoi* and *T. pubescens* contain only a single temperature variable (`bio9`, mean temperature of the driest quarter, contributing just 2.4% and 1.9% PC; Table 2), while *T. transcaucasicus* contains only mean diurnal range (`Bio2`, 6.1% PC) and no seasonal or annual mean temperature variable at all. Meanwhile, static variables (`Alt` + `Slope` + `Solar` + `Ocr`) account for 68.3% PC (88.7% PI) in *T. fedtschenkoi* and 81.2% PC (56.7% PI) in *T. pubescens*. When projecting to 2050 and 2070, these static variables cannot change, artificially anchoring the species to their current elevation bands while projected range changes are driven almost entirely by shifts in summer precipitation (`bio14`, `bio18`).

* **MaxEnt models achieved excellent predictive accuracy ($\text{AUC} = 0.940\text{–}0.975$) across 9 environmental variables per species: [Fragile].**  
  First, the manuscript never reports the sample size ($N$) of presence points for *T. fedtschenkoi*, *T. pubescens*, or *T. transcaucasicus*, nor does it apply spatial thinning to remove herbarium collection clustering. Second, because all three species are restricted to the Alborz, Zagros, and Kopet-Dagh mountain chains in northern and western Iran (Fig. 2), drawing background points across the entirety of Iran ($1.65 \times 10^6\text{ km}^2$, dominated by the Lut and Dasht-e Kavir interior deserts) guarantees $\text{AUC} > 0.90$ simply by separating high mountains from lowland deserts. Third, the text in "Results" (line 94) contradicts itself: it states "9 variables were selected for each (Table 2)," yet Table 2 shows that each species actually used only 7 variables (with two variables marked `–` per species).

* **The modeled current elevational niche of *T. fedtschenkoi* (1,400–3,500 m a.s.l.) agrees with published autecological studies in Iran: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In the Discussion (line 198), the authors write that suitable regions for *T. fedtschenkoi* are "distinguished by an elevation of 1400–3500 m" and immediately follow with: "Autecology studies of *T. fedschenkoi*, conducted by Ghelichnia at an altitude of Mazandaran province in Iran, have revealed that the suitable distribution altitude range of the mentioned species was from sea level to 1400 m.a.s.l. Our findings are in line with the mentioned studies." An interval of $1,400\text{–}3,500\text{ m}$ has zero overlap (except at the boundary point $1,400\text{ m}$) with $0\text{–}1,400\text{ m}$; this directly contradicts the authors' claim of agreement.

* **Quantitative range-change percentages in Table 3 accurately reflect habitat gain minus loss: [Fragile].**  
  In Table 3, $\text{Change (\%)} = \text{Gain (\%)} - \text{Loss (\%)}$, which holds for most rows, but fails in two rows: for *T. fedtschenkoi* under RCP 4.5 in 2070, $\text{Gain} = 0.4\%$ and $\text{Loss} = 8.75\%$, which yields $0.40 - 8.75 = -8.35\%$, yet Table 3 reports $-8.71\%$ (copied from the 2050 column); for *T. transcaucasicus* under RCP 8.5 in 2070, $\text{Gain} = 2.5\%$ and $\text{Loss} = 23.14\%$, which yields $2.50 - 23.14 = -20.64\%$, yet Table 3 reports $-21.09\%$. Furthermore, the threshold rule used to binarize continuous MaxEnt suitability into presence/absence to compute Gain and Loss is never specified.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Replace Static Altitude with Direct Bioclimatic Temperature Predictors in Climate Change Projections
* **WHY:** Including static `Altitude` causes collinearity filtering to drop biologically causal temperature variables ($\text{Bio1}, \text{Bio6}, \text{Bio10}, \text{Bio11}$) and locks 68%–81% of the model's predictive weight onto static terrain layers, preventing simulated upward elevational migration under 2050/2070 warming.
* **WHAT:** Exclude `Altitude` from the predictor pool before running the $|r| > 0.70$ collinearity filter so that physiologically direct temperature variables (e.g., $\text{Bio1}$ [Annual Mean Temperature] or $\text{Bio10}/\text{Bio11}$ [Warmest/Coldest Quarter Mean Temperature]) are retained alongside topographic `Slope`, `Solar`, and edaphic `Ocr`.
* **HOW:** Recompute the Pearson correlation matrix across the 19 WorldClim bioclimatic variables + `Slope` + `Solar` + `Ocr` (omitting `Alt`), select the non-collinear bioclimatic temperature and precipitation set ($|r| \le 0.70$), refit the 10-fold cross-validated MaxEnt models for all three *Thymus* species, and re-project the 2050 and 2070 habitat suitability maps and Table 3 range-change metrics.

#### 2. Report Sample Sizes, Spatial Thinning, and Binarization Thresholds
* **WHY:** A species distribution modeling study cannot be evaluated or reproduced without knowing how many presence points ($N$) were used per species, whether spatially clustered points were thinned, and what suitability threshold defined binary habitat gain/loss.
* **WHAT:** Report the raw and spatially thinned occurrence counts ($N$) for each of the three species, the thinning distance (e.g., `spThin` at 5 km or 10 km), the cross-validation test AUC $\pm$ SD across the 10 folds, and the exact threshold value (e.g., maximum training sensitivity plus specificity [MTSS] or 10th percentile training presence) used for Table 3.
* **HOW:** Add a summary table in "Materials and methods" listing raw $N$, thinned $N$, 10-fold mean $\text{AUC} \pm \text{SD}$, True Skill Statistic (TSS), and the binary threshold value for *T. fedtschenkoi*, *T. pubescens*, and *T. transcaucasicus*, and include the occurrence coordinate table as a supplementary CSV file.

#### 3. Clarify the Variable-Selection Procedure Across Species
* **WHY:** Line 49 states that 9 variables were selected after Pearson correlation filtering, line 94 states that 9 variables were selected for each species, and Table 2 shows only 7 variables per species with different variables dropped for different species.
* **WHAT:** Explain how the variable subset was chosen for each species and align the text with Table 2.
* **HOW:** Specify in "Materials and methods" whether Pearson correlation was calculated across all raster cells of Iran or at each species' occurrence points, or whether an initial 9-variable model was pruned to 7 variables per species by dropping variables with low percent contribution. Update the Abstract and line 94 to state that 7 species-specific variables (out of 9 candidate variables) were retained per species.

#### 4. Resolve the Elevational Contradiction and Technical/Editorial Errors
* **WHY:** Multiple arithmetic errors, mislabeled units, broken figure/table cross-references, and unedited submission boilerplate undermine technical credibility.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  1. **Elevational contradiction (line 198):** Explain why the modeled suitable elevation for *T. fedtschenkoi* ($1,400\text{–}3,500\text{ m}$) differs from Ghelichnia's Mazandaran report ($0\text{–}1,400\text{ m}$)—rather than stating they are "in line"—or verify whether response curves in `dismo` were misread.
  2. **Arithmetic in Table 3 (lines 165, 170):** Correct the `Change (%)` values for *T. fedtschenkoi* RCP 4.5 2070 (from $-8.71\%$ to $-8.35\%$) and *T. transcaucasicus* RCP 8.5 2070 (from $-21.09\%$ to $-20.64\%$, or correct the underlying Gain/Loss numbers).
  3. **Table 1 unit error (line 63):** Change the unit of `bio15` (Precipitation seasonality, coefficient of variation) from `mm` to `%` (or dimensionless).
  4. **Software version error (line 76):** Correct "MaxEnt version 1.0–3" to the actual MaxEnt Java version used (e.g., v3.4.3 or v3.4.4 called via `dismo` v1.3-9).
  5. **Broken figure and table cross-references:** In line 129, change "well-matched in Fig. 3" to "Fig. 4"; in line 187, change "explored in Table 3 and Fig. 3" to "Table 2 and Fig. 3"; in lines 198–200, change "Fig. 3, panel A / Fig. 3 B / Fig. 3 C" (which are environmental variable maps) to "Fig. 4 A / Fig. 4 B / Fig. 4 C" (the current habitat suitability maps).
  6. **Spelling and editorial cleanup:** Standardize species and province spellings throughout (*T. fedtschenkoi* instead of *T. fedschenkoi*; *T. pubescens* instead of *T. pubescense*; *East Azerbaijan* instead of *Eest Azarbaijan*; *Kurdistan* instead of *Kurditan*; *Mazandaran* instead of *Mazandran*), and remove the submission cover-letter sentence on line 221.
