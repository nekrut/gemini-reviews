### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This bioinformatics manuscript presents `iNGNN-DTI`, a deep learning architecture for binary drug–target interaction (DTI) prediction that integrates 2D/3D graph representations with pre-trained sequence language models. Drug SMILES are converted via RDKit into molecular graphs (78-dimensional atom features), and target protein sequences are folded with AlphaFold2 to construct $8\text{ \AA}$ $C_\alpha$ contact graphs (54-dimensional residue features including a MSA-derived Position Probability Matrix). Both graphs are encoded by a $k$-subgraph nested graph neural network (`NGNN`) with a virtual node, coupled through a bidirectional cross-attention-free transformer (`cross-AFT`) module, and concatenated with projected sequence embeddings from pre-trained `Chemformer` (512D $\to$ 128D) and `ESM` (768D $\to$ 128D) models before classification via a multilayer perceptron. Evaluated across five random 7:1:2 splits on Davis, KIBA, and BIOSNAP benchmarks—and on unseen-drug and unseen-protein splits of Davis—`iNGNN-DTI` reports higher mean AUROC and AUPRC than four baselines (`DeepDTA`, `MolTrans`, `ML-DTI`, and `DGraphDTA`). The authors also visualize implicit `cross-AFT` attention weights against PDB crystal complexes and perform a COVID-19 virtual screening case study targeting human ACE2. Source code is publicly available on GitHub.

Combining subgraph-expressive GNNs on AlphaFold2 contact maps with pre-trained chemical and protein language models is a sensible architectural direction, and providing an open GitHub repository supports reproducibility. However, a forensic audit reveals several mathematical formulation errors, benchmark and ablation inconsistencies, and a biologically flawed COVID-19 case study. First, Equations (2), (9), (11)–(12), and (15) contain mathematical and dimensional errors: the amino acid Position Probability Matrix (Eq. 2) divides the pseudocount by $4$ (the DNA nucleotide alphabet size) instead of $20$ or $21$; the `cross-AFT` module (Eq. 9) defines a fixed learnable position-bias matrix $w \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{drug}} \times N_{\text{prot}}}$ across unordered molecular graphs of variable atom/residue counts; Equations (11)–(12) concatenate unpooled 2D node matrices $Y \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times d}$ directly with 1D vectors $P \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$; and Equation (15) uses a single index $n = 1, \dots, N_{\text{prot}}$ for both drug queries and protein keys. Second, in the ablation study on Davis (Table 6), adding the pre-trained `Chemformer`/`ESM` features actually *reduces* AUPRC (from $0.478$ in `NGNN + cross-AFT` to $0.473$ in the full model, and from $0.464$ to $0.431$ without `cross-AFT`), contradicting the text's claim of synergistic improvement, while Table 5 contains an exact 4-decimal-place copy-paste duplication for `DeepDTA` AUPRC ($0.145 \pm 0.0300$). Third, the COVID-19 case study screens for binders to human ACE2 catalytic/surface pockets without distinguishing spike-RBD interface blockers from ACE2 enzymatic inhibitors (misinterpreting the host MMP/ADAM inhibitor Marimastat as an ACE2-targeted drug and reporting an artifactual $-14.5\text{ kcal/mol}$ docking score for erythromycin as "lowest binding affinity").

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **`iNGNN-DTI` outperforms `DeepDTA`, `MolTrans`, `ML-DTI`, and `DGraphDTA` on random 7:1:2 splits of Davis, KIBA, and BIOSNAP (Table 4) and cold-drug/cold-protein splits of Davis (Table 5): [Moderately Supported].**  
  On Davis, `iNGNN-DTI` improves random-split AUPRC from $0.381$ (`ML-DTI`) to $0.473$ and unseen-drug AUPRC from $0.169$ to $0.224$. However, under a random 7:1:2 pair split on Davis ($68\text{ drugs} \times 442\text{ kinases}$, a complete matrix) and KIBA, 100% of test drugs and 100% of test proteins are present in the training set, so random-split metrics primarily reflect transductive matrix completion rather than inductive generalization. Moreover, cold-drug and cold-protein splits were evaluated only on the smallest dataset (Davis, where 20% of 68 drugs is just 13 test drugs, resulting in wide standard deviations of $\pm 0.049\text{–}0.070$) and omitted KIBA, BIOSNAP, and the cold-pair (unseen drug $\times$ unseen protein) setting. In Table 5, the `DeepDTA` AUPRC entries for `unseen drugs` ($0.145 \pm 0.0300$) and `unseen proteins` ($0.145 \pm 0.0300$) are identical to four decimal places in both mean and SD, indicating a tabular transcription error.

* **Both the `cross-AFT` interaction module and the unsupervised pre-trained sequence models (`Chemformer` and `ESM`) consistently improve DTI prediction in the ablation study (Table 6): [Overstated].**  
  In Table 6 (Davis dataset), adding `cross-AFT` to `NGNN` (Test 3 vs. Test 2) improves AUPRC from $0.464 \pm 0.0357$ to $0.478 \pm 0.0222$. However, adding the unsupervised pre-trained models (`Chemformer` + `ESM`) to `NGNN` (Test 4 vs. Test 2) **decreases** AUPRC from $0.464$ to $0.431 \pm 0.0103$, and adding the unsupervised models to `NGNN + cross-AFT` (Test 5 vs. Test 3) **decreases** AUPRC from $0.478$ to $0.473 \pm 0.0167$. On the heavily imbalanced Davis dataset ($1,506$ positives vs. $28,550$ negatives; $5.0\%$ positive rate), AUPRC is far more informative than AUROC, yet the text (Section 3.4, line 366) compares Test 5 ($0.473$) only against Test 2 ($0.464$) while ignoring that Test 3 (`NGNN + cross-AFT` without pre-trained models, $0.478$) achieved the highest AUPRC.

* **The mathematical formulation of the PPM feature (Eq. 2), `cross-AFT` module (Eqs. 9, 15), and feature fusion (Eqs. 11–12) accurately specifies the implemented neural network: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  Four mathematical errors appear in the equations:
  1. In Eq. (2) (line 125), the amino acid Position Probability Matrix is written as $M_{r,k} = \frac{F_{r,k} + p/4}{N + p}$ over $R = 21$ residue types (Table 3). Because the uniform pseudocount prior for 21 residue types must be $p/21$ (not $p/4$, which is for 4 nucleotides), the column probabilities sum to $\frac{N + 5.25p}{N + p} \ne 1$.
  2. In Eq. (9) (line 204), `cross-AFT` includes a learnable pairwise position bias $w \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{drug}} \times N_{\text{prot}}}$. Unlike fixed-length NLP token sequences, molecular graphs have variable atom/residue counts ($N_{\text{drug}}, N_{\text{prot}}$) and non-canonical atom indexing in RDKit, so a fixed positional matrix $w_{n,n'}$ is neither permutation-invariant nor well-defined without truncation/padding.
  3. In Eqs. (11)–(12) (lines 225, 231), the unpooled node-feature matrices $Y_{\text{drug}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{drug}} \times d}$ and $Y_{\text{prot}} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_{\text{prot}} \times d}$ are concatenated directly with the 1D global vectors $P_{\text{drug}}, P_{\text{prot}} \in \mathbb{R}^{128}$, omitting the graph-level readout/pooling operator ($\text{Pool}: \mathbb{R}^{N \times d} \to \mathbb{R}^d$) required to match tensor ranks.
  4. In Eq. (15) (line 328), the implicit attention definition writes $a_{\text{drug}}^{n,i} = \sigma_q(Q_{\text{drug}}^{n,i}) \frac{\exp(K_{\text{prot}}^{n,i} + w_n)}{\sum_{n'=1}^{N_{\text{prot}}} \exp(K_{\text{prot}}^{n',i} + w_{n,n'})}$ for $n = 1, \dots, N_{\text{prot}}$, which conflates the drug atom index $n \in \{1, \dots, N_{\text{drug}}\}$ with the protein residue index $n' \in \{1, \dots, N_{\text{prot}}\}$ in the numerator.

* **Implicit `cross-AFT` attention weights localize protein binding sites and repurpose clinically relevant COVID-19 ACE2 inhibitors (Marimastat, Erythromycin, Pseudoephedrine): [Overstated].**  
  In Section 3.3.1 (line 337), the authors visualize the single residue with the highest weight across *each of the $d$ feature dimensions* ("residues with the highest weight in each dimension"), which places up to $d$ markers across the protein and creates the visual impression of binding-site coverage even under uniform random weights; as the authors candidly note in the Conclusion (line 422), "the attention weights assigned to the protein target do not always exhibit a strong correlation with the actual positions of the residues." In the COVID-19 case study (Section 3.5), predicting generic binders to human ACE2 conflates blocking the exterior SARS-CoV-2 Spike-RBD interface with inhibiting ACE2's endogenous carboxypeptidase catalytic cleft: Marimastat (cited from Yamamoto et al., 2022) inhibits host MMP/ADAM metalloproteases rather than acting as a therapeutic ACE2 blocker, and line 399 misinterprets an artifactual docking score of $-14.5\text{ kcal/mol}$ for the macrolide erythromycin as "exhibiting the lowest binding affinity" (confusing most negative $\Delta G$ with lowest affinity).

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Correct the Mathematical Equations for PPM, Cross-AFT, Graph Pooling, and Implicit Attention
* **WHY:** Equations (2), (9), (11)–(12), and (15) are dimensionally or probabilistically invalid as written and prevent accurate replication of the architecture from the text.
* **WHAT:** Fix the pseudocount normalization in Eq. (2), specify how $w_{n,n'}$ handles variable graph sizes and atom permutation invariance in Eq. (9), include the explicit graph readout/pooling equations before Eqs. (11)–(12), and separate the drug atom index $m \in \{1, \dots, N_{\text{drug}}\}$ from the protein residue index $n \in \{1, \dots, N_{\text{prot}}\}$ in Eqs. (14)–(15).
* **HOW:**
  1. In Eq. (2), replace $p/4$ with $p/21$ (for $R = 21$ amino acid symbols) so that $\sum_{r=1}^{21} M_{r,k} = 1$.
  2. In Section 2.3.2, clarify whether $w_{n,n'}$ is omitted ($w = 0$) to preserve graph permutation invariance or padded/truncated to a fixed maximum size $(N_{\text{drug}}^{\max}, N_{\text{prot}}^{\max})$.
  3. Before Eq. (11), define the graph-level pooling operation $\bar{y}_{\text{drug}} = \text{Readout}(Y_{\text{drug}}) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ (e.g., mean, sum, or virtual-node readout) so that $F_{\text{drug}} = \text{FC}([\bar{y}_{\text{drug}} \parallel P_{\text{drug}}])$.
  4. In Eqs. (14)–(15), index drug atoms by $m \in \{1, \dots, N_{\text{drug}}\}$ and protein residues by $n \in \{1, \dots, N_{\text{prot}}\}$ so that $a_{\text{drug}}^{m,n,i} = \sigma_q(Q_{\text{drug}}^{m,i}) \frac{\exp(K_{\text{prot}}^{n,i} + w_{m,n})}{\sum_{n'=1}^{N_{\text{prot}}} \exp(K_{\text{prot}}^{n',i} + w_{m,n'})}$.

#### 2. Expand Unseen-Drug, Unseen-Protein, and Unseen-Pair Benchmarks to KIBA and BIOSNAP
* **WHY:** Evaluating cold-start generalization solely on Davis (where 20% of 68 drugs is only 13 drugs) has high variance and leaves out KIBA (2,068 drugs) and BIOSNAP (4,510 drugs), as well as the strict unseen-pair (both drug and protein unseen) regime.
* **WHAT:** Run the unseen-drug, unseen-protein, and unseen-pair (cold-pair) splits across Davis, KIBA, and BIOSNAP, and fix the duplicated `DeepDTA` AUPRC entry in Table 5.
* **HOW:** Apply the 7:1:2 entity-level clustering/splitting protocol to KIBA and BIOSNAP, add a third column block in Table 5 for the cold-pair (`unseen drug & unseen protein`) setting, check the raw logs for `DeepDTA` on Davis `unseen drugs` vs. `unseen proteins` to correct the duplicated $0.145 \pm 0.0300$ value in Table 5, and run a sensitivity ablation onDavis and KIBA Explaining why adding `Chemformer` + `ESM` reduced AUPRC in Table 6 (e.g., testing dropout or L2 regularization on the 128D sequence projection layers to prevent overfitting to majority-class negatives).

#### 3. Quantify Binding-Site Localization Accuracy Across All PDB-Annotated Test Complexes
* **WHY:** Plotting the per-dimension argmax residue on 4 cherry-picked proteins (Fig. 3) does not quantitatively test whether `cross-AFT` attention identifies true binding pockets better than random or surface-exposed baselines.
* **WHAT:** Compute a quantitative top-$k$ binding-site hit rate (or ROC-AUC for pocket residue identification) across all drug–target pairs in the test set that have solved co-crystal structures in the PDB.
* **HOW:** For each test pair with a reference PDB complex, define ground-truth binding pocket residues as those within $5\text{ \AA}$ of the co-crystallized ligand heavy atoms, aggregate the residue-level `cross-AFT` importance score by taking the $L_2$ norm or mean across the $d$ feature dimensions (rather than taking $d$ separate argmax indices), and report the precision@10 and AUROC of pocket residue recovery compared against a random-residue baseline.

#### 4. Reframe the COVID-19 Case Study Around Interface Specificity and Correct Docking Terminology
* **WHY:** In Section 3.5, binding to ACE2's catalytic zinc cleft (e.g., by the metalloprotease inhibitor Marimastat) inhibits physiological angiotensin cleavage without necessarily blocking the distal Spike-RBD binding surface, and $-14.5\text{ kcal/mol}$ is misdescribed as "lowest binding affinity."
* **WHAT:** Distinguish docking poses at the Spike-RBD interface (residues 19–42, 353–357 of ACE2) from poses inside the catalytic peptidase cleft, include a random-50 drug negative control in Figure 7, and correct the thermodynamic terminology.
* **HOW:** Re-evaluate the top 50 predicted ACE2 binders by computing the distance between each docked pose and the SARS-CoV-2 RBD binding interface on ACE2, add a third boxplot in Figure 7 showing docking scores of 50 randomly selected BIOSNAP drugs (with a Mann–Whitney $U$ test comparing Top 50 vs. Random 50), clarify that Marimastat targets host MMP/ADAM proteases in Yamamoto et al. (2022), and change "lowest binding affinity with a score of $-14.5\text{ kcal/mol}$" (line 399) to "most negative predicted binding energy ($-14.5\text{ kcal/mol}$)."
