### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript investigates how climate-driven phenological mismatches between 23 *Viola* plant species and 7 solitary bee pollinators (6 generalists in *Andrena* and *Osmia*, and 1 specialist, *Andrena violae*) could affect plant distributions across the eastern United States between historical/current climates (1895–2022) and the 2070s (SSP5-8.5). Using 4,075 crowdsourced herbarium specimens representing main-flowering *Viola* and 6,714 bee collection records, the authors fit spatial-temporal linear mixed-effects models (`spaMM`) to predict flowering and bee activity day-of-year (DOY), build species distribution models (GLM, GAM, MaxEnt) to estimate plant occurrence probabilities $P(\text{occurrence})$, and define a "secondary local extinction risk" metric ($S_e$) from the non-overlapping fraction of the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of predicted plant and bee DOYs. They report that *Viola* flowering advances by $14 \pm 1.0\text{ days}$ per SD of long-term mean annual temperature (MAT; $4^\circ\text{C}$) and $2 \pm 0.3\text{ days}$ per SD of MAT anomaly ($0.7^\circ\text{C}$), whereas bee activity responds only to long-term MAT. They further conclude that future secondary local extinction risk increases with latitude and that the latitudinal sensitivity of $S_e$ to phenological mismatch differs between generalist and specialist pollinators. Data and R scripts are publicly available on GitHub.

While the historical phenological dataset and the mixed-effects estimates of plant and bee thermal sensitivities represent a useful empirical contribution, the manuscript's central conclusions regarding "secondary local extinction risk" ($S_e$) and its latitudinal sensitivity rest on a circular mathematical construction. Specifically, the authors neither measure plant population decline nor observe local extinctions; instead, in Equations (5)–(7), they algebraically define $S_e$ as the product of SDM occurrence probability and phenological mismatch, $S_e \equiv P(\text{occurrence}) \times \text{Mismatch}$. They then fit power ($y = a x^b$) and logistic functions regressing this synthetic product $S_e$ against $\text{Mismatch}$ itself, use those fitted curves to project 2070s $S_e$ from future $\text{Mismatch}$, and finally run mixed-effects beta regressions (Table 2; $Z = 382.4$) testing whether their predicted $\hat{S}_e(\text{Mismatch})$ depends on $\text{Mismatch}$ and latitude. Furthermore, the pipeline conflates the 95% confidence interval of the regression mean ($\hat{y} \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$) with the biological duration of flowering and bee activity, assumes all 7 bee species are universally present across every grid cell from Florida to Maine, and contains contradictions in the Methods regarding grid size and bee phenology modeling. Salvaging this work requires removing the circular $S_e$ regression pipeline and reframing the manuscript around empirically supported phenological overlap shifts.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Long-term mean annual temperature (MAT) and temperature anomalies advance *Viola* flowering, whereas bee activity DOY responds primarily to spatial MAT gradients: [Well Supported].**  
  The spatial-temporal linear mixed-effects models (`spaMM` with Matérn spatial and AR(1) temporal random structures) fitted to 4,075 main-flowering *Viola* specimens and 6,714 bee records show high cross-validated predictive performance ($\text{LOOCV } R^2 = 0.796$ for plants, $0.697$ for generalist bees, and $0.590$ for *A. violae*) and provide credible estimates of phenological advancement along thermal gradients.

* **Secondary local extinction risk ($S_e$) of *Viola* increases monotonically with phenological mismatch and follows distinct power or logistic functional responses across plant–pollinator pairs: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  Substituting Equation (5) ($\text{Mismatch} = M = 1 - O/D_p$) and Equation (6) ($P(\text{mismatch}) = P(\text{occurrence}) \cdot (O/D_p)$) into Equation (7) ($S_e = P(\text{occurrence}) - P(\text{mismatch})$) yields the exact algebraic identity:
  $$S_e = P(\text{occurrence}) \times M$$
  Thus, $S_e$ is not an independent empirical measure of local extinction; it is simply phenological mismatch $M$ rescaled by SDM habitat suitability $P(\text{occurrence})$. Regressing $S_e = P(\text{occurrence}) \times M$ against $M$ using power ($S_e = a M^b$) or logistic functions does not uncover a biological mechanism or "tipping-point response" (Methods, p. 11)—it merely fits a curve to $M$ weighted by the spatial covariance between $P(\text{occurrence})$ and $M$. Filtering for pairs with $R^2 > 0.5$ selects those species where $P(\text{occurrence})$ happens to have low variance or monotonic covariance with $M$.

* **The sensitivity of secondary local extinction risk to phenological mismatch decreases with latitude for specialist bees, and either decreases (power model) or increases (logistic model) with latitude for generalist bees (Fig. 6, Table 2): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  To forecast 2070s $S_e$, the authors substitute future mismatch $M_{\text{2070s}}$ directly into the deterministic power or logistic curves $\hat{S}_e(M)$ fitted under current conditions, and then fit a mixed-effects beta regression of $\hat{S}_e(M_{\text{2070s}})$ against $M_{\text{2070s}}$ and latitude (Table 2, yielding artificial test statistics such as $Z = 382.4$). Because $M_{\text{2070s}}$ increases with latitude (Fig. 5B), any concave power function ($S_e = a M^b$ with $0 < b < 1$) has a derivative $\frac{d S_e}{d M} = a b M^{b-1}$ that mathematically *must* decrease as $M$ (and thus latitude) increases, whereas a logistic function evaluated below its inflection point has a derivative $\frac{d S_e}{d M}$ that *must* increase as $M$ increases. The opposite latitudinal sensitivity trends in Fig. 6A vs. Fig. 6C are pure mathematical artifacts of the second derivatives of the two chosen equations, not ecological strategies of generalist vs. specialist pollination networks.

* **Predicted flowering duration and bee activity duration decrease with latitude, driving elevated northern secondary extinction risk (Fig. 5): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In the Methods ("Relationships between climate and phenology" and "Calculation of secondary local extinction risk"), the authors state that they used `predict()` on the `spaMM` linear mixed models to obtain the standard error (SE) of the predicted mean DOY and used the resulting 95% CI ($\hat{y} \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$) as the proxy for the biological duration of flowering ($D_p$) and bee activity ($D_b$). The 95% CI around a regression prediction quantifies the *statistical uncertainty of the mean flowering date* (which scales with local sample size and predictor leverage), not the biological phenophase duration (the spread of flowering dates across individuals/time within a grid cell). Moreover, the Methods section ("Bee Phenology") contradicts this by stating that bee activity periods were extracted from the 95% CI of `MaxLike` spatial occurrence probabilities across 100 random 80/20 splits.

* **Future phenological mismatches will threaten self-sustaining *Viola* populations across the eastern United States: [Overstated].**  
  Two biological assumptions invalidate direct translation of chasmogamous DOY non-overlap into local plant extinction: (1) the authors assume all 7 bee species are present in every grid cell across the entire eastern United States instead of restricting interactions to each bee species' actual geographic range, and (2) as acknowledged in the Methods, 60 of 73 North American *Viola* species produce self-pollinating cleistogamous flowers throughout the growing season, which maintain local seed production and population persistence even when spring chasmogamous pollination fails completely.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Eliminate the Circular $S_e$ Regression Pipeline and Analyze Empirical Phenological Overlap Directly
* **WHY:** Defining $S_e \equiv P(\text{occurrence}) \times M$, fitting power/logistic curves of $S_e$ against $M$, and running beta regressions of the fitted curve outputs $\hat{S}_e(M_{\text{2070s}})$ against $M_{\text{2070s}}$ and latitude is mathematically circular and generates artificial $Z$-statistics ($Z = 382.4$) and curve-dependent latitudinal derivatives.
* **WHAT:** Remove Equations (6)–(7), the power/logistic curve fittings (Fig. 2, Fig. 6, Table 2, SI Tables S8–S27), and claims of "secondary local extinction risk" unless independent demographic/fitness or historical local extirpation data are used.
* **HOW:** Reframe the analysis around (a) the predicted shift in phenological mismatch $\Delta M = M_{\text{2070s}} - M_{\text{1970-2000}}$ (in days and as a fraction of the flowering window) across latitudes, and (b) the joint spatial exposure index combining climatic habitat loss ($\Delta P_{\text{plant}}$) and pollinator phenological divergence ($\Delta M$) within grid cells where both the plant and the bee species are predicted to co-occur.

#### 2. Estimate Phenophase Duration from Residual Phenological Variance Rather Than the Standard Error of the Mean
* **WHY:** The standard error of a regression prediction ($\text{SE}(\hat{y})$) shrinks with sample density and measures uncertainty in the mean DOY, not the length of the flowering or flight season.
* **WHAT:** Replace the 95% CI of the mean prediction with a proper estimate of within-grid-cell phenophase duration (e.g., the 80% or 95% prediction interval, or quantile regression of onset and offset).
* **HOW:** Using the fitted `spaMM` models (or quantile regression via `quantreg`/`qgam` for the 10th and 90th percentiles of DOY), compute the prediction interval width $\pm 1.96 \sqrt{\text{SE}(\hat{y})^2 + \hat{\sigma}_\epsilon^2}$ (or explicitly model $\log(\sigma_\epsilon)$ as a function of latitude/climate in `glmmTMB` using a `dispformula = ~ scale(MAT) + scale(latitude)` specification) so that phenophase duration reflects biological dispersion in flowering and flight dates rather than estimation error of the mean.

#### 3. Incorporate Bee Species Distribution Models Instead of Assuming Universal Presence
* **WHY:** Assuming that all 7 bee species occur in every grid cell from Florida to Maine creates artificial mismatch estimates in grid cells outside a bee species' thermal and geographic range.
* **WHAT:** Restrict plant–pollinator mismatch calculations at each grid cell to bee species predicted to be climatically suitable in that grid cell.
* **HOW:** Use the 6,714 geocoded bee occurrence records already compiled on the $40 \times 40\text{ km}$ grid to fit SDMs (GLM, GAM, MaxEnt) for each of the 7 bee species—or use the `MaxLike` models already described in the "Bee Phenology" section—and mask each plant–bee pair to grid cells where both plant and bee occurrence probabilities exceed their respective TSS/MPA thresholds.

#### 4. Resolve Methodological Contradictions and Technical Errors
* **WHY:** Internal contradictions regarding spatial resolution, bee phenology modeling, and anomaly formulas prevent independent reproduction.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  1. **Grid Resolution Contradiction:** Results ("SDMs and Primary Local Extinction Risk", p. 4) states that the eastern United States was divided into `20 × 20 km grid cells`, whereas Materials and Methods ("Viola Occurrence Data", "Bee Occurrence Data", and "SDMs to estimate the primary extinction risk") repeatedly specifies `40×40-km grid cells` and removes border cells `< 800 km^2` (half of $1,600\text{ km}^2$). Reconcile the text to state the exact grid resolution used.
  2. **Bee Phenology Method Contradiction:** In "Bee Phenology" (p. 8), the authors state they used `MaxLike` with five bioclimatic variables (bio4, bio10, bio15, bio16, bio17) to estimate spatial occurrence probabilities and "extracted the 95% CI to present their main activity periods." However, in "Relationships between climate and phenology" (p. 9), they state bee activity time and its 95% CI were predicted using `spaMM` linear mixed-effects models with DOY as the response variable. Clarify which method actually generated $[L_b, U_b]$ in Equations (2)–(3).
  3. **Precipitation Anomaly Definition:** In "Climatic and geographic data of specimen localities" (p. 9), anomalies are defined as "the difference in climatic conditions between the year of collection and the long-term mean", and later "centered and scaled to a mean value of 0 and a SD of 1." Yet in "Relationships between climate and phenology" (p. 10), future precipitation anomalies are described as "calculated as a proportion of its long-term mean precipitation." Ensure historical training anomalies and future projection anomalies use identical mathematical scaling.
  4. **Table 1 Sign Typo:** In Table 1, the interaction `Scale(latitude): Specialist` has `Estimate ± SE = -0.044 ± 0.011`, but the `Z-value` is printed as positive `3.91`. Correct this to `-3.91` (or `-4.00`).
