### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This study applies `Biomod2` ensemble species distribution modeling (combining 7–8 algorithms with $\text{AUC} > 0.8$ and $\text{TSS} > 0.7$) to project current and future (2041–2060, 2061–2080, 2081–2100 under SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5 using `BCC-CSM2-MR`) habitat suitability across China for the endangered swallowtail butterfly *Luehdorfia chinensis chinensis* ($n = 94$ thinned records) and its two obligate Aristolochiaceae larval host plants, *Asarum forbesii* ($n = 156$) and *Asarum sieboldii* ($n = 162$). Using WorldClim v2.1 bioclimatic layers, topography, and NASA SEDAC Human Influence (HI), alongside `ENMTools` niche overlap indices (Schoener's $D$ and Hellinger's $I$), the authors report that driest-period/annual precipitation (`Bio17`, `Bio14`, `Bio12`) and `HI` are the dominant predictors, that *L. c. chinensis* and *A. forbesii* will expand their ranges northwestward while *A. sieboldii* contracts southwestward, and conclude that climate change will cause *L. c. chinensis* to shift its larval host preference from *A. forbesii* to *A. sieboldii*.

Compiling field and herbarium occurrences for an endemic Data Deficient butterfly and its two obligate host plants is a useful empirical contribution. However, the study's core conclusions and geospatial methodology suffer from major logical and methodological flaws: (1) the headline claim in the Abstract, Discussion, and Conclusion—that climate change will cause *L. c. chinensis* to switch its larval host preference from *A. forbesii* to *A. sieboldii*—is directly refuted by the authors' own statistical analysis in Table 3, where changes in niche overlap ($D$ and $I$) are statistically non-significant (`ns` across all pairs), where niche overlap with *A. forbesii* ($D = 0.709 \pm 0.026$, "High overlap") remains much higher than with *A. sieboldii* ($D = 0.556 \pm 0.028$, "Moderate overlap") across every single future scenario, and where the butterfly's range expansion and northwest centroid shift mirror *A. forbesii* rather than *A. sieboldii*; (2) in Section 2.4 (line 62), despite calculating objective `maxSSS` thresholds ($\sim 0.27–0.30$), the authors discard them and define "total suitable habitat" asymmetrically—summing low + medium + high suitability ($\ge 0.10$) for the butterfly, but summing only medium + high suitability ($\ge 0.42$ and $\ge 0.46$) for the two host plants; and (3) including static present-day Human Influence (`HI`, contributing $8.2\%–20.7\%$) as a predictor confounds intensive survey/specimen collection effort in densely populated eastern China (Jiangsu/Zhejiang/Anhui) with biological habitat preference. Re-analyzing the rasters under symmetric `maxSSS` thresholds and revising the unsupported host-switching narrative are required.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Climate change will drive *L. c. chinensis* to shift its larval host choice from *Asarum forbesii* to *Asarum sieboldii*: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  This central claim (Abstract lines 8, 12; Discussion lines 258–260; Conclusion line 278) contradicts the manuscript's own quantitative results on three levels:
  1. **Statistical non-significance:** Table 3 explicitly reports `ns` ("no significant difference in niche overlap between current and future situations") for both $D$ and $I$ in both species pairs. For *L. c. chinensis* vs. *A. forbesii*, mean future $D$ ($0.709 \pm 0.026$) differs from current $D$ ($0.713$) by only $-0.004$ (and under SSP5-8.5 in 2061–2080 and 2081–2100, $D$ actually *increases* to $0.734$ and $0.745$).
  2. **Absolute niche overlap:** Under every single current and future climate scenario in Table 3, *L. c. chinensis* has substantially higher niche overlap with *A. forbesii* ($D = 0.668–0.745$; $I = 0.892–0.929$) than with *A. sieboldii* ($D = 0.527–0.606$; $I = 0.787–0.856$).
  3. **Internal contradiction between Sections 4.2 and 4.3:** In Section 4.2 (line 253), the authors state that *"The centroid of L. c. chinensis shifts northwest, mirroring that of A. forbesii, whereas A. sieboldii shifts southwest... Both L. c. chinensis and A. forbesii exhibit range expansions over time, while A. sieboldii suffers a pronounced contraction. Overall, L. c. chinensis shifts align closely with A. forbesii but contrast sharply with A. sieboldii."* Claiming in Section 4.3 (line 258) that the butterfly will therefore switch from expanding, co-shifting *A. forbesii* to contracting, divergently shifting *A. sieboldii* is logically incoherent.

* **Under the current climate, *L. c. chinensis* occupies $1,146,520\text{ km}^2$ of suitable habitat, overlapping across 89.0% ($1,020,280\text{ km}^2$) with its host plants: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In Section 2.4 (line 62), the authors compute the objective `maxSSS` threshold for each species ($0.2999$ for *L. c. chinensis*, $0.2908$ for *A. forbesii*, and $0.2713$ for *A. sieboldii*), but then ignore those thresholds and apply arbitrary, asymmetric cutoffs: for the butterfly, "total suitable habitat" includes Low + Medium + High suitability (all grid cells $\ge 0.10$), whereas for the two host plants, "total suitable habitat" includes only Medium + High suitability (grid cells $\ge 0.42$ for *A. forbesii* and $\ge 0.46$ for *A. sieboldii*, discarding Low suitability $0.13–0.42$ and $0.19–0.46$). Using a threshold of $0.10$ for the herbivore and $0.42/0.46$ for the plants invalidates all absolute area comparisons and binary spatial overlay calculations.

* **Dry-season and annual precipitation (`Bio17`, `Bio14`, `Bio12`) are the primary climatic determinants of the distributions of *L. c. chinensis* and its *Asarum* host plants: [Well Supported].**  
  The dominant contribution of precipitation in the driest quarter/month (`Bio17` = 56.4% for the butterfly; `Bio14` = 65.6% for *A. forbesii*; `Bio12` = 38.8% for *A. sieboldii*; Table 2) is biologically well grounded in the life history of *L. c. chinensis* (which spends a 300-day summer/winter pupal diapause in above-ground litter/twigs vulnerable to desiccation) and the shallow surface-rooting habit of *Asarum* understory herbs.

* **Human Influence (`HI`) is a major ecological driver of habitat suitability ($8.2\%–20.7\%$ contribution) for the butterfly and its hosts: [Fragile].**  
  As noted in the Introduction (line 21) and Discussion (line 269), *L. c. chinensis* and its medicinal *Asarum* hosts are negatively impacted by human disturbance and harvesting. The high importance of `HI` in Table 2 (20.7% for *A. forbesii*, 13.7% for *A. sieboldii*, 8.2% for *L. c. chinensis*) is a classic spatial confounding/ascertainment artifact: both the species' subtropical lowland ranges and Chinese specimen/photo collection efforts (`PPBC`, `NSII`, field surveys in Nanjing/Hangzhou) are concentrated in densely populated eastern provinces (Jiangsu, Zhejiang, Anhui). Moreover, keeping present-day `HI` static while projecting climate to 2081–2100 ignores future land-use change.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Apply Consistent `maxSSS` Suitability Thresholds Across Both the Butterfly and Host Plants
* **WHY:** Defining "suitable habitat" as $\ge 0.10$ for *L. c. chinensis* (including the Low suitability class) while defining it as $\ge 0.42$ and $\ge 0.46$ for *A. forbesii* and *A. sieboldii* (excluding the Low suitability class) is an asymmetric methodological artifact that inflates the butterfly's apparent range relative to its hosts.
* **WHAT:** Use the calculated `maxSSS` threshold ($0.2999$, $0.2908$, and $0.2713$; line 62) consistently across all three species as the binary cutoff between Unsuitable ($< \text{maxSSS}$) and Suitable ($\ge \text{maxSSS}$) habitat, and partition the suitable range above `maxSSS` into three equal intervals (Low, Medium, High) for all three species.
* **HOW:** Re-run the raster reclassification and raster algebra overlay in ArcGIS 10.8 using `0.2999`, `0.2908`, and `0.2713` as the lower bound of total suitable habitat for all three taxa, and update the area totals in the Abstract, Section 3.3, Section 3.4, Figure 3, Figure 7, and Table S2.

#### 2. Incorporate Host-Plant Availability as a Biotic Constraint on Butterfly Projections and Revise the Host-Switch Claim
* **WHY:** Because *L. c. chinensis* larvae feed obligately on *A. forbesii* and *A. sieboldii*, any grid cell predicted as climatically suitable for the butterfly where neither host plant is present cannot support a viable butterfly population. Furthermore, Table 3 proves that niche overlap with *A. forbesii* ($D \approx 0.71$) remains far higher than with *A. sieboldii* ($D \approx 0.56$) in all future scenarios, and that temporal changes in $D$ and $I$ are statistically non-significant (`ns`).
* **WHAT:** (1) Compute and plot the future dynamics of the **jointly suitable habitat** ($\text{Butterfly} \cap [\text{Host 1} \cup \text{Host 2}]$) across all nine future scenarios, not just under the current climate (Figure 7). (2) Remove the unsupported claim that *L. c. chinensis* will switch its host preference from *A. forbesii* to *A. sieboldii*.
* **HOW:** Apply the raster intersection performed in Figure 7 to all nine future scenario rasters to quantify how much of the butterfly's projected $1,243,670\text{ km}^2$ future range actually contains at least one host plant. Revise the Abstract (lines 8, 12), Results (line 230), Discussion Section 4.3 (lines 258–260), and Conclusion (line 278) to state accurately that *L. c. chinensis* maintains high niche overlap and parallel northwestward range shifts with *A. forbesii* ($D = 0.67–0.75$) and moderate overlap with higher-elevation *A. sieboldii* ($D = 0.53–0.61$), with no statistically significant change in overall niche overlap (`ns` in Table 3).

#### 3. Test a Climate-and-Topography-Only Model to Separate Sampling Bias from Human Influence (`HI`)
* **WHY:** Specimen and citizen-science photo databases (`NSII`, `PPBC`) oversample accessible, human-modified lowland areas near cities and roads, causing SDMs to learn a positive response to intermediate/high `HI` (`20.7%` contribution in *A. forbesii*) and lock future range projections to current human footprint patterns.
* **WHAT:** Inspect and report the direction (response curve) of the `HI` variable, and run a sensitivity ensemble model using only bioclimatic and topographic variables (excluding `HI`) to verify whether future centroid shifts and range size projections are constrained by the static `HI` layer.
* **HOW:** Re-run `Biomod2` without `HI`, compare the resulting current and future suitable areas against the full model, and discuss how human footprint in eastern China acts as a limiting threat rather than a favorable habitat requirement.

#### 4. Correct Mathematical, Numerical, and Editorial Errors in the Text and Tables
* **WHY:** Several equations and numerical statements in the text contain typographical errors or contradict the tables:
  1. **Hellinger's $I$ formula (line 81):** The equation is printed as $I(p_X, p_Y) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_i (\sqrt{p_{X,i}} - \sqrt{p_{Y,i}})^2$, which omits the leading `1 - ` (per Warren et al., 2008, $I = 1 - \frac{1}{2}\sum_i (\sqrt{p_{X,i}} - \sqrt{p_{Y,i}})^2$).
  2. **Line 230 numerical contradiction:** Line 230 states `"although its average I value in the future (I = 0.815) was slightly lower than currently (I = 0.799)"`—$0.815$ is **higher**, not lower, than $0.799$.
  3. **Table 3 footnote (line 228):** States `"under six future climate scenarios"` when Table 3 averages across **nine** future scenarios (3 periods $\times$ 3 SSPs). Also clarify how a "one-sample $t$-test" (line 87) was used to compare future scenarios against the current baseline value.
  4. **Table 2 vs. text (lines 144, 150):** For *L. c. chinensis*, `Bio 9` is `7.2%` in Table 2 but `7.4%` in line 150; for *A. sieboldii*, `Bio 12` is `38.8%` in Table 2 (and line 242) but `38.3%` in line 150.
  5. **Figure numbering cross-references (lines 155, 244, 271):** Line 155 cites `Figure 4A2`, `Figure 5A3`, and `Figure 6A1`, whereas the captions of Figures 4, 5, and 6 label *L. c. chinensis* as `A1–J1` (Fig 4), *A. forbesii* as `A2–J2` (Fig 5), and *A. sieboldii* as `A3–J3` (Fig 6). Lines 244 and 271 cite `Figure 6` for butterfly–host overlap when the overlap map is `Figure 7`.
* **WHAT & HOW:** Fix the Hellinger's $I$ formula on line 81, correct "lower" to "higher" on line 230, change "six" to "nine" in the Table 3 footnote, reconcile the percentage contributions in line 150 with Table 2, and fix the Figure 4–7 cross-references.
