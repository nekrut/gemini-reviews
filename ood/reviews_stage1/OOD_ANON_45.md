### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This cross-sectional resting-state fMRI study examines intrinsic brain network segregation across 10 functional networks (279 nodes derived from an independent 110-subject Washington University dataset) in 21 healthy young adults (age $23.1 \pm 3.4$ years) and 81 community-dwelling older adults categorized by Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB) scores into high physical function ($\text{SPPB} > 10$, $n = 27$, age $73.5 \pm 5.0$) and low physical function ($\text{SPPB} \le 10$, $n = 54$, age $76.0 \pm 7.2$). Using one-way ANCOVAs (controlling for sex), canonical correlation analysis (CCA), and univariate-screened backward stepwise multiple linear regressions, the authors evaluate group and hemispheric laterality differences in network segregation, within-network connectivity, and between-network connectivity, as well as their associations with 400 m self-selected walking speed and NIH Toolbox list-sorting working memory scores. They report that sensorimotor and vestibular network segregation is higher in high-functioning than low-functioning older adults, that there are no Group $\times$ Laterality interactions in overall network segregation, and that multivariate combinations of network segregation scores are associated with walking speed and working memory. Raw data are available only upon request and no analysis code is released.

Defining the 279 network nodes on an independent external dataset (`ds00243`) and using cerebellar-specific SUIT normalization are clear methodological strengths that avoid circular ROI definition. However, the statistical analysis and interpretation suffer from several substantial flaws: (1) an average lag of $81.5 \pm 94.8$ days (over 2.5 months, with SD $>3$ months) between MRI acquisition and behavioral testing; (2) severe and asymmetric missing data in the working memory outcome ($19/54 = 35.2\%$ missing in the low-functioning older adult group), handled in CCA via unspecified "regularization" (`CCA` R package) and via listwise deletion ($N = 76$) in regression without sensitivity checks; (3) high-dimensional overfitting in CCA (correlating 4 function variables with 10 network variables on $N = 81$ or $N = 102$, where the sample canonical correlation is guaranteed to be upwardly biased) paired with univariate p-value screening + stepwise regression without family-wise or FDR correction across the 6–10 networks tested in ANCOVAs and regressions; and (4) a sign-interpretation error in the Discussion (Section 4.3, line 138) that misinterprets the opposite signs of canonical weights for walking speed ($-0.698$) and working memory ($+0.451$) on the first canonical variate as evidence that slower-walking older adults have *better* working memory, contradicting Table 1 (where high-functioning older adults have both faster gait and higher mean working memory: $16.7$ vs. $15.5$). Re-running the analyses with continuous age adjustments, false discovery rate control, proper missing-data handling, and corrected CCA interpretation is feasible and necessary.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Sensorimotor and vestibular network segregation are significantly higher in high-functioning older adults than in low-functioning older adults, driven primarily by preserved within-network connectivity: [Moderately Supported].**  
  The ANCOVA and Holm–Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons within each network (Tables 2–3, Figs. 2 and 4) show lower sensorimotor ($p = 0.017$) and vestibular ($p = 0.046$) segregation in low-functioning vs. high-functioning older adults. However, the omnibus F-tests across the 6 network models (Sensorimotor, Vestibular, DLPFC, ACC, DMN, Visual) were not corrected for multiple testing across networks (where DLPFC $p = 0.036$, Visual $p = 0.038$, and the OA LF vs. HF vestibular contrast $p = 0.046$ are marginal), and the older adult subgroup comparison did not control for continuous chronological age despite the low-functioning group being 2.5 years older on average with a wider age spread ($76.0 \pm 7.2$ vs. $73.5 \pm 5.0$ years, Table 1).

* **Hemispheric laterality of network segregation does not differ across age or physical function groups (no Group $\times$ Laterality interaction): [Well Supported].**  
  In all four bilateral network pairs (sensorimotor, vestibular, DLPFC, ACC), main effects of hemisphere are highly significant ($F(1,98) = 15.79\text{ to }141.86, p < 0.001$), whereas Group $\times$ Laterality interactions for network segregation are uniformly null ($F(2,98) = 0.02\text{ to }0.84, p \ge 0.437$), even though separate within- and between-network connectivity components show modest laterality shifts.

* **Stepwise multiple regressions identify specific lateralized network segregation predictors of walking speed and working memory (Tables 6, 7, and 9): [Fragile].**  
  First, two-stage variable selection—screening 10 networks plus 10 Group $\times$ Network interactions via uncorrected univariate regressions ($p < 0.05$) and then feeding the survivors into backward stepwise selection on the exact same dataset (Section 2.7, line 107)—invalidates nominal standard errors and inflates Type I error (Freedman's paradox). Second, in Table 9 (walking speed in older adults), the authors include the binary `Functional group (HF)` indicator ($p < 0.001$) as a predictor alongside network segregation when predicting 400 m walking speed, even though `Functional group` was defined using the SPPB, which directly includes usual walking speed as one of its three sub-scores—creating circular predictor-outcome overlap. Third, the working memory regression (Table 7, $N = 76$) excludes $35.2\%$ ($19/54$) of the low-functioning older adults due to missing data, and its sole significant term (`Left Sensorimotor x Age group`, $p = 0.045$) would not survive any multiple-comparison correction.

* **Within the older adult cohort, slower walking speed is associated with higher working memory performance and compensatory DLPFC/visual segregation (Section 3.4, line 64; Section 4.3, line 138): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In Section 3.4 (line 64) and Section 4.3 (line 138), the authors observe that in the first canonical variate of the older-adult CCA (Fig. 6A), walking speed has a negative canonical coefficient ($-0.698$), physical function group has a negative coefficient ($-0.396$), and working memory has a positive coefficient ($+0.451$), and conclude: *"Within our older adult group, we found an unexpected dissociation between walking speed and working memory... Specifically, individuals who had slower walking speed had higher working memory"* (line 138). This is a fundamental misinterpretation of canonical correlation coefficients: a linear combination $U_1 = -0.698 Z_{\text{gait}} + 0.451 Z_{\text{WM}} - 0.396 Z_{\text{group}}$ that maximally correlates with a linear combination of 10 brain networks $V_1$ does **not** mean that $Z_{\text{gait}}$ and $Z_{\text{WM}}$ are negatively correlated with each other in the population (in fact, Table 1 shows that the high-functioning group has both faster gait [$1.1$ vs. $0.9\text{ m/s}$] and higher working memory [$16.7$ vs. $15.5$]). Rather, the opposite signs in $U_1$ represent a contrast vector (gait minus working memory, or a suppression effect) extracted to maximize correlation with contrasting motor vs. prefrontal network scores, compounded by the fact that 23 of the 81 older adults (35% of the LF group) had missing working memory scores that were imputed or regularized by an undisclosed method in the `CCA` package.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Correct the CCA Interpretation and Report the Raw Bivariate Correlation Matrix Between Behavioral Outcomes
* **WHY:** Canonical weights ($\mathbf{a}$ in $U = \mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{X}$) are partial regression-like weights that maximize $\text{Corr}(\mathbf{a}^T \mathbf{X}, \mathbf{b}^T \mathbf{Y})$; opposite signs on $a_{\text{gait}}$ and $a_{\text{WM}}$ define a behavioral contrast axis and do not imply that slower walkers have superior working memory. Moreover, the `CCA` R package (`González et al., 2008`) does not natively impute 35% missing outcome data via ridge regularization (`rcc`) unless missing values are pre-imputed.
* **WHAT:** Report the direct bivariate Pearson/Spearman correlation between 400 m walking speed and working memory within the older adult cohort ($n = 57$ with complete data), clarify exactly how the 26 missing working memory values and 2 missing gait values were handled in `CCA`, and revise Section 3.4 (line 64) and Section 4.3 (line 138) to remove the unsupported claim that slower-walking older adults had better working memory.
* **HOW:** Compute `cor.test(gait_speed, working_memory)` in R for the older adults, report Canonical Structure Loadings (correlations between original variables and canonical variates) alongside standardized weights in Figs. 5–6, and re-run the CCA on complete cases ($N = 74$ across all groups; $N = 57$ in older adults) as a sensitivity check.

#### 2. Control for Continuous Age and Scan-to-Behavior Time Lag, and Apply FDR Correction Across Networks
* **WHY:** Categorizing older adults into two SPPB bins leaves residual confounding by chronological age ($76.0 \pm 7.2$ vs. $73.5 \pm 5.0$ years), and performing 6 separate omnibus ANCOVAs (and 20 univariate screening regressions) without multiple-testing correction inflates false positives. Additionally, an average gap of $81.5 \pm 94.8$ days between MRI and behavioral testing introduces state variance.
* **WHAT:** Include continuous age (within the older adult models) and scan-to-behavior interval (in days) as covariates in the older-adult regressions and ANCOVAs, and report Benjamini–Hochberg FDR-adjusted $p$-values across the 6 network omnibus tests in Section 3.1.
* **HOW:** Add `age` and `days_between_scan_and_behavior` to the linear models in R and add an FDR-adjusted $p$-value column to Table 2.

#### 3. Replace Post-Selection Stepwise Regression with Penalized Regression (Lasso/Elastic Net) or Full Enter Models Without Circular SPPB Predictors
* **WHY:** Univariate $p$-value screening followed by backward elimination on the same sample biases $p$-values downward and inflates $R^2$, and including `Functional group (HF)` (derived from SPPB, which measures walking speed) as a predictor of 400 m walking speed in Table 9 is partially circular.
* **WHAT:** Re-estimate the walking speed and working memory models in older adults without `Functional group (HF)` as a covariate (treating continuous 400 m walking speed directly as the outcome of interest predicted by the 10 network segregation scores, age, and sex), using either a single simultaneous multiple regression or cross-validated LASSO/ridge regression.
* **HOW:** Fit `lm(walking_speed ~ sex + age + [10 network scores])` on the $n = 80$ older adults and report the unscreened regression coefficients, standard errors, and adjusted $R^2$ alongside Table 9.
