### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This study models current and future (2050 and 2070 under RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5, averaged across 16 CMIP5 general circulation models at 30-arcsecond [$\sim 1\text{ km}^2$] resolution) habitat suitability in Iran for three medicinal thyme species (*Thymus fedtschenkoi*, *T. pubescens*, and *T. transcaucasicus*) using MaxEnt (`dismo` package in R with 10-fold cross-validation). Using presence records compiled from Iranian herbaria (HSBU, IRAN, TARI), literature, GBIF, and field verification alongside 9 environmental predictors (5 bioclimatic, 1 edaphic [soil organic carbon], and 3 topographic [altitude, slope, solar radiation] screened at $|r| \le 0.70$), the authors report cross-validated AUC values of 0.960 (*T. fedtschenkoi*), 0.940 (*T. pubescens*), and 0.975 (*T. transcaucasicus*). They identify altitude and driest-month precipitation (`bio14`) as the top contributors for *T. fedtschenkoi*, altitude and solar radiation for *T. pubescens*, and soil organic carbon (`Ocr`) and `bio14` for *T. transcaucasicus*, and project net range contractions for all three species across all future scenarios (ranging from $-8.71\%$ to $-29.89\%$ by 2070 under RCP 8.5). Occurrence coordinates and R scripts are not publicly archived ("available with the corresponding author on reasonable request").

The study has several positive elements, including herbarium and field verification of presence records, averaging future projections across a 16-GCM ensemble rather than relying on a single climate model, and ecologically sensible interpretation of montane Irano-Turanian and Hyrcanian habitat requirements. However, the methodology has notable weaknesses that limit its quantitative rigor: (1) the authors never report the exact sample size ($N$) of presence records per species in the text or tables, nor do they perform spatial thinning or background sampling bias correction before running random 10-fold cross-validation; (2) including `Altitude` directly alongside temperature and precipitation variables in climate-change projections holds the `Altitude` contribution ($33.8\%\text{--}47.9\%$ of model gain) static in 2050 and 2070, artificially anchoring species to their current elevation bands and preventing the model from projecting upslope thermal tracking; and (3) MaxEnt was run with default hyperparameters in `dismo` without tuning regularization multipliers or feature classes, and the exact probability threshold used to compute binary range gain/loss in Table 3 is not specified. These limitations can be addressed in a straightforward re-analysis by reporting sample sizes, applying spatial thinning, testing climate models without static altitude, and archiving the occurrence dataset.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Current suitable habitats for *T. fedtschenkoi*, *T. pubescens*, and *T. transcaucasicus* are concentrated in the mountainous regions of northwestern and northern Iran (Alborz and Zagros ranges), with cross-validated $\text{AUC} > 0.94$: [Moderately Supported].**  
  The predicted current distributions (Figure 4) match known floristic records in the Irano-Turanian and Hyrcanian regions. However, the exact number of occurrence points ($N$) for each of the three species is omitted from the Materials and Methods and Results, and no spatial filtering (thinning to 1 km or 5 km) was applied to reduce spatial clustering of herbarium/field collections. When presence points are spatially clustered in northwestern Iran and evaluated against random pseudo-absences drawn across the entire $1.65\text{ million km}^2$ extent of Iran (much of which consists of hyper-arid central deserts, Dasht-e Kavir and Dasht-e Lut), random 10-fold cross-validation yields inflated AUC values ($>0.94$) and overpredicts potential suitability in unsampled northeastern mountains (e.g., *T. pubescens* in North Khorasan, which the authors note in Line 200 is outside the species' actual range).

* **Altitude is the primary environmental factor governing the distribution of *T. fedtschenkoi* (33.8% PC) and *T. pubescens* (47.9% PC), whereas soil organic carbon content (34.6% PC) and driest-month precipitation (29.7% PC) dominate *T. transcaucasicus*: [Fragile].**  
  First, Table 2 reveals a sharp divergence between Percent Contribution (PC, which is path-dependent on the MaxEnt training algorithm) and Permutation Importance (PI, which measures actual model dependence on the final surface): for *T. fedtschenkoi*, `Solar radiation` has by far the highest Permutation Importance ($\text{PI} = 65.6\%$ vs. $14.7\%$ for `Altitude`), for *T. pubescens*, `Precipitation seasonality (bio15)` has the highest Permutation Importance ($\text{PI} = 37.4\%$ vs. $34.2\%$ for `Altitude`), and for *T. transcaucasicus*, `Mean diurnal range (Bio2)` has the highest Permutation Importance ($\text{PI} = 48.7\%$ vs. $5.0\%$ for `Soil organic carbon`). Relying solely on PC in the Abstract and Conclusions while ignoring PI misidentifies the variables on which the final predictions actually depend. Second, in the Discussion (Line 198), the authors state that *T. fedtschenkoi* suitable habitats are distinguished by `"an elevation of 1400–3500 m"`, and in the very next sentence state that Ghelichnia found its suitable altitude was `"from sea level to 1400 m.a.s.l. Our findings are in line with the mentioned studies"`—whereas $1400\text{--}3500\text{ m}$ and $0\text{--}1400\text{ m}$ are non-overlapping elevation zones.

* **Under RCP 4.5 and RCP 8.5 in 2050 and 2070, all three *Thymus* species will experience net habitat contractions (up to $-29.89\%$ for *T. pubescens*, $-21.09\%$ for *T. transcaucasicus*, and $-14.60\%$ for *T. fedtschenkoi* under RCP 8.5 in 2070): [Fragile].**  
  Because static `Altitude` accounts for $18.1\%\text{--}47.9\%$ of PC (and $14.7\%\text{--}34.2\%$ of PI) and static `Solar radiation`, `Slope`, and `Soil organic carbon` account for another $15.9\%\text{--}45.1\%$ of PC (Table 2), between $62.9\%$ and $81.2\%$ of the model's percent contribution comes from static non-climatic variables that cannot change between 2020, 2050, and 2070. Including elevation directly in a climate-change SDM prevents the model from projecting upward elevational range shifts as high mountains warm, because higher elevations have different raw `Altitude` values even when their future temperature matches the species' current thermal niche. In addition, the exact threshold rule (e.g., Maximum Training Sensitivity Plus Specificity vs. Equal Sensitivity and Specificity) used to convert continuous probabilities into binary Gain/Loss percentages in Table 3 is not stated in the Methods.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Report Exact Sample Sizes ($N$), Apply Spatial Thinning, and Specify the Binary Threshold Rule
* **WHY:** Without knowing how many occurrence records exist for *T. fedtschenkoi*, *T. pubescens*, and *T. transcaucasicus*, readers cannot assess model degrees of freedom or overfitting risk. Similarly, binary range change percentages (Table 3) depend strongly on the chosen binarization threshold.
* **WHAT:** Report total and spatially thinned occurrence counts per species, thin points to $\ge 1\text{--}5\text{ km}$ spacing, and state the exact threshold metric and numerical cutoff used for each species.
* **HOW:** Use `spThin` in R to thin records to a minimum distance of $5\text{ km}$ (matching the typical spatial accuracy of herbarium records), report both raw and thinned $N$ per species in Section "Species occurrence data" and Table 2, and report the exact logistic/cloglog threshold values (e.g., `max_sens_spec` from `dismo::evaluate`) used to compute Gain, Loss, and Net Change in Table 3.

#### 2. Remove Static `Altitude` (or Replace with Topographic Roughness/Slope Only) When Projecting Range Shifts Under Climate Change
* **WHY:** In mountainous terrain, `Altitude` acts as a proxy for temperature and atmospheric pressure. When `Altitude` ($33.8\%\text{--}47.9\%$ contribution) is included alongside bioclimatic variables and held constant in 2050/2070, the SDM penalizes upslope dispersal into higher elevations even when future warming makes those elevations thermally optimal.
* **WHAT:** Run a sensitivity comparison excluding `Altitude` (retaining `Slope`, `Solar radiation`, `Ocr`, and temperature/precipitation bioclimatic variables) and compare the projected 2050/2070 range changes and elevational shifts.
* **HOW:** Re-run the correlation screening ($|r| \le 0.70$) including temperature variables (`Bio1` or `Bio10`/`Bio11`) in place of `Altitude`, refit the 10-fold cross-validated MaxEnt models, and compare the resulting 2050 and 2070 Gain/Loss percentages against Table 3.

#### 3. Integrate Both Permutation Importance (PI) and Percent Contribution (PC) in Interpreting Limiting Factors
* **WHY:** Table 2 shows that the variables with the highest Permutation Importance (`Solar` at $65.6\%$ for *T. fedtschenkoi*, `bio15` at $37.4\%$ for *T. pubescens*, and `Bio2` at $48.7\%$ for *T. transcaucasicus*) differ substantially from the variables with the highest Percent Contribution (`Alt` and `Ocr`).
* **WHAT:** Revise the Abstract, Results, and Discussion to discuss both PC and PI, acknowledging the major role of solar radiation, precipitation seasonality (`bio15`), and diurnal temperature range (`Bio2`) revealed by PI.
* **HOW:** Update the Abstract and Section "Environmental variables affecting the distribution of Thymus spp" to report both metrics from Table 2 and include the Jackknife test bar plots in the supplementary or main figures.

#### 4. Tune MaxEnt Regularization and Feature Classes Using `ENMeval`
* **WHY:** Default MaxEnt settings in `dismo` (which enable complex hinge, product, and threshold features depending on sample size) are prone to overfitting, especially when modeling narrow-range mountain endemics across a large country-wide background.
* **WHAT:** Optimize the regularization multiplier ($\text{RM} = 0.5\text{--}4.0$) and feature classes (`L`, `LQ`, `LQH`, `LQHP`) using `ENMeval` with spatial block cross-validation.
* **HOW:** Run `ENMeval::ENMevaluate` on the thinned occurrence points for each *Thymus* species using `partition = "block"`, select the model with $\Delta\text{AICc} = 0$ (or minimum 10% test omission rate), and report the selected RM and feature class alongside the cross-validated test AUC and Continuous Boyce Index (CBI).
