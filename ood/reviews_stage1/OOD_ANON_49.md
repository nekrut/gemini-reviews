### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This epidemiological and geoecological study characterizes 100 Rift Valley fever (RVF) disease events (91 small clusters with $\le 20$ confirmed cases and 9 larger outbreaks) recorded across Kenya (2008–2022, $n = 41$ events / $35$ small clusters), Tanzania (2008–2022, $n = 15$ events / $12$ small clusters), and Uganda (2016–2022, $n = 44$ events / $44$ small clusters). Using presence-only case counts (human and livestock combined) modeled via negative binomial regression against 45 candidate demographic, livestock, soil, elevation, land-use, and lagged climatic/NDVI variables, alongside 40-year gridded climate trend analyses (CHIRPS daily precipitation and CRU TS v4.07 monthly temperature, 1981–2022), the authors report that 67% of small RVF clusters occurred in highlands ($\ge 1,000\text{ m}$), 35% occurred in previously unaffected areas, and cluster case counts were positively associated with temperature 3 months prior ($\text{IRR} = 1.20$, 95% CI $1.11\text{--}1.29$) and positive NDVI change 1 month prior ($\text{IRR} = 1.93$, 95% CI $1.01\text{--}3.71$). Data are available only upon request and no analysis code is provided.

Documenting the geographic distribution of inter-epidemic and highland RVF clusters across East Africa is important for regional surveillance. However, the statistical modeling and causal attribution to long-term climate change suffer from several major methodological flaws: (1) the authors conflate **event occurrence (presence vs. absence)** with **cluster case count given presence ($Y \ge 1$)**—because the dataset is strictly presence-only ($N = 100$ confirmed RVF events with zero unaffected control locations or non-outbreak time periods, Section "Collection of RVF disease data", line 81), fitting a negative binomial regression on the number of cases within confirmed clusters ($n = 76$ complete events in Table 2) estimates predictors of *cluster size among detected clusters*, NOT the incidence rate ratio of *RVF occurrence* as claimed throughout the Abstract, Results, and Discussion; (2) severe surveillance ascertainment bias in Uganda, where national viral hemorrhagic fever surveillance and PCR/IgM testing began in 2016 (so data were only collected for 2016–2022 in Uganda vs. 2008–2022 in Kenya and Tanzania, line 83), which directly drives the apparent post-2016 surge in highland clusters (93% of Ugandan clusters are at $\ge 1,000\text{ m}$ because most of southwestern/central Uganda lies above $1,000\text{ m}$); and (3) numerous mathematical inconsistencies between the text, Abstract, and Table 2 (including an algebraically malformed offset definition, mismatched lag months in the Abstract vs. Table 2, and confidence intervals/p-values in Table 2 that violate standard Wald relationships). Re-analyzing the data with proper spatio-temporal control locations (case-control or Poisson point-process / space-time panel modeling) and correcting the Table 2 inconsistencies are essential.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Recent RVF disease events in East Africa (2008–2022) include a high proportion of small clusters ($\le 20$ cases) occurring during or shortly after rainy seasons (82%) and in highland agricultural ecosystems ($\ge 1,000\text{ m}$): [Well Supported].**  
  The descriptive epidemiology of the 91 confirmed small clusters across Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania (Table 1, Figs. 1–2) clearly establishes that inter-epidemic viral transmission and localized clinical spillovers occur in highland semi-intensive and intensive livestock systems with clay-rich soils and natural or anthropogenic water pooling, rather than being restricted solely to lowland pastoralist floodplains during major ENSO events.

* **Multivariate negative binomial regression demonstrates that a $1^\circ\text{C}$ temperature increase and a 1-unit NDVI increase are associated with increased RVF occurrence (IRRs of 1.20 and 1.93, Table 2): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  First, the study contains **only confirmed RVF disease events** (presence-only data, line 81; $n = 76$ events in Table 2 after dropping missing values). A negative binomial regression fit exclusively to positive disease events ($Y_i = \text{human} + \text{livestock cases} \ge 1$) without any zero-case control districts or non-outbreak months cannot estimate the probability or incidence rate of **RVF occurrence**; it only models why some detected clusters had 8 cases instead of 2 cases (and even then, using a standard negative binomial likelihood instead of a zero-truncated negative binomial likelihood on $Y_i \ge 1$ is misspecified). Second, the offset term is defined in Section "Data analysis" (line 98) as *"total host density minus number of hosts per $\text{km}^2$"*—which is a tautological zero since host density *is* the number of hosts per $\text{km}^2$ (likely a garbled description of $\log(\text{total host population})$ or $\log(\text{host density})$). Third, a "1-unit increase in NDVI" (`First month prior NDVI` $\text{IRR} = 9.88$ or `Positive NDVI change` $\text{IRR} = 1.93$) is physically impossible or mislabeled: satellite NDVI is bounded on $[-1, +1]$ and across East African vegetated land spans roughly $0.2$ to $0.8$, so a 1-unit continuous change exceeds the entire dynamic range of terrestrial vegetation, whereas in Table 1 (`No. of RVF events with positive NDVI change 1 month prior: Yes/No`), `Positive NDVI change` is actually a **binary indicator variable (Yes vs. No)**, not a 1-unit continuous NDVI increase as stated in the Abstract (line 21). Fourth, the Abstract (line 21) states that both temperature and NDVI were measured *"one months prior"*, whereas Table 2 (line 174, 190) states temperature was measured in the **third month prior**. Fifth, the coefficients, standard errors, 95% CIs, and $p$-values in Table 2 are mathematically inconsistent with one another:
  - For `Tanzania`: $\beta = 0.90, \text{SE} = 0.42 \implies \exp(0.90 \pm 1.96 \times 0.42) = [1.08, 5.60]$, not $[1.07, 5.92]$.
  - For `First month prior NDVI`: $\beta = 2.29, \text{SE} = 1.05 \implies z = 2.29/1.05 = 2.181, p = 0.029$, yet the 95% CI is reported as $[0.85, 119.52]$ (which crosses 1.0, corresponding to $\text{SE} \approx 1.26, p = 0.07$), while $p$ is printed as `0.03` without an asterisk.
  - For `Positive NDVI change`: $\beta = 0.66, \text{SE} = 0.30 \implies z = 2.20, p = 0.028$ (printed as `0.03*`), whereas $\exp(0.66 \pm 1.96 \times 0.30) = [1.07, 3.48]$, not $[1.01, 3.71]$.

* **Long-term decadal warming and wetting trends in the highlands of Uganda and Kenya (1981–2022) drove the post-2016 surge in highland RVF clusters: [Overstated].**  
  In Section "Collection of RVF disease data" (line 83), the authors state that RVF data were collected from **2008 to 2022 in Kenya and Tanzania**, but only from **2016 to 2022 in Uganda** (when Uganda established enhanced viral hemorrhagic fever diagnostic surveillance). Because Uganda accounts for 44 of the 100 events (48% of all small clusters) and 93% of Ugandan clusters are at elevations $\ge 1,000\text{ m}$ (Table 1, since southwestern and central Uganda sit on a $>1,000\text{ m}$ plateau), pooling 2008–2015 (Kenya/Tanzania only) with 2016–2022 (Kenya/Tanzania + Uganda) creates an artificial jump in highland clusters after 2016. Furthermore, in the hotspot climate analysis (Section "Long-term climate variability in areas with a high density of RVF clusters", line 230; Fig. 4H–K), the authors themselves note that the 40-year rainfall trends in Hotspot Area A (Uganda) and Hotspot Area B (Kenya) are **not statistically significant**, and Visual/descriptive overlay of 7–15 years of surveillance counts onto 40-year linear temperature trends (Fig. 5) does not control for secular increases in diagnostic PCR/IgM testing capacity.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Add Uninfected Control Locations/Time Periods or Reframe Table 2 as a Zero-Truncated Cluster-Size Model
* **WHY:** Regressing case counts within 76 confirmed RVF events ($Y_i \ge 1$) without any negative controls ($Y = 0$) does not estimate risk factors for **RVF occurrence**; it only estimates covariates of cluster size among positive events, and ignores truncation at zero.
* **WHAT:** Either (a) construct a district-by-month space-time panel (or matched case-control dataset sampling 4–5 unaffected control districts/months per RVF event across Kenya, Uganda, and Tanzania) and fit a logistic or zero-inflated negative binomial regression to model true **occurrence** ($Y > 0$ vs. $Y = 0$), or (b) fit a zero-truncated negative binomial regression (`vglm(..., posnegbinomial)` in R) on the positive events and explicitly reframe Table 2 throughout the Abstract, Results, and Discussion as modeling *cluster size conditional on an outbreak occurring* rather than occurrence.
* **HOW:** Extract CHIRPS rainfall, CRU/CHIRTS temperature, MODIS NDVI, FAO soil, and elevation values for random control district-months (matched or stratified by country) using the existing GIS layers, fit a case-control logistic regression for event occurrence alongside the zero-truncated count model for cluster size, and update Table 2.

#### 2. Stratify Temporal Trend Analyses by Country and Surveillance Period (2008–2022 vs. 2016–2022)
* **WHY:** Including Uganda only from 2016 onward (44 clusters, 93% highland) mechanically inflates the post-2016 proportion of highland and "new area" clusters in East Africa.
* **WHAT:** Report temporal trends in highland vs. lowland clusters separately for Kenya/Tanzania (which have continuous 2008–2022 coverage) and Uganda (2016–2022), and explicitly test whether the proportion of highland clusters in Kenya alone increased significantly between 2008–2014 and 2015–2022.
* **HOW:** Tabulate the Kenyan and Tanzanian cluster counts and median elevations by multi-year period (2008–2012, 2013–2017, 2018–2022) and add a sensitivity analysis restricting the pooled East African time-series to 2016–2022 across all three countries.

#### 3. Correct the Offset Definition, Variable Coding, and Numerical Inconsistencies in Table 2 and Text
* **WHY:** Multiple numbers and variable definitions conflict across the Abstract, Methods, Results, Table 1, and Table 2.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  1. **Offset definition (line 98):** Replace *"with an offset term (total host density minus number of hosts per km 2 )"* with the exact mathematical expression used in the regression (e.g., $\log(\text{human population} + \text{livestock population})$ or $\log(\text{host density per km}^2)$).
  2. **`Positive NDVI change` vs. `First month prior NDVI` (Abstract line 21, Results line 154, Table 2 lines 176–177):** Clarify in the Abstract and Results that $\text{IRR} = 1.93$ corresponds to the binary indicator `Positive NDVI change (Yes vs. No)` (Table 1), whereas continuous `First month prior NDVI` had $\beta = 2.29$ ($\text{IRR} = 9.88$, 95% CI $0.85\text{--}119.52$, non-significant since CI includes 1.0; remove $p = 0.03$ or recompute exact Wald/profile-likelihood CI and $p$-value).For continuous NDVI ($[0, 1]$), report the IRR per **0.1-unit** increase ($\exp(0.229) = 1.26$) rather than a 1.0-unit increase.
  3. **Temperature lag (Abstract line 21 vs. Table 2 line 174):** Fix the Abstract (*"A 1°C increase in temperature and a 1-unit increase in NDVI, one months prior"*) to state **three months prior** for temperature, matching Table 2 and line 154.
  4. **Median human cases (Abstract line 21 vs. Results line 110 vs. Table 1 line 121):** Abstract line 21 and Table 1 Total column (line 121) state a median of **1 human case** (IQR 1–3) and **3 livestock cases** (IQR 2–7 in Abstract, 1–7 in Table 1), whereas Results line 110 states *"a median of 3 human cases (IQR, 1–3)"*. Reconcile these numbers.
  5. **Log-likelihood label in Table 2 (line 185):** For $\text{AIC} = 423.69$ with $k = 10$ parameters ($9$ coefficients $+ \theta$), $-2 \log L = \text{AIC} - 2k = 403.69$, so $2 \times \log L = -403.69$ (as printed). Clarify the label as `2 × log-likelihood` or `-2 × log-likelihood = 403.69`.
