### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This study applies an `ENMeval`-optimized MaxEnt ecological niche modeling framework to predict current and future (2040s under SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5 from a single climate model, BCC-CSM2-MR) habitat suitability in Yunnan Province, China, for the common tiger butterfly (*Danaus genutia*, 37 spatially thinned occurrence points) and one of its larval host plants (*Cynanchum annularium*, 27 spatially thinned points). The authors compare four model configurations: M-I (host plant with 6 abiotic predictors), M-II (butterfly with 9 abiotic predictors), M-III (butterfly with 2 biotic predictors: vegetation type and the M-I host-plant suitability raster), and M-IV (butterfly with 6 abiotic predictors plus the host-plant suitability raster). Finding that all models achieve $\text{AUC} > 0.92$ and that the host-plant raster accounts for 81.3% of percent contribution (and road proximity LRDL accounts for 51.6% of permutation importance) in M-IV, the authors project that suitable habitat for *D. genutia* in Yunnan will expand northward from $10.494 \times 10^4\text{ km}^2$ currently to $11.905\text{--}15.291 \times 10^4\text{ km}^2$ by 2040. Occurrence records and GIS layers are not publicly archived ("available on request from the authors").

While incorporating biotic host-plant interactions and using `ENMeval` checkerboard cross-validation to tune MaxEnt regularization and feature classes are sound conceptual choices, the study's future climate projections and predictor importance rankings suffer from three major methodological flaws: (1) although Model M-IV derives 81.3% of its predictive contribution from the host-plant suitability layer (M-I), the authors do not clarify whether M-I was dynamically re-projected under each 2040s SSP climate scenario before running M-IV, and simultaneously keep human infrastructure (roads LRDL, which has the highest permutation importance at 51.6%, and facility points RFCP) static, where the strong dependence on road proximity is a classic symptom of unaddressed roadside opportunistic sampling bias in iNaturalist/GBIF citizen-science records (74 of 87 *D. genutia* points); (2) the Minimum Training Presence (MTP / "lowest presence threshold") logistic threshold used to define "suitable habitat" is extremely permissive, classifying 96.35% of the butterfly's total suitable range ($10.111 \times 10^4\text{ km}^2$ out of $10.494 \times 10^4\text{ km}^2$, Table 3) as "lowly suitable" (between MTP and 0.4), so that the claimed 2040s range expansion is driven almost entirely by marginal low-suitability pixels; and (3) tables and text confuse Model M-I and Model M-II in multiple places (e.g., Table 2 has two columns labeled `M-I (6)` and `M-I (9)`, and Table 4 and Section 3.3 state that Model M-IV used the predictor `M-II` rather than `M-I`). These issues can be resolved by correcting roadside sampling bias, re-projecting the dynamic host-plant layer across multiple GCMs, and fixing the model label inconsistencies.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Under current climatic conditions, highly suitable habitats for both *D. genutia* and *C. annularium* are concentrated in the dry-hot Yuanjiang River Valley and southern Xishuangbanna, driven primarily by annual mean temperature (Bio1, optimal 19.8–24 °C) and annual precipitation (Bio12): [Moderately Supported].**  
  The bioclimatic optima for *D. genutia* ($19.8\text{--}22\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) and *C. annularium* ($22\text{--}24\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) align well with published thermal rearing experiments for *D. genutia* larvae ($20.0\text{--}27.5\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) and the known biogeography of the Yuanjiang dry-hot valley. However, the sample sizes after 1-km thinning are small ($n = 37$ for *D. genutia*, $n = 27$ for *C. annularium*), and 27 of the 28 *C. annularium* points came from the authors' own field surveys (likely concentrated along accessible valley transects), whereas 74 of 87 *D. genutia* points came from GBIF/iNaturalist citizen science records, creating asymmetric sampling biases between the two species.

* **In Model M-IV, the host plant *C. annularium* contributes 81.3% of model gain while road proximity (LRDL) has the highest permutation importance (51.6%): [Fragile].**  
  First, there is a major labeling contradiction in Section 3.3 (Line 182) and Table 4 (Line 199): both state that the dominant biotic predictor in Model M-IV is `M-II` ("the contribution of the potential distribution of *C. annularium* (from M-II) was markedly high at 81.3%"), whereas `M-II` is the abiotic model for *D. genutia* itself and `M-I` is the host plant model (if `M-II` was inadvertently fed into `M-IV`, the model would be completely circular). Second, even assuming `M-I` was used, `M-I` is itself 85.8% determined by `Bio1` (Annual Mean Temperature, Table 4). Feeding a raster (`M-I`) that is a smooth monotonic transformation of `Bio1` into `M-IV` alongside `Bio1` creates severe collinearity, causing MaxEnt to assign 81.3% path-dependent percent contribution to the composite `M-I` raster while suppressing `Bio1` to 1.4%. Third, the 51.6% permutation importance of roads (`LRDL`) is an artifact of citizen-science roadside collection bias (85% of butterfly records came from GBIF/iNaturalist) rather than a biological requirement of tiger butterflies for paved roads.

* **By 2040, suitable habitats for *D. genutia* in Yunnan will expand northward across all four SSP scenarios (up to $+4.797 \times 10^4\text{ km}^2$ under SSP5-8.5): [Fragile].**  
  This projection rests on a single climate model (BCC-CSM2-MR) downscaled from 2.5 arc-minutes ($\sim 4.5\text{ km}$) to 30 arc-seconds ($\sim 1\text{ km}$) via nearest-neighbor assignment (which creates blocky 4.5-km stair-step artifacts instead of bilinear or local lapse-rate interpolation). Furthermore, the Methods do not specify whether Model M-I (*C. annularium*) was first projected to 2040 under each SSP scenario and then used to update the biotic layer in Model M-IV, or whether the current M-I layer was held static. Finally, Table 5 shows that 96% of the total "suitable area" ($10.111 \times 10^4\text{ km}^2$ currently; $14.663 \times 10^4\text{ km}^2$ under SSP5-8.5) consists of "lowly suitable area" defined by the Minimum Training Presence (MTP) threshold—a threshold determined by the single most extreme/outlier occurrence record in the dataset.

* **Depletion of *C. annularium* is the critical biological factor driving recent wild declines of *D. genutia*: [Overstated].**  
  While *C. annularium* is an important local host plant in the Yuanjiang Valley, *Danaus genutia* is a widespread, polyphagous milkweed butterfly across South and Southeast Asia that utilizes numerous Asclepiadoideae host genera (*Cynanchum*, *Asclepias*, *Ceropegia*, *Graphistemma*, *Marsdenia*, *Raphistemma*, *Tylophora*), including *Cynanchum corymbosum* as the authors acknowledge in the Discussion (Line 270). Modeling *D. genutia* across all of Yunnan as strictly constrained by a single local host plant species (*C. annularium*, sampled at only 27 points) underestimates its trophic niche breadth outside the Yuanjiang Valley.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Correct for Roadside Sampling Bias and Remove Anthropogenic Infrastructure Artifacts
* **WHY:** In Model M-IV, road proximity (`LRDL`) has by far the highest permutation importance ($51.6\%$, compared to $26.7\%$ for the host plant and $4.8\%$ for temperature, Table 4). This indicates that the model is predicting where citizen scientists and surveyors drive along roads in Yunnan rather than the biological niche of *D. genutia*.
* **WHAT:** Control for spatial sampling effort using a target-group background bias file and remove `LRDL` (roads) and `RFCP` (facility points) as direct predictors, or explicitly separate sampling effort from biological suitability.
* **HOW:** Download all Lepidoptera occurrence records across Yunnan from GBIF/iNaturalist to construct a 2D Gaussian kernel density raster representing observer effort, and pass this raster as a `biasfile` to MaxEnt/ENMeval during background point sampling. Re-run Models M-II and M-IV without `LRDL` and `RFCP` to obtain unbiased bioclimatic and host-plant response curves.

#### 2. Clarify Dynamic Host-Plant Projection Under 2040s Scenarios and Use Multi-GCM Ensembles with Bilinear Interpolation
* **WHY:** Using nearest-neighbor resampling to convert 2.5-arcminute ($\sim 4.5\text{ km}$) future climate layers to 30-arcsecond ($\sim 1\text{ km}$) resolution introduces artificial square grid blocks, and relying on a single GCM (BCC-CSM2-MR) ignores inter-model climate uncertainty. Moreover, projecting M-IV into the 2040s requires projecting M-I (*C. annularium*) into the 2040s first.
* **WHAT:** Download native 30-arcsecond WorldClim v2.1 CMIP6 projections across at least 3–5 GCMs (e.g., BCC-CSM2-MR, MRI-ESM2-0, IPSL-CM6A-LR, MIROC6) and explicitly document the two-stage future projection of M-I into M-IV.
* **HOW:** Obtain the 30-arcsecond CMIP6 bioclimatic rasters directly from WorldClim v2.1 (eliminating the need for 2.5-arcminute resampling). For each GCM and SSP scenario, first project Model M-I (*C. annularium*) to 2040, and then use the resulting 2040 host-plant suitability raster as the biotic input layer to project Model M-IV (*D. genutia*). Report the ensemble mean and standard deviation of suitable habitat areas across GCMs in Table 5.

#### 3. Replace the Minimum Training Presence (MTP) Threshold with 10th Percentile Training Presence or MTSPS
* **WHY:** Using the "lowest presence threshold" (MTP) means that the suitability cutoff is dictated by the single least-suitable outlier point among the 37 butterfly records, resulting in $96.35\%$ of the "suitable" area falling in the "lowly suitable" class ($\text{MTP}\text{--}0.4$, Table 3), where actual occurrence probability is near zero.
* **WHAT:** Use the 10th Percentile Training Presence or Maximum Training Sensitivity Plus Specificity (MTSPS) threshold to define the binary suitability boundary.
* **HOW:** Extract the `10 percentile training presence Logistic threshold` or `Maximum training sensitivity plus specificity Logistic threshold` from the MaxEnt output summary, reclassify the rasters in ArcGIS (`0 to threshold` = Unsuitable), and update Tables 3 and 5.

#### 4. Resolve Contradictory Model Labels (`M-I` vs. `M-II`) and Typographical Errors
* **WHY:** Confusing `M-I` (the host plant model) and `M-II` (the butterfly abiotic model) in the text and tables makes it unclear whether Model M-IV was accidentally trained using the butterfly's own abiotic prediction (`M-II`) as an input predictor.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  * **Table 2 (Line 73):** Change the second column header from `M-I (9)` to `M-II (9)`.
  * **Section 3.3 (Line 182) & Table 4 (Line 199):** Verify whether the biotic predictor included in Model M-IV was indeed the output of `M-I` (*C. annularium*) as stated in Table 1 and Figure 6, and correct `"the potential distribution of C. annularium (from M-II)"` in Line 182 and `"M-II"` in Table 4 (Line 199) to `"M-I"`.
  * **Abstract (Line 8):** Correct the genus spelling `"Cynanchun annularium"` to `"Cynanchum annularium"`.
  * **Data Availability:** Provide the 37 *D. genutia* and 27 *C. annularium* thinned coordinate records as a supplementary CSV table.
