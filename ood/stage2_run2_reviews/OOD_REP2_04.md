### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This cross-sectional neuroimaging study examines resting-state fMRI brain network segregation across 10 functional networks (279 nodes defined on an independent WashU-120 cohort, combining whole-brain MNI and SUIT cerebellar parcellations) in 21 young adults, 27 high-functioning older adults (Short Physical Performance Battery $[\text{SPPB}] > 10$), and 54 low-functioning older adults ($\text{SPPB} \le 10$). Using mixed ANCOVAs, canonical correlation analysis (CCA), and two-stage stepwise linear regressions, the authors report that sensorimotor and vestibular network segregation are preserved in high-functioning older adults relative to low-functioning older adults, that age-related reductions in segregation are driven primarily by lower within-network connectivity rather than elevated between-network connectivity, and that network segregation exhibits multivariate associations with 400-m walking speed and NIH Toolbox list-sorting working memory. ROI coordinates are provided in Supplementary Table 1, while raw neuroimaging and behavioral data are available only upon request.

Defining functional network nodes on an external dataset (`ds00243`), applying dedicated cerebellar normalization (CERES/SUIT), and stratifying older adults by objective physical function are clear methodological strengths. However, the statistical analyses supporting Aim 2 (brain–behavior associations) suffer from four substantive flaws: (1) $35.2\%$ ($19/54$) of low-functioning older adults are missing working memory data, and passing this incomplete matrix into the `CCA` package distortingly drives the reported "unexpected dissociation" in which slower walkers appear to have higher working memory; (2) the older-adult walking speed regression (Table 9) and CCA (Table 8) include `Physical function group` (defined via SPPB, which directly incorporates walking speed) as a predictor alongside walking speed; (3) predictors and interactions in Tables 6–9 were selected via unadjusted two-stage univariate screening followed by backward stepwise selection, and the abstract reports non-significant terms ($p = 0.148$ and $p = 0.114$) as positive associations; and (4) the working memory regression (Table 7) fits an uncentered interaction where the apparent model fit ($R^2 = 0.33$) is driven by the main effect of age rather than network segregation ($\Delta R^2 \approx 0.04, p = 0.045$ uncorrected). All of these issues can be resolved through computational re-analysis of the existing dataset.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Sensorimotor and vestibular network segregation are higher in high-functioning older adults than in low-functioning older adults, driven primarily by within-network connectivity: [Well Supported].**  
  The Holm-Bonferroni corrected ANCOVAs (Tables 2–4) clearly show that high-functioning older adults maintain sensorimotor segregation comparable to young adults ($p = 0.564$) and significantly higher than low-functioning older adults ($p = 0.017$), as well as higher vestibular segregation ($p = 0.046$). Decomposing the segregation ratio $(Z_w - Z_b)/Z_w$ into within-network ($Z_w$) and between-network ($Z_b$) components usefully demonstrates that younger and high-functioning older adults have higher $Z_w$ rather than lower $Z_b$.

* **Hemispheric laterality of network segregation does not differ across age or physical function groups: [Well Supported].**  
  While significant main effects of hemisphere exist across the bilateral networks, none of the `Group x Laterality` interactions for network segregation approach significance ($p = 0.437$ to $0.983$). The authors appropriately note in Section 3.2 that within- and between-network connectivity show opposing laterality interactions that cancel out in the composite segregation ratio.

* **In older adults, canonical correlation analysis reveals a dissociation where slower walking speed is associated with higher working memory and distinct network segregation profiles: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In Table 1, low-functioning older adults have *both* slower walking speed ($0.9 \pm 0.2$ vs. $1.1 \pm 0.2\text{ m/s}$) and *lower* mean working memory ($15.5 \pm 3.3$ vs. $16.7 \pm 1.7$) than high-functioning older adults. However, $19/54$ ($35.2\%$) of low-functioning older adults and $4/27$ ($14.8\%$) of high-functioning older adults were missing working memory scores (Table 1 caption). Despite 26 total participants missing working memory and 2 missing walking speed, the CCA degrees of freedom in Table 5 ($df_2 = 91$ for the 4th root, corresponding to $N = 102$) and Table 8 ($df_2 = 70$, corresponding to $N = 81$) show that all participants were retained via the `CCA` package's missing-data handling (which replaces missing entries with column means/zeros on standardized variables). Imputing the grand mean (inflated by young and high-functioning adults) for over one-third of the low-functioning group, while simultaneously entering `Physical function group` and `Walking speed` into the same canonical variate, induces an artificial contrast between walking speed ($-0.698$) and working memory ($+0.451$) that is contradicted by the univariate data (Supplementary Fig. 4, where zero networks correlate with working memory in older adults).

* **Higher left sensorimotor, left vestibular, right ACC, and left ACC $\times$ age interaction segregation are associated with faster walking speed, and left sensorimotor $\times$ age interaction is associated with working memory: [Overstated].**  
  First, the Abstract states that "Higher left sensorimotor, left vestibular, right anterior cingulate cortex, and interaction of left anterior cingulate cortex network segregation and age groups were associated with faster walking speed," directly contradicting Table 6 and Section 3.3 (line 24), where `Left Vestibular` ($t = 1.46, p = 0.148$) and `Right ACC` ($t = 1.59, p = 0.114$) are statistically non-significant. Second, in the older-adult walking speed regression (Table 9), `Functional group (HF)` is included as a covariate ($t = 3.98, p < 0.001$). Because the HF/LF group split was defined by SPPB—which includes usual gait speed as one of its three subtests—conditioning `Walking speed` on `Functional group` removes the primary between-group mobility variance and risks collider/collinearity bias. Third, in Table 7 (working memory), the interaction `Left Sensorimotor x Age group` has an unadjusted $p = 0.045$ after screening 10 networks and their interactions (which would not survive correction for 10 comparisons), and because `Left Sensorimotor` was not mean-centered prior to forming the product term, the main effect of `Age group` appears negative and non-significant ($\beta = -5.00, p = 0.272$) despite young adults scoring 4.2 points higher than older adults.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Re-Run CCA on Complete Cases (or Multiple Imputation) and Remove Redundant Group Indicators
* **WHY:** Retaining 26 participants with missing working memory scores via zero/mean replacement in `CCA` distorts the cross-covariance structure, especially when $35.2\%$ of the low-functioning group is missing working memory. Moreover, including both `Physical function group` (derived from SPPB gait/balance/chair-stand) and `Walking speed` in the same behavioral block creates severe redundancy.
* **WHAT:** Re-estimate the CCAs on the complete-case subset ($n = 75$ across all groups; $n = 57$ in older adults) or use Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (`mice`) within age/function strata, and remove the redundant binary group indicator from the continuous behavioral variable set.
* **HOW:** Filter the dataset to participants with complete behavioral observations (`na.omit`), run `candisc::cancor` (or permutation-tested `CCP::p.perm`) between the continuous functional outcomes (`Walking speed`, `Working memory`, adjusted for `Sex` and `Age`) and the 10 network segregation scores, and verify whether the opposite-sign loading between walking speed and working memory persists.

#### 2. Remove SPPB Functional Group as a Predictor of Walking Speed in Table 9 and Mean-Center Interaction Terms
* **WHY:** In Table 9, predicting `Walking speed` from `Functional group (HF)` predicts gait speed from a composite clinical score that already contains gait speed. In Tables 6 and 7, forming interaction terms (`Network x Age group`) without mean-centering the continuous network segregation score makes the main effect of `Age group` uninterpretable (evaluated at $\text{Segregation} = 0$) and inflates collinearity ($\beta_{\text{std}} = 1.24$ in Table 7).
* **WHAT:** Re-fit the older-adult walking speed regression (Table 9) without `Functional group (HF)` on the right-hand side (or control for continuous `Age` and `Sex` instead), mean-center all continuous network segregation variables before computing interaction terms in Tables 6 and 7, and report Benjamini-Hochberg FDR-adjusted $p$-values across the 10 candidate networks.
* **HOW:** Subtract the sample mean from each network's segregation score ($S_{ij}^* = S_{ij} - \bar{S}_j$), re-fit the linear models in R (`lm(walking_speed ~ Sex + Age + ...)`), and report partial $R^2$ for each predictor alongside the overall model $R^2$.

#### 3. Account for the Scan-to-Behavior Time Lag as a Sensitivity Check
* **WHY:** Section 4.4 notes an average lag of $81.5 \pm 94.8\text{ days}$ between the MRI scan and behavioral assessments due to study scheduling and COVID-19 disruptions. A standard deviation of $95\text{ days}$ implies that some older adults were tested $6$–$12\text{ months}$ apart, over which physical function and gait speed can change substantially in low-functioning older adults.
* **WHAT:** Include the absolute number of days between MRI acquisition and behavioral testing ($\Delta t_{\text{days}}$) as a covariate in a sensitivity analysis, or verify that results hold when restricting to participants tested within $\le 90\text{ days}$.
* **HOW:** Add `abs(scan_date - behavior_date)` as a nuisance covariate in the linear regression models of Tables 6, 7, and 9 and report whether any network segregation coefficients change by $>10\%$.

#### 4. Correct Textual Contradictions, Methodological Omissions, and Typos
* **WHY:** Discrepancies between the Abstract and Table 6, an inverted hypothesis statement in the Discussion, and an incomplete ANCOVA description in Methods reduce clarity.
* **WHAT & HOW:** Fix the following specific items:
  1. **Abstract (line 8):** Remove `left vestibular` ($p = 0.148$) and `right anterior cingulate cortex` ($p = 0.114$) from the list of predictors significantly associated with faster walking speed in the age-group model so the Abstract matches Table 6 and Section 3.3 (line 24).
  2. **Methods Section 2.7 (line 103):** Update the statistical description from "one-way ANCOVA" to a two-way mixed-effects ANCOVA (`Group` [between-subject, 3 levels] $\times$ `Laterality` [within-subject, 2 levels: Left vs. Right], controlling for `Sex`) to match the $F(1, 98)$ laterality and $F(2, 98)$ interaction tests reported in Sections 3.1 and 3.2.
  3. **Discussion Section 4.1 (line 118):** Correct the inverted sentence "We expected within-network connectivity to be higher and between-network connectivity to be lower in older adults compared to younger adults" to state "...lower within-network connectivity and higher between-network connectivity in older adults compared to younger adults" (matching the Introduction, line 19).
  4. **Results Section 3.3 (line 24 of second part):** Fix the missing decimal point in `r 2 = 360` to read `r 2 = 0.360`.
