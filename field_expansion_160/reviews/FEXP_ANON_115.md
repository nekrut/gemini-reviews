## 2. Executive Summary & Editorial Verdict

This study surveys 1,475 students at a Chinese forestry university to examine demographic, academic, and psychosocial correlates of attitude toward biodiversity conservation ("very important" vs. lower, 68.68%) and willingness to participate in conservation activities ("willing" vs. lower, 84.88%). Using multivariate logistic regression and path analysis, the authors report that majoring in agriculture/forestry and higher perceived social support (measured via the 40-item Child and Adolescent Social Support Scale, CASSS) are positively associated with both attitude and willingness, that general social support mediates 3.94% of the association between attitude and willingness, and that sex, ethnicity, and family residence modify this mediation (reporting `0%` mediation in females, ethnic minorities, and rural village students). Data are restricted by institutional ethics committee access (contact email provided), and no analysis scripts or questionnaire files are included.

**Editorial Verdict: Reject / Fundamentally Flawed Statistical and Causal Specification (3.8 / 10.0).** While the sample size ($N = 1,475$) is large and data entry used double-entry verification, the study's core analytical framework suffers from severe methodological, causal, and statistical defects:
1. **Reversed causal specification in the mediation model:** The path model specifies $\text{Biodiversity Attitude} \to \text{Perceived Social Support (from parents, teachers, classmates, friends)} \to \text{Biodiversity Willingness}$. Treating a student's general perceived social support from parents, teachers, and peers as a *causal consequence* of their attitude toward biodiversity conservation in a cross-sectional survey is causally backwards; general social support is a background psychosocial trait (confounder or moderator), not a mediator generated by biodiversity attitudes.
2. **Invalid "effect modification" inference by replacing non-significant estimates with `0%` (Table 3):** In Table 3, whenever a subgroup indirect effect had $p \ge 0.05$ (e.g., females, minorities, village residents), the authors replaced the point estimate with `0%` and declared "prominent effect modification" without conducting any formal interaction test (e.g., multi-group structural invariance or bootstrap difference testing between subgroups).
3. **Sample-size calculation error, unmodeled classroom clustering, and impossible psychometric reporting:** The sample-size calculation in Section 2.1 ($p = 0.50$, $\alpha = 0.05$, error $= 15\% \implies n = 1,112$) is mathematically wrong by an order of magnitude ($d = 0.15$ yields $n = 43$; $n = 1,112$ corresponds to $d \approx 0.0294$). Classroom cluster sampling ($\text{DEFF} = 1.2$) is ignored in all logistic regressions and path analyses. Moreover, Section 2.2 reports Cronbach's $\alpha = 0.87\text{–}0.94$ for "all modules," yet Section 2.3 reveals that biodiversity attitude and willingness were each measured by a **single 5-point Likert question**, for which Cronbach's $\alpha$ is undefined.

### Assessment of Specific Claims

* **Claim 1 (Agriculture/forestry major, course grades $\ge 80$, and perceived social support are the significant independent predictors of biodiversity conservation attitude and willingness): `[Moderately Supported]`.**  
  The directions of association for academic major ($\text{aOR} = 0.52$ and $0.63$) and social support ($\text{aOR} = 1.56$ and $1.47$) are plausible, though standard errors are anti-conservative because classroom cluster correlation was ignored. Furthermore, the Results text (Section 3.2) omits statistically significant associations with father's education ($\text{aOR} = 0.60$, 95% CI: $0.41\text{–}0.88$) and mother's education ($\text{aOR} = 1.47$, 95% CI: $1.10\text{–}1.95$ for attitude; $\text{aOR} = 0.63$, 95% CI: $0.40\text{–}0.99$ for willingness) that are present in Table 2.
* **Claim 2 (Perceived social support from parents, teachers, classmates, and friends significantly mediates 3.94% of the effect of biodiversity attitude on willingness to practice): `[Methodologically Flawed]`.**  
  In a cross-sectional survey, modeling general social support from parents and peers (CASSS) as a downstream mediator caused by a student's single-item biodiversity attitude violates temporal and causal ordering; general social support is a common cause (confounder) or moderator (`Attitude × Social Support`), not a causal mediator.
* **Claim 3 (Sex, race, and family residence significantly modify the mediation by social support, with 0% mediation in females, ethnic minorities, and rural village students): `[Methodologically Flawed]`.**  
  Table 3 artificially truncates non-significant subgroup indirect effects to `0%` and compares nominal significance ($p < 0.05$ in larger subgroups such as Han majority [$n = 1,105$] vs. $p \ge 0.05$ in smaller subgroups such as ethnic minorities [$n = 370$]) without testing whether the indirect effects actually differ across subgroups.
* **Claim 4 (Psychotherapy interventions targeting social support, such as group-based interpersonal psychotherapy, should be considered to boost university students' biodiversity conservation attitude and willingness): `[Overstated]`.**  
  Recommending clinical interpersonal psychotherapy based on a 3.94% cross-sectional correlational overlap in a single forestry university vastly oversteps the empirical evidence.

## 3. Forensic Defect Inventory

### Defect 1: Reversed Causal Ordering in Mediation Analysis and Unspecified Binary Path Estimation [ Pillar 1, Pillar 3 — Critical ]
* **Location:** Section 2.3 (lines 37–47), Section 3.3 (lines 123–132), and Figures 1–2.
* **Forensic Evidence:**
  1. **Causal misspecification:** Mediation requires that the independent variable ($X$, Biodiversity Attitude) causally influences the mediator ($M$, Perceived Social Support from parents, teachers, classmates, and close friends), which in turn influences the outcome ($Y$, Willingness to participate). However, the CASSS measures general perceived frequency of care and assistance from parents, teachers, classmates, and friends. A student's rating of the importance of biodiversity conservation does not cause their parents or childhood/college friends to provide general social support. Instead, social support is an exogenous background variable (or moderator, as suggested by the Indonesian entrepreneurship study cited by the authors themselves in Section 1, line 14!).
  2. **Dichotomization and path model mismatch:** In Section 2.3 (line 47), the authors state that both attitude and willingness were dichotomized into binary `0/1` variables before analysis. However, standard linear path analysis assumes continuous endogenous variables; the manuscript never specifies how binary mediator/outcome paths and the "proportion of mediation" ($3.94\%$) were parameterized (e.g., whether linear probability models, probit WLSMV, or counterfactual causal mediation `mediation` package in R were used), nor are path coefficients, standard errors, or confidence intervals reported in the text or Table 3.
* **Fix:** Reframe the cross-sectional analysis to treat general social support as a **confounder** and test for **moderation** (by adding an `Attitude × Social Support` interaction term in an ordinal logistic regression model using the full 5-point Likert scales rather than arbitrary dichotomization).

### Defect 2: Fallacious Subgroup "Effect Modification" Claims via Truncation of Non-Significant Estimates to `0%` [ Pillar 4, Pillar 5 — Critical ]
* **Location:** Section 3.3 (line 125), Table 3 (lines 134–144), and Discussion (line 154).
* **Forensic Evidence:**
  In Table 3, every cell for `Female` ($n = 810$), `Minorities` ($n = 370$), and `Village` ($n = 1,037$), as well as `SS (Parents)` and `SS (Friends)` among `Agriculture or forestry` majors ($n = 465$), is reported as exact **`0%`**, without point estimates or confidence intervals. As stated in line 125 (*"the mediation was only statistically significant in male, Han majority, and students whose family residences located in townships or cities"*), the authors coded any subgroup indirect effect with $p \ge 0.05$ as `0%` and interpreted the difference between $p < 0.05$ (e.g., $5.17\%$ in Han majority, $n = 1,105$) and $p \ge 0.05$ (in Minorities, $n = 370$) as "prominent effect modification." Comparing subgroup $p$-values against 0.05 without testing the interaction contrast $(\hat{\theta}_{\text{group 1}} - \hat{\theta}_{\text{group 2}})$ is invalid and conflates sample-size differences with effect modification.
* **Fix:** Report the actual point estimates and 95% bootstrap confidence intervals for the indirect effect in every subgroup in Table 3 (never replace non-significant estimates with `0%`), and compute formal two-group difference tests ($p_{\text{interaction}}$) before claiming effect modification.

### Defect 3: Incorrect Sample Size Calculation and Unmodeled Classroom Cluster Sampling [ Pillar 2, Pillar 3, Pillar 4 — Major ]
* **Location:** Section 2.1 (line 24) and Section 2.3 (line 47).
* **Forensic Evidence:**
  1. **Sample size arithmetic error:** Section 2.1 states: *"we set it as the most conservative 50%, statistical significance set as 0.05, acceptable error set was no more than 15%, we yielded a sample size of 1112."* Under the simple random sampling formula $n = Z_{1-\alpha/2}^2 p(1-p) / d^2$ with $p = 0.50$ and $Z_{0.975} = 1.96$:
     - An absolute error of $d = 0.15$ yields $n = 0.9604 / 0.0225 = 42.7 \approx 43$.
     - A relative error of $15\%$ ($d = 0.15 \times 0.50 = 0.075$) yields $n = 0.9604 / 0.005625 = 170.7 \approx 171$.
     - To obtain $n = 1,112$, the margin of error must be $d = \sqrt{0.9604 / 1112} = 0.0294$ ($\approx 3\%$ absolute error, or $\approx 6\%$ relative error).
  2. **Unaccounted clustering in regression and path models:** Although the authors multiplied $1,112 \times 1.2 = 1,335$ to account for classroom cluster sampling, all subsequent univariate/multivariate logistic regressions (Table 2) and path models (Table 3) treat the 1,475 students as independent observations without classroom random effects (`glmer`) or cluster-robust standard errors (`survey::svyglm`), underestimating standard errors—particularly for `SS (Class)` (social support from classmates within the same sampled classroom).
* **Fix:** Correct the stated margin of error in Section 2.1, and re-estimate all regression and path models using cluster-robust standard errors or generalized linear mixed models with a random intercept for classroom cluster.

### Defect 4: Psychometric Contradictions (Cronbach's $\alpha$ on Single Items, Child/Adolescent Scale on Adults, and Omitted Significant Covariates) [ Pillar 3, Pillar 7 — Major ]
* **Location:** Section 2.2 (line 29), Section 2.3 (lines 37–43), Section 3.1 (Table 1, lines 75–78), and Section 3.2 (line 96 vs. Table 2, lines 108–111).
* **Forensic Evidence:**
  1. **Undefined Cronbach's $\alpha$ for single-item modules:** Section 2.2 states that *"Cronbach's $\alpha$ for all modules ranged from 0.87 to 0.94"*, yet Section 2.3 defines the biodiversity attitude module as a single question and the willingness module as a single question. Cronbach's $\alpha$ requires at least two items.
  2. **Age-inappropriate instrument and "Pre-college students" in a university survey:** The authors used the *Child and Adolescent Social Support Scale* (CASSS, designed for grades 3–12) on university students and postgraduates. Meanwhile, Table 1 (line 76) lists 279 (18.92%) "Pre-college students" (`预科生`, preparatory students) alongside 1,080 undergraduates and 116 postgraduates without explaining this category in the Methods.
  3. **Omission of statistically significant parental education effects in Results text:** In Table 2, Father's education (Senior high school and above: $\text{aOR} = 0.60$, 95% CI: $0.41\text{–}0.88$) and Mother's education (Junior high school: $\text{aOR} = 1.47$, 95% CI: $1.10\text{–}1.95$) are statistically significant predictors of Attitude, and Mother's education (Senior high school and above: $\text{aOR} = 0.63$, 95% CI: $0.40\text{–}0.99$) is a significant predictor of Willingness, yet Section 3.2 (lines 96 and 120) ignores parental education entirely and claims only major, course score, and social support were associated.
