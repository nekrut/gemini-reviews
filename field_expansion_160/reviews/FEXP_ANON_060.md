## 1. Blinded Metadata Perception

* **Perceived Field & Subfield:** Reproductive Toxicology / Gut–Ovary Axis / Microbiome and Oocyte Transcriptomics
* **Study Design:** Preclinical murine toxicology and intervention study combining direct oral Bisphenol S (BPS) exposure, fecal microbiota transplantation (`FMT-BPS` vs. `FMT-control`), and alginate oligosaccharide (`AOS`) rescue with oocyte functional assays, IVF preimplantation embryo culture, Smart-seq2 oocyte RNA-seq, and V4 16S rDNA sequencing of small-intestinal luminal microbiota.
* **Model System & Sample Size:** Female ICR mice ($\sim 200$ total used across experiments, 4 weeks old at start, treated for 4 weeks across 6 groups: Control, BPS [1 mg/kg/d], FMT-control, FMT-BPS, AOS [10 mg/kg/d], and AOS/BPS; $n = 3$ independent biological experiments/mice per group for oocyte Smart-seq2, IVF, and intestinal histology/WB/IF, and $n = 5$ independent biological experiments/mice per group for 16S rDNA sequencing, ATP content, and serum IL-6 ELISA).
* **Perceived Methodological Maturity:** Well-designed multi-modal experimental progression (direct exposure $\rightarrow$ LC-MS check for residual fecal BPS $\rightarrow$ FMT phenocopy $\rightarrow$ AOS rescue) with transparent reporting of exact mouse and oocyte numbers and public SRA deposition of both RNA-seq and 16S datasets; moderated by pairwise Student's $t$-tests/Wilcoxon tests without multi-group ANOVA correction when reusing the same Control and BPS cohorts across figures, small biological sample sizes ($n = 3\text{–}5$ mice/group), lack of a luminal binding/pharmacokinetic control for co-gavaged AOS+BPS, and a gene symbol misannotation (`Tnr`).

## 2. Executive Summary & Editorial Verdict

This study investigates whether oral exposure to Bisphenol S (BPS; $1\text{ mg/kg/day}$ for 4 weeks) impairs female mouse oocyte quality via gut microbiota dysbiosis and whether co-administration of alginate oligosaccharide (AOS; $10\text{ mg/kg/day}$) prevents this toxicity. In 8-week-old female ICR mice, BPS reduces germinal vesicle (GV) oocyte mitochondrial membrane potential (JC-1 ratio to 66.4% of control) and ATP content (to 89.4%), increases Annexin-V early apoptosis, decreases first polar body extrusion (from 83.0% to 74.7%), and lowers MII oocyte IVF fertilization (93.5% to 84.1%) and blastocyst development rates (85.1% to 68.5%) (Figure 1). Critically, transplanting small-intestinal luminal microbiota from BPS-treated donors into antibiotic-pretreated recipient mice (`FMT-BPS`)—after verifying via triple-quadrupole LC-MS MRM that residual BPS in the fecal suspension is negligible (Figure S2)—reproduces the oocyte mitochondrial, apoptotic, and IVF defects (Figure 2), small-intestinal T-cell infiltration (`CD3e` IHC and Western blot), tight-junction `ZO-1` loss (~30%), and serum IL-6 elevation (>60%) (Figure 4). Both V4 16S rDNA sequencing (`PRJNA1045335`, $n = 5$/group) and Smart-seq2 oocyte RNA-seq (`PRJNA1045813`, $n = 3$/group) show concordant shifts between BPS and `FMT-BPS` (shared enrichment of `Clostridia_UCG-014`, `Mucispirillum`, and `[Eubacterium]_nodatum_group`, depletion of `Parabacteroides` and `Odoribacter`, and oocyte upregulation of `Dusp1`/`Nox4`/MAPK stress pathways). Co-gavage with AOS (`AOS/BPS`) restores `Parabacteroides`, suppresses pro-inflammatory taxa, prevents intestinal `CD3e` infiltration and `ZO-1` loss, and rescues oocyte quality and IVF blastocyst development (Figures 6–8).

**Editorial Verdict: Minor-to-Moderate Revision (Strong / Sound).** This is a comprehensive, logically structured experimental study that goes beyond descriptive microbiome correlations by performing both a causal phenocopy via FMT (backed by LC-MS MRM quantification to rule out direct chemical transfer of BPS in the FMT inoculum) and a prebiotic rescue (`AOS`), while depositing both raw sequencing datasets in NCBI SRA (`PRJNA1045813` and `PRJNA1045335`) and transparently disclosing in the figure legends where control and BPS groups from concurrent experiments are replotted (Figures 6 and 7). Four statistical and mechanistic points should be addressed to strengthen the manuscript:
1. **Multi-Group Statistical Testing When Reusing Concurrent Control and BPS Groups:** As disclosed in the legends of Figures 6 and 7, all six experimental arms (`Control`, `BPS`, `FMT-control`, `FMT-BPS`, `AOS`, `AOS/BPS`) were run concurrently, and the `Control` and `BPS` data from Figures 1 and 4 are replotted in Figures 6 and 7 for comparison against `AOS` and `AOS/BPS`. Analyzing a 4-group ($2 \times 2$ `BPS` $\times$ `AOS`) design via multiple unadjusted pairwise Student's $t$-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (Methods line 79) rather than a two-way ANOVA (or one-way ANOVA with Tukey/Dunnett post-hoc correction) inflates the family-wise Type I error rate.
2. **Unruled Luminal Chelation / Bioavailability Confounding in Oral `AOS + BPS` Co-Gavage:** As the authors candidly note in the Discussion (line 120), in the oocyte RNA-seq PCA (Figure 8A), the `AOS/BPS` group clusters near `BPS` and `Control` while `AOS` alone separates distinctly, and *"Another less likely, but not excluded, possibility is that AOS may interact directly with BPS, thus suppressing/preventing its activity."* Because negatively charged alginate oligosaccharides were co-gavaged daily with BPS (`10 mg/kg AOS + 1 mg/kg BPS`), AOS could physically adsorb BPS or reduce its intestinal absorption in the gut lumen unless systemic serum/urinary BPS levels or an `FMT-AOS/BPS` transfer experiment are evaluated.
3. **Small Biological Sample Sizes ($n = 3$ to $5$ Mice/Group) and Nested Oocyte Measurements:** Several oocyte assays (JC-1, Annexin-V, PB1 extrusion) pool 65–115 oocytes harvested from $n = 3$ independent experiments ($1\text{–}2$ mice per experiment), and the 16S Wilcoxon tests ($n = 5$ mice/group) do not report Benjamini–Hochberg FDR-adjusted $q$-values.
4. **Gene Symbol Misannotation (`Tnr`):** On line 69, `Tnr` is correctly defined as **tenascin R (`Tnr`)**, whereas on line 97 the same gene symbol is misidentified as **"transgenic Notch reporter (`Tnr`)"**.

### Specific Conclusions and Assessment of Support

* **Oral BPS exposure (1 mg/kg/day for 4 weeks) impairs mouse oocyte mitochondrial function, meiotic maturation, and post-IVF blastocyst development: [Well Supported].**  
  Consistent reductions in JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential, ATP content, PB1 extrusion, 2-pronuclei fertilization, and blastocyst formation are demonstrated across $n = 3\text{–}5$ independent experimental replicates (Figure 1).

* **FMT of small-intestinal luminal microbiota from BPS-treated mice into antibiotic-depleted recipients phenocopies BPS-induced intestinal inflammation and oocyte defects independent of residual fecal BPS: [Well Supported].**  
  The combination of `FMT-BPS` phenocopying all oocyte/IVF endpoints (Figure 2), small-intestinal `CD3e` infiltration, `ZO-1` loss, and serum IL-6 elevation (Figure 4), together with LC-MS MRM verification that residual BPS in the fecal suspension is below toxic levels (Figure S2), provides strong causal evidence for microbiota-mediated toxicity.

* **Co-administration of AOS (10 mg/kg/day) rescues BPS-induced oocyte damage solely by reshaping the gut microbiota: [Moderately Supported].**  
  While AOS/BPS co-treatment clearly prevents intestinal inflammation, suppresses BPS-enriched pathobionts (`Clostridia_UCG-014`, `Mucispirillum`), restores `Parabacteroides`, and rescues oocyte IVF competence (Figures 6–7), daily oral co-gavage of AOS and BPS could also reduce intestinal BPS absorption via physical interaction in the gut lumen (as acknowledged on line 120), which was not excluded by serum BPS LC-MS measurement or `FMT-AOS/BPS` transplantation.

* **Oocyte RNA-seq identifies shared MAPK/ERK and cell-adhesion transcriptional dysregulation in BPS and FMT-BPS oocytes that is reversed by AOS: [Moderately Supported].**  
  Smart-seq2 (`limma` $\text{padj} < 0.05$, $|\log_2\text{FC}| \ge 1$) identifies ~33% overlap in DEGs between BPS and `FMT-BPS` oocytes (Figure 5D–E) and 171 rescued / 181 suppressed genes with AOS (Figure 8B–C), though in the global transcriptome PCA (Figure 8A) `AOS/BPS` does not clearly separate from `BPS` (line 120), and `Tnr` is misannotated on line 97.

## 3. Forensic Defect Inventory

### 1. Pairwise Student's $t$-Tests Instead of Two-Way ANOVA on Concurrent $2 \times 2$ (`BPS` $\times$ `AOS`) Experiments (Pillars 3, 4)
* **Defect:** In Figures 6, 7, and 8, four groups forming a $2 \times 2$ factorial design (`Control`, `BPS`, `AOS`, `AOS/BPS`) were run concurrently, reusing the `Control` and `BPS` data from Figures 1 and 4. However, statistical significance was evaluated using multiple unadjusted pairwise Student's $t$-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (Methods line 79) rather than a two-way ANOVA with post-hoc multiple-comparison adjustment. Additionally, in the oocyte imaging/maturation assays (Figures 1A, 1C, 1D, 2A, 2C, 2D, 6A, 6C, 6D), it should be explicitly confirmed that the statistical unit of analysis ($n$) in the $t$-test/ANOVA is the **independent biological experiment / mouse ($n = 3$)** rather than the pooled individual oocytes ($N = 64\text{–}115$), which are technical subsamples nested within mice.
* **WHY:** Reusing the same `Control` and `BPS` groups across multiple pairwise $t$-tests inflates the family-wise false-positive rate, and treating 80 oocytes from 3 mice as $N = 80$ independent degrees of freedom would constitute nested pseudoreplication.
* **WHAT:** Re-analyze the 4-group (`Control`, `BPS`, `AOS`, `AOS/BPS`) oocyte, histological, and ELISA datasets using two-way ANOVA (`BPS * AOS` interaction) followed by Tukey's HSD post-hoc test with $n = 3$ (or $n = 5$) independent biological replicates as the unit of analysis, and apply Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction to the 16S genus-level Wilcoxon tests ($n = 5$/group).
* **HOW:** Compute the experiment-level means ($n = 3$ or $n = 5$), run a two-way ANOVA (`lm(y ~ BPS * AOS)`) in R or GraphPad Prism, and update the adjusted $P$-values in Figures 6 and 7.

### 2. Pharmacokinetic / Luminal Chelation Control for Oral `AOS + BPS` Co-Gavage (Pillars 3, 5, 6)
* **Defect:** In the rescue experiments (Methods chunk 2, line 3), mice were gavaged daily with a combined regimen of $1\text{ mg/kg}$ BPS and $10\text{ mg/kg}$ AOS (`AOS/BPS`). Although the authors used LC-MS MRM ($m/z\text{ }249.1 \to 108.0$) to measure BPS in fecal suspensions for the FMT control (line 67, Figure S2), they did not measure serum or ovarian BPS concentrations in `BPS` vs. `AOS/BPS` mice to verify that AOS did not simply bind BPS in the gastrointestinal lumen and block its systemic absorption.
* **WHY:** Oligosaccharides and dietary fibers can sequester bisphenols in the gut lumen and accelerate their fecal excretion. If systemic BPS bioavailability is lower in `AOS/BPS` mice, the rescue of oocyte quality could be partly pharmacokinetic rather than purely microbiota-mediated.
* **WHAT & HOW:** If banked serum or fecal samples from the `BPS` and `AOS/BPS` groups are available, use the existing Agilent 1290 Infinity II triple-quadrupole LC-MS MRM assay ($m/z\text{ }249.1 \to 108.0$) to compare serum/fecal BPS concentrations between `BPS` and `AOS/BPS` mice; otherwise, expand the discussion on line 120 to recommend staggered dosing (e.g., gavaging AOS 6 hours apart from BPS, or performing `FMT-AOS/BPS` transplantation) in future studies.

### 3. Small-Intestinal Luminal Sampling vs. Fecal Terminology and `Tnr` Gene Misannotation (Pillars 6, 7)
* **Defect:**
  1. **Anatomical Terminology Inconsistency:** In Results line 42, the authors state *"microbiota were collected in fecal samples from BPS-treated or control mice"*, whereas Methods (chunk 2, lines 8 and 45) states: *"Fecal samples were collected from the small intestine of control or 1 mg/kg BPS-treated mice"* and *"8-week female ICR mice were sacrificed to collect the luminal contents of the small intestine."* Small-intestinal chyme/luminal content is anatomically and microbiologically distinct from colonic feces.
  2. **Gene Symbol Misannotation (`Tnr`):** On line 69, the mouse gene symbol `Tnr` is correctly defined as **tenascin R (`Tnr`)**, an extracellular matrix glycoprotein. However, on line 97, the authors write: *"and transgenic Notch reporter (`Tnr`), have important functions in promoting oocyte development and maturation"*. "Transgenic Notch reporter" (abbreviated TNR in the literature) is an engineered GFP reporter transgene, not the endogenous mouse locus `Tnr` (Tenascin R) quantified by aligning wild-type ICR mouse RNA-seq reads to `mm39`.
  3. **Ethics Statement Omission:** Unlike standard animal studies, the Materials and Methods section (lines 128–131) does not state the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) approval name and protocol number.
* **WHY:** Conflating small-intestinal luminal contents with excreted feces obscures the sampling site, misidentifying endogenous Tenascin R (`Tnr`) as a "transgenic Notch reporter" is a factual annotation error, and animal ethics approval details are required for regulatory compliance.
* **WHAT & HOW:** Clarify throughout the Results and Discussion that **small-intestinal luminal contents** were used for both 16S rDNA sequencing and microbiota transplantation; replace *"transgenic Notch reporter (`Tnr`)"* on line 97 with *"tenascin R (`Tnr`)"*; and add the institutional animal ethics committee name and approval number to the "Animals" subsection of the Materials and Methods.
