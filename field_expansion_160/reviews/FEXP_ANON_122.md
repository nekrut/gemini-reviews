## 2. Executive Summary & Editorial Verdict

This study examines whether the three primary overt disfluency types in persistent developmental stuttering—repetitions, prolongations, and blocks—correlate with distinct resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) edges within the DIVA and GODIVA speech motor control frameworks across $N = 20$ adults who stutter. Combining surface-based cortical ROIs (SpeechLab atlas) and volumetric subcortical/cerebellar ROIs in the CONN toolbox, the authors extract 58 hypothesis-driven FB and FF connections and fit bootstrapped LASSO regressions (`cv.glmnet`, 1,000 resamples). They report that repetitions ($\hat{\beta} = 0.21$) and prolongations ($\hat{\beta} = 0.04$) are primarily predicted by increased connectivity between left ventral motor cortex (`vMC`) and right ventral premotor cortex (`vPMC`, FB network), whereas blocks are predicted by reduced connectivity between right anterior cerebellum (`aCb`) and left ventral lateral thalamus (`VL`, $\hat{\beta} = -0.18$, FF network) and increased connectivity between left preSMA and left posterior inferior frontal sulcus (`pIFS`, $\hat{\beta} = 0.17$, FF network). Subject-level behavioral data are fully tabulated in Table 1.

**Editorial Verdict: Major Revision / Salvageable Exploratory Study Undermined by a Bootstrap Standard Error Calculation Flaw (5.4 / 10.0).** The theoretical framing linking DIVA/GODIVA computational predictions to specific disfluency phenotypes is well-reasoned, the hybrid surface/volume anatomical pipeline is rigorous, and the Discussion and Limitations sections are commendably balanced and transparent about the exploratory nature of the $N = 20$ cohort. However, the statistical inference pipeline in the LASSO analysis contains a critical mathematical error that invalidates the reported standard errors and confidence intervals:
1. **Conflation of the Monte Carlo standard error of the bootstrap mean ($\text{SD}_{\text{boot}} / \sqrt{1000}$) with the bootstrap standard error ($\text{SD}_{\text{boot}}$):** In lines 124, 139, and 144, the authors report tiny "standard errors" for LASSO coefficients on $N = 20$ subjects with $p = 59$ predictors ($\text{SE} = 0.008$ on $\hat{\beta} = 0.21$; $\text{SE} = 0.003$ on $\hat{\beta} = 0.04$; $\text{SE} = 0.015$ on $\hat{\beta} = -0.18$) and conclude that **27, 26, and 23 of the 58 predictors are statistically significant after Bonferroni correction**. In bootstrap inference, the standard error of $\hat{\beta}$ is the **standard deviation** across the $B = 1,000$ bootstrap replicates ($\text{SD}_{\text{boot}}$), *not* $\text{SD}_{\text{boot}} / \sqrt{1000}$. Dividing by $\sqrt{1000} \approx 31.62$ shrinks standard errors and confidence intervals by a factor of **31.6**, creating an illusion of extreme precision ($t \approx 13\text{–}26$) that increases arbitrarily with the number of bootstrap iterations $B$.
2. **Severe leverage of 1–2 extreme behavioral outliers in $N = 20$:** Inspection of Table 1 shows that repetition rate is heavily driven by a single outlier (`AWS16` at $5.37\%$, $>3.5\text{ SD}$ above the remaining 19 subjects), prolongation rate contains six exact zeros, and block rate is dominated by two outliers (`AWS02` at $15.50\%$ and `AWS08` at $11.02\%$).
3. **Discrepancies between Table 1 and text summary statistics, plus copy-pasted task-fMRI denoising text.**

### Assessment of Specific Claims

* **Claim 1 (Across 1,000 bootstrap iterations, 27, 26, and 23 of the 58 connectivity predictors show statistically significant Bonferroni-corrected non-zero effects on repetitions, prolongations, and blocks, with $\text{SE} = 0.003\text{–}0.015$): `[Methodologically Flawed]`.**  
  Dividing the bootstrap standard deviation by $\sqrt{1000}$ ($\approx 31.62$) computes the Monte Carlo precision of the bootstrap average rather than the sampling variability of $\hat{\beta}$ across $N = 20$ subjects. When multiplied back by $\sqrt{1000}$, the true bootstrap standard deviations ($\text{SD}_{\text{boot}} \approx 0.253, 0.095, 0.474, 0.443$) exceed the point estimates ($\hat{\beta} = 0.21, 0.04, -0.18, 0.17$), meaning the 95% bootstrap percentile intervals include zero.
* **Claim 2 (Left `vMC` – right `vPMC` connectivity is the most stable positive predictor selected for repetitions and prolongations, whereas right `aCb` – left `VL` and left `preSMA` – left `pIFS` are the most stable predictors selected for blocks): `[Fragile]`.**  
  As exploratory rank-ordered features from stability selection, these top connections align well with DIVA/GODIVA theory; however, with $p = 59$ predictors and $N = 20$ subjects containing extreme skewed outliers (`AWS16`, `AWS02`, `AWS08`), Pearson correlations and LASSO selection frequencies are highly sensitive to single data points.
* **Claim 3 (Repetitions, prolongations, and blocks reflect partially overlapping rather than mutually exclusive contributions of FB and FF speech motor control networks): `[Moderately Supported]`.**  
  The authors' nuanced interpretation in the Discussion—explicitly rejecting a strict one-to-one mapping and framing the results as hypothesis-generating trends—is well aligned with the behavioral and neural overlap observed in the cohort.

## 3. Forensic Defect Inventory

### Defect 1: Division of Bootstrap Standard Deviations by $\sqrt{B} = \sqrt{1000}$ in LASSO Confidence Intervals [ Pillar 3, Pillar 4 — Critical ]
* **Location:** Section "Statistical analyses" (lines 124–125) and Section "Brain-behavior analysis" (lines 139, 144, 149).
* **Forensic Evidence:**
  Line 124 states: *"Next, we calculated the average coefficient values and standard deviation across the 1000 iterations for each predictor. To assess whether each coefficient value was statistically significantly different from zero, we constructed 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each raw LASSO coefficient using the standard errors derived from the bootstrapped samples."*
  In bootstrap estimation (Efron & Tibshirani 1993), the standard deviation of the bootstrap estimates across the $B$ resamples ($\text{SD}^*(\hat{\beta}) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{B-1}\sum_{b=1}^B (\hat{\beta}^{*(b)} - \bar{\beta}^*)^2}$) **is itself the standard error** of $\hat{\beta}$. However, the values reported as `SE` in line 144 (`SE = 0.008` for $\hat{\beta} = 0.21$; `SE = 0.003` for $\hat{\beta} = 0.04$; `SE = 0.015` for $\hat{\beta} = -0.18$; `SE = 0.014` for $\hat{\beta} = 0.17$) were obtained by dividing the bootstrap standard deviation by $\sqrt{1000} = 31.623$ (the standard error of the mean of 1,000 numbers).
  1. Multiplying the reported SEs by $\sqrt{1000}$ recovers the actual bootstrap standard deviations across resamples: $\text{SD}^* = 0.253$ (for $\hat{\beta} = 0.21$), $\text{SD}^* = 0.095$ (for $\hat{\beta} = 0.04$), $\text{SD}^* = 0.474$ (for $\hat{\beta} = -0.18$), and $\text{SD}^* = 0.443$ (for $\hat{\beta} = 0.17$).
  2. This $\sqrt{1000}$ error explains how a high-dimensional LASSO model with $p = 59$ predictors fitted to only $N = 20$ subjects could purportedly yield **27, 26, and 23 Bonferroni-significant predictors** (line 139). Furthermore, because LASSO coefficients have a point mass at zero whenever a variable is not selected ($P(\hat{\beta}^{*(b)} = 0) = 1 - \pi_j$), symmetric normal-theory CIs ($\bar{\beta}^* \pm z \cdot \text{SE}$) are inappropriate for penalized regression estimators.
* **Fix:** Replace the $\text{SD}^* / \sqrt{1000}$ calculation with the empirical **2.5th and 97.5th percentiles** of the 1,000 bootstrap estimates (or report the exact bootstrap selection probability $\hat{\pi}_j = \frac{1}{B}\sum \mathbb{I}(\hat{\beta}_j^{*(b)} \neq 0)$ under formal Bach/Meinshausen & Bühlmann stability selection bounds), and report simple bivariate Spearman/Pearson correlations ($r$, $p$, and $95\%\text{ CI}$ across the $N = 20$ subjects) alongside Fig. 2 so readers can directly evaluate the unpenalized effect sizes.

### Defect 2: Extreme Skewness/Outlier Leverage in $N = 20$ and Penalization of the Scanner Covariate [ Pillar 2, Pillar 3, Pillar 5 — Major ]
* **Location:** Table 1 (lines 58–78) and Section "Statistical analyses" (line 122).
* **Forensic Evidence:**
  1. **Single-subject leverage:** In Table 1, repetition rate across the 20 subjects has a median of $1.41\%$ (IQR: $0.75\%\text{–}1.91\%$), with one extreme outlier (`AWS16` at $5.37\%$, $z = +3.35$). Similarly, block rate has a median of $3.60\%$ (IQR: $1.74\%\text{–}5.49\%$), with two extreme outliers (`AWS02` at $15.50\%$ and `AWS08` at $11.02\%$). Prolongation rate has 5 exact zeros (25% of the sample). In $N = 20$ ordinary least squares / LASSO, squared-error loss is heavily dominated by 1–2 extreme leverage points, and non-parametric bootstrap resampling with replacement createsamples containing 0, 1, 2, or 3 copies of `AWS16` or `AWS02`, driving selection instability.
  2. **Penalized scanner covariate and site-by-gender imbalance:** Line 122 states that a binary scanner variable (`1 = MGH`, `0 = BU`) was included among the predictors in `cv.glmnet` to control for inter-site differences (Siemens Skyra 32-ch vs. Prisma 64-ch). Unless `penalty.factor` is set to `0` for the scanner covariate, LASSO shrinks the scanner coefficient to zero (especially for blocks where $\bar{\lambda} = 0.75$), failing to residualize site effects. Moreover, Table 1 shows that 4 of the 5 females (`AWS01–AWS04`) were scanned at MGH and only 1 female (`AWS05`) at BU.
* **Fix:** Set `penalty.factor = 0` for the scanner (and sex/age) nuisance covariates (or ComBat-harmonize / residualize the 58 RSFC features against scanner site prior to LASSO), and perform a leave-one-subject-out sensitivity check (specifically verifying whether the `vMC–vPMC` association with repetitions survives removing `AWS16` and whether the `aCb–VL` and `preSMA–pIFS` associations with blocks survive removing `AWS02` and `AWS08`).

### Defect 3: Discrepancies Between Table 1 and Text Summary Statistics, Plus Copy-Pasted Task-fMRI Denoising Text [ Pillar 7 — Minor ]
* **Location:** Section "Participants" (line 53), Table 1 (lines 56–78), Section "Behavioral data acquisition" (line 82), Section "Preprocessing" (line 99), Section "Statistical analyses" (line 122), and Section "Behavioral analysis" (line 134).
* **Forensic Evidence:**
  1. **Age range and mean discrepancy:** Line 53 reports *"aged 18–52 years, mean age = 30.50 +/- 11.89 years."* However, in Table 1, participant `AWS09` (line 67) is **58 years old** (making the range **18–58 years**), and the mean of the 20 ages listed in Table 1 is $600 / 20 = \mathbf{30.00}\text{ years}$ ($\text{SD} = 11.97$).
  2. **Disfluency percentage definition and mean discrepancy:** Line 134 reports mean disfluency rates of $1.21\% \pm 1.14\%$ (repetitions), $0.47\% \pm 0.57\%$ (prolongations), and $3.72\% \pm 4.47\%$ (blocks). However, the means of the 20 participant percentages printed in Table 1 are **$1.58\% \pm 1.19\%$** (repetitions), **$0.55\% \pm 0.61\%$** (prolongations), and **$4.44\% \pm 3.84\%$** (blocks). This discrepancy arises because line 82 defines the rate as the unweighted average across the three tasks (conversation, reading, phone), whereas the Table 1 caption (line 56) and line 122 define it as total disfluencies divided by total syllables pooled across tasks. Clarify which of the two definitions was actually used as the outcome vector $Y$ in `cv.glmnet`.
  3. **Task-fMRI copy-paste artifact in resting-state denoising:** Line 99 states that resting-state BOLD denoising included *"individual regressors for each run and session, task condition, and error trial [88]"*—even though the scan was a single 6-minute eyes-open resting-state run with no task conditions or error trials (line 89).
  4. **Figure 3 legend typo:** Line 157 defines `vPMC = ventral primary motor cortex` instead of `ventral premotor cortex`.
