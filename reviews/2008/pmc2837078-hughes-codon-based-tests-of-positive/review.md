### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This paper critiques two approaches for detecting positive Darwinian selection in protein-coding sequences: (1) comparing branch-level nonsynonymous ($b_N$) and synonymous ($b_S$) substitution rates along individual phylogenetic branches, and (2) the maximum-likelihood (ML) branch-site test of Zhang, Nielsen, and Yang (2005). First, using a simple Poisson model of codon substitutions, the author shows that individual codons with zero synonymous differences and $\ge 1$ nonsynonymous differences ($p_N > p_S$) occur with non-zero probability under strong purifying selection ($p_N / p_S = 0.1$ to $0.5$). Second, analyzing 69 single-copy orthologous genes across 7 mammalian species (11 branches per tree, 759 gene-branches total) aligned with `ClustalW` and analyzed with `PAML`, the author reports that $b_N > b_S$ occurs on 72/759 branches (9.5%), predominantly on the shortest primate branches where stochastic variance (coefficient of variation) is highest. Applying the branch-site likelihood ratio test (LRT) separately to all 759 branches as foreground at nominal $\alpha = 0.05$, the author finds 82 significant branches (10.8%) and 117 Bayes Empirical Bayes (BEB) positively selected sites across 35 branches, noting that neither $b_N > b_S$ nor the branch-site test preferentially flags immune system genes despite immune genes having a higher median $b_N / b_S$ ($\approx 0.35$ vs. $\approx 0.25$ in non-immune genes). No alignment files or analysis scripts are deposited, though gene identifiers are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

While the empirical demonstration that raw branch-level $b_N > b_S$ point estimates on very short branches (such as human and chimpanzee terminal branches where $b_S \approx 0.005$) are dominated by Poisson sampling variance is completely valid and a useful caution against naive point-estimate comparisons, the manuscript's theoretical and empirical critique of ML codon models (`PAML` branch-site test) rests on fundamental mathematical and statistical misconceptions. Specifically, the paper (1) erects a straw-man claim that ML codon methods assume observed $p_N > p_S$ at a single codon deterministically implies positive selection, ignoring that the Goldman–Yang continuous-time Markov chain explicitly integrates over Poisson mutation stochasticity in its likelihood function; (2) performs 759 exploratory branch-site tests at an uncorrected $\alpha = 0.05$ without multiple-testing correction—guaranteeing dozens of false positives by design—and then uses those expected Type I errors (plus uncurated `ClustalW` alignment artifacts and double-mutation serine switches) to claim the test is invalid; and (3) conflates chronic relaxation of purifying selection across an entire gene ($\omega \approx 0.35 < 1$) with episodic positive selection ($\omega_2 > 1$ at a subset of sites on a foreground branch).

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Point estimates of $b_N > b_S$ on short phylogenetic branches are frequently driven by stochastic sampling error rather than positive selection: [Well Supported].**  
  Table 1 and Fig. 3 clearly demonstrate that across the 69 mammalian orthologs, branches 1–3 (human, chimpanzee, and human-chimpanzee ancestor) have the smallest mean $b_S$ ($0.0054$ to $0.0273$), the highest coefficients of variation in $b_S$ and $b_N$ ($r_S = -0.745$ and $-0.773$), and the highest proportion of genes with $b_N > b_S$ ($r_S = -0.817, P = 0.002$). Taking raw $b_N > b_S$ on a short branch without testing whether $b_N$ is significantly greater than $b_S$ is indeed a statistical artifact of discrete mutation counts.

* **Immune system genes (particularly non-cytokine receptors and surface proteins) exhibit higher average $b_N$ and $b_N / b_S$ across the mammalian tree than non-immune genes: [Well Supported].**  
  Across the 759 gene-branches, median $b_N$ and $b_N / b_S$ are significantly elevated in `Im+/Sig-` genes compared to other functional categories (Kruskal–Wallis $P < 0.001$, Fig. 4), and this divergence scales consistently with branch length ($r_S = 0.745$, Fig. 5), reflecting a persistent gene-wide elevation in amino acid substitution rate (consistent with relaxed purifying constraint and/or localized adaptive evolution).

* **Probability theory (Inequalities 3 and 6) proves that codon-based ML tests rely on a false assumption because $p_N > p_S$ can occur at individual codons by chance under purifying selection: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  Equations (1)–(6) state that *"codon-based tests of positive selection depend on the assumption that the existence of one or more codons with $d_N > d_S$ implies positive selection. This assumption is false if there exists in the universe even a single codon for which, in the absence of positive selection, $f_S(x)[1-f_N(x)] > 0$."* This confuses the *underlying rate parameter* $\omega = d_N / d_S$ of a continuous-time Markov process with the *realized count of substitutions* ($p_N, p_S$) at a single codon along a single branch. The Zhang et al. (2005) branch-site model does not infer positive selection whenever a codon has 0 synonymous and 1 nonsynonymous change; rather, its null model ($\omega_0 < 1, \omega_1 = 1$) already accounts for the exact binomial/Poisson probability of observing multiple nonsynonymous and zero synonymous substitutions at neutral or constrained sites. A significant LRT requires that the density of nonsynonymous substitutions across sites on the foreground branch significantly exceeds what can be generated by Poisson fluctuations under a mixture of $\omega_0 < 1$ and $\omega_1 = 1$.

* **The branch-site test of Zhang et al. (2005) "focuses largely on statistical artifacts" because 10.8% of branches were significant at $P < 0.05$ and most BEB sites had $p_S = 0$ or $p_N \approx p_S$ on the foreground branch: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  This conclusion is compromised by three methodological errors in how the author applied and evaluated the test:
  1. **Uncorrected "fishing expedition" across 759 branches:** The branch-site test was designed for testing an *a priori* designated foreground branch; when scanning all 11 branches across 69 trees (759 tests) at nominal $\alpha = 0.05$ without Bonferroni or FDR control (e.g., Rom's or Holm's correction as recommended by Anisimova & Yang 2007), ~38–80 false-positive branches are expected under the null hypothesis, especially when model assumptions (such as homogeneous synonymous rates or error-free alignments) are slightly violated.
  2. **Evaluating single-branch parsimony counts ($p_N, p_S$) at BEB sites:** On a single mammalian branch of length $b_S \approx 0.10$, the expected number of synonymous substitutions at a single codon (with ~0.75 synonymous sites) is $0.10 \times 0.75 = 0.075$, meaning $>92\%$ of all codons—whether neutral, purifying, or positively selected—will have zero synonymous substitutions ($p_S = 0$) on that branch. Finding that 57.3% of BEB sites have $p_S = 0$ (and the remainder have 1 synonymous change, giving $p_S \approx 1/(0.75) \approx 1.07$) is a mathematical necessity of single-codon discrete counts on a single branch, not evidence of "chance reduction in synonymous substitution."
  3. **Uncurated `ClustalW` alignments:** In the Discussion (line 233), the author acknowledges running automated `ClustalW` alignments without visual inspection or alignment filtering. Feeding misaligned indels into codon models is a well-known user error that generates spurious clusters of nonsynonymous changes, not a theoretical defect of the likelihood framework.

* **The failure of the branch-site test to disproportionately flag immune genes proves it cannot detect biologically meaningful selection: [Overstated].**  
  As shown in Fig. 4c, the median branch-wide $b_N / b_S$ of the immune genes in this dataset is $\approx 0.25$–$0.38$, which is well below $1.0$ and reflects gene-wide relaxation of purifying selection (or chronic background evolution across all branches). The branch-site test specifically tests for an *episodic burst* of $\omega_2 > 1$ restricted to a single foreground branch relative to the background branches; if an immune gene evolves at a uniformly elevated $\omega \approx 0.4$ across all mammalian branches, the branch-site null model (which allows a proportion $p_1$ of sites to evolve at $\omega_1 = 1$ on all branches) is not violated and *should not* reject the null hypothesis.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Distinguish Between Underlying Markov Rate Parameters ($\omega = d_N/d_S$) and Realized Single-Codon Substitution Counts ($p_N, p_S$)
* **WHY:** Equations (1)–(6) conflate the instantaneous rate ratio parameter $\omega$ in codon substitution models with the empirical count of substitutions at an individual codon (`p_N`, `p_S`), misrepresenting how maximum-likelihood codon models formulate the null and alternative hypotheses.
* **WHAT:** Revise the "Theoretical study" section to clearly distinguish between naive counting heuristics (claiming positive selection whenever a single codon or short branch has $p_N > p_S$) and model-based likelihood ratio tests that integrate over the Poisson variance of substitution counts.
* **HOW:** Rewrite the paragraph accompanying Equations (1)–(6) to state that while raw $p_N > p_S$ at a single codon or short branch occurs frequently under purifying selection ($\omega < 1$), maximum-likelihood methods test whether the *rate parameter* $\omega$ exceeds $1.0$ after accounting for stochastic count variance under a null distribution of $\omega \le 1$.

#### 2. Apply Multiple-Testing Correction Across the 759 Branch-Site Tests
* **WHY:** Testing all 11 branches in each of 69 gene trees (759 tests) at an unadjusted $\alpha = 0.05$ inflates the family-wise error rate to near 100% per gene tree and guarantees a large pool of Type I errors, which the paper then analyzes as if they were representative detections of the branch-site method.
* **WHAT:** Apply a formal multiple-testing correction (e.g., Benjamini–Hochberg FDR at $q < 0.05$ across all 759 tests, or Bonferroni correction $\alpha = 0.05 / 11$ per gene tree) and re-evaluate how many branches and BEB sites remain significant.
* **HOW:** Using the 759 LRT statistics ($2\Delta \ell$), compute exact $p$-values from the $50:50$ mixture of point mass $0$ and $\chi^2_1$ (as specified in Zhang et al. 2005), apply Benjamini–Hochberg FDR adjustment, and report the number of branches surviving FDR $< 0.05$ and $< 0.01$.

#### 3. Curate Sequence Alignments and Separate Tandem `TCN <-> AGY` Serine Codon Switches
* **WHY:** Automated `ClustalW` alignments frequently misplace gaps around indels in divergent mammalian orthologs, creating artificial clusters of nonsynonymous substitutions on single branches. Similarly, simultaneous doublet/triplet changes between `TCN` and `AGY` serine codons violate the single-nucleotide instantaneous substitution assumption of standard codon models (`MG94`/`GY94`), a genuine model misspecification issue that deserves to be separated from alignment errors and Type I errors.
* **WHAT:** Inspect and filter the alignments containing the 35 putative significant branches (e.g., using `Gblocks` or `PRANK` codon alignment), and quantify what fraction of the branch-site signals come from (a) alignment errors, (b) `TCN <-> AGY` serine switches, and (c) well-aligned nonsynonymous substitutions that survive multiple-testing correction.
* **HOW:** Re-align the 28 genes containing the 117 BEB sites using a phylogeny-aware aligner (`PRANK` or `MAFFT` L-INS-i), mask gapped/ambiguous columns, re-run `PAML` branch-site Model A vs. Model A null, and tabulate the surviving sites.

#### 4. Use Site Models (`M7` vs. `M8` or `M1a` vs. `M2a`) or Clade Models (`CmC`) When Testing for Gene-Wide Immune System Selection
* **WHY:** The branch-site test (Model A) is parameterized to detect episodic positive selection on a single foreground branch against a conserved background. When selection or relaxed constraint acts continuously across all branches of a tree (as in immune surface receptors), all branches share the elevated rate, reducing the contrast between foreground and background in a single-branch test.
* **WHAT:** Test the 69 genes using site-level models (`M1a` vs. `M2a` and `M7` vs. `M8` in `PAML`) that pool signal across all 11 branches of the tree, and compare their detection rate between immune (`Im+`) and non-immune (`Im-`) genes.
* **HOW:** Run `codeml` with `NSsites = 1 2 7 8` (`model = 0`) on the 69 alignments and test via a $2 \times 2$ contingency table whether genes with significant `M8 vs M7` LRTs are enriched in the `Im+` category.
