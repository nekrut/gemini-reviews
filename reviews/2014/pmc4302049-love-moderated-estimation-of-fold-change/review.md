### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript introduces `DESeq2`, a statistical methodology and R/Bioconductor software package for differential analysis of overdispersed high-throughput sequencing (HTS) count data (such as RNA-seq gene counts) using negative binomial generalized linear models (GLMs). The core methodological advances over its predecessor (`DESeq`) include empirical Bayes shrinkage of gene-wise Cox–Reid adjusted dispersion estimates toward a smooth mean-dependent trend, empirical Bayes ridge shrinkage of logarithmic fold-change (LFC) estimates toward a zero-centered Gaussian prior to stabilize effect sizes in low-information regimes, Wald tests for standard and thresholded composite null hypotheses ($|\beta_{ir}| \le \theta$ and $|\beta_{ir}| \ge \theta$), automatic independent filtering based on mean normalized count, Cook's distance outlier detection/imputation, and a regularized logarithm (`rlog`) transformation for homoskedastic ordination and clustering. The authors evaluate `DESeq2` against `DESeq`, `edgeR`, `edgeR-robust`, `DSS`, `EBSeq`, `limma-voom`, `SAMseq`, `GFOLD`, and `Cuffdiff 2` using negative binomial simulations ($10,000$ genes, $m \in \{6, 8, 10, 20\}$) and empirical RNA-seq datasets (Pickrell et al. lymphoblastoid cell lines, Bottomly et al. mouse strains, and Hammer et al. rat dorsal root ganglion). All source code, processed data objects, and Sweave vignettes reproducing every figure and table are publicly distributed via Bioconductor and a dedicated `DESeq2paper` reproducibility package.

`DESeq2` represents a rigorous, practical, and well-engineered contribution to statistical genomics that directly solves the pervasive problem of noisy fold-change estimates in low-count genes while maintaining competitive sensitivity and false discovery rate (FDR) control. The primary methodological limitations are subtle but important: (1) an internal mathematical discrepancy between the main text/figures—which state that LFC standard errors are obtained from the curvature (inverse Hessian) of the log-posterior—and the Materials and Methods formula, which instead specifies the frequentist ridge sandwich covariance matrix; (2) the empirical prior width $\sigma_r^2$ for LFCs does not deconvolve estimation sampling variance and relies on a thin-tailed Gaussian prior that can over-shrink large true fold changes; and (3) normal-theory ($N(0,1)$) Wald $P$-values ignore finite residual degrees of freedom and dispersion estimation uncertainty in very small sample sizes. Clarifying the exact variance estimator, evaluating heavy-tailed LFC simulation scenarios, and refining the small-sample calibration diagnostics can be accomplished entirely through analytical clarification and minor simulation extensions.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Claim 1: Empirical Bayes shrinkage of dispersions toward a parametric mean-dispersion trend improves sensitivity over `DESeq` while controlling false positives via a dispersion-outlier bypass. [Well Supported]**  
  By replacing `DESeq`'s conservative maximum-of-trend-and-individual-estimate rule with a log-normal prior whose variance $\sigma_d^2 = \max(s_{\text{lr}}^2 - \psi_1((m-p)/2), 0.25)$ analytically subtracts the trigamma sampling variance of the log-dispersion estimator, `DESeq2` adapts shrinkage strength to the residual degrees of freedom $m - p$. The heuristic bypass that retains the unshrunken Cox–Reid MLE $\alpha_i^{\text{gw}}$ whenever $\log \alpha_i^{\text{gw}} > \log \alpha_{\text{tr}}(\bar{\mu}_i) + 2 s_{\text{lr}}$ effectively prevents false positives in hyper-variable outlier genes, as confirmed by the null mock comparisons on Pickrell et al. (Fig. 7).

* **Claim 2: Empirical Bayes shrinkage of logarithmic fold changes substantially reduces LFC estimation error and increases gene-ranking reproducibility across independent replicates. [Well Supported]**  
  In the balanced split-half benchmark of the Bottomly et al. dataset (Fig. 3 and Fig. S3), ranking genes by MAP LFCs increases the top-100 overlap between independent sub-experiments from 21/100 (unshrunken MLEs) to 81/100 and sharply reduces root-mean-square error across expression levels. One caveat—partially visible in the simulation benchmarks (lines 170–171, Figs. S12–S16), where `edgeR`'s predictive LFCs match `DESeq2` on truly differentially expressed (DE) genes while `DESeq2` has lower error over all (mostly null) genes—is that `DESeq2` fits a single thin-tailed Gaussian prior $N(0, \sigma_r^2)$ by matching the 95th percentile of $|\hat{\beta}^{\text{MLE}}|$ without subtracting sampling noise. When true biological effects follow a sparse, heavy-tailed distribution, a Gaussian prior introduces downward bias on large true LFCs at moderate read depths.

* **Claim 3: The `DESeq2` Wald test and automatic independent filtering achieve superior or comparable sensitivity while controlling the false discovery rate at the nominal threshold. [Moderately Supported]**  
  Across negative binomial simulations (Fig. 6) and split-sample verification benchmarks on Bottomly et al. (Figs. 8–9), `DESeq2` consistently ranks among the top methods that keep empirical FDR near or below the nominal $\alpha = 0.10$ level, and the Basu's theorem justification for filtering on $\bar{\mu}_i$ is mathematically sound. However, two caveats affect this claim. First, the simulation benchmark generates counts strictly from a homoskedastic-within-group Negative Binomial distribution with symmetric fold changes ($\pm \log_2 2, 3, 4$), which matches `DESeq2`'s exact parametric model and lacks real-world transcriptomic complexities such as sample-specific GC/length bias, zero-inflation, or isoform-length shifts. Second, in the Bottomly et al. verification benchmark (chunk 2, line 6), the authors intentionally omitted the known 3-batch covariate from all GLM design matrices; while done for symmetry with tools that cannot model covariates, unmodeled batch structure inflates within-group dispersion and alters the reference call set.

* **Claim 4: The regularized logarithm (`rlog`) transformation stabilizes variance across the dynamic range and outperforms standard $\log_2(\text{count}+1)$ and VST when library size factors vary widely. [Well Supported]**  
  Both empirical ordination on the Hammer et al. dataset (Fig. 5) and adjusted Rand index clustering benchmarks across unequal size factors (Fig. S17) demonstrate that shrinking sample-specific fold changes toward an intercept-only baseline prevents low-count Poisson noise from dominating Euclidean distances while naturally adjusting for wide variations in sequencing depth ($s_j$).

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Reconcile the Mathematical Contradiction in the LFC Posterior Variance Formula
* **WHY:** In the main text (lines 70, 91) and Figure 2D caption, the authors state that the standard error $\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_{ir})$ used in the Wald test is *"obtained from the curvature of the coefficient's posterior at its maximum."* Mathematically, the inverse of the negative Hessian of the log-posterior at the MAP estimate is $(X^t W X + \vec{\lambda} I)^{-1}$. However, in *Materials and Methods* ("Wald test", chunk 2, line 292), the authors write the frequentist ridge sandwich covariance matrix:
  $$\Sigma_i = (X^t W X + \vec{\lambda} I)^{-1} (X^t W X) (X^t W X + \vec{\lambda} I)^{-1}$$
  These two quantities are not equal: the sandwich variance is strictly smaller by an extra factor of $(X^t W X)(X^t W X + \vec{\lambda} I)^{-1}$. In the scalar case, dividing the shrunken MAP estimate $\frac{J}{J+\lambda}\hat{\beta}^{\text{MLE}}$ by the sandwich standard error $\frac{\sqrt{J}}{J+\lambda}$ cancels the ridge penalty $\lambda$ almost entirely, whereas dividing by the posterior curvature standard error $(J+\lambda)^{-1/2}$ shrinks the Wald $z$-statistic by $\sqrt{J/(J+\lambda)}$.
* **WHAT:** Clarify whether `DESeq2` software computes $\text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_{ir})$ from the sandwich covariance matrix (line 292) or the inverse Hessian of the log-posterior (lines 70, 91, Fig. 2D), and explain the statistical properties of the chosen estimator.
* **HOW:** Audit the C++/R source code of `DESeq2` (`fitBeta`), revise lines 70, 91, and Fig. 2D (or line 292) so that the prose and equations match exactly, and briefly note how the sandwich covariance vs. posterior curvature affects the shrinkage of the Wald $z$-statistic relative to the unshrunken MLE Wald statistic.

#### 2. Deconvolve Sampling Variance and Evaluate Heavy-Tailed Effect Distributions in LFC Prior Estimation
* **WHY:** Unlike the dispersion prior variance $\sigma_d^2$ (which subtracts the expected estimation variance $\psi_1((m-p)/2)$), the LFC prior variance $\sigma_r^2$ is set directly from the 95th percentile of raw MLEs ($\hat{\beta}_{ir}^{\text{MLE}}$) without subtracting the Fisher-information-based sampling variance $(X^t W_i X)^{-1}_{rr}$. Consequently, noisy low-count genes inflate $\sigma_r^2$, while the thin tails of the normal prior $N(0, \sigma_r^2)$ over-shrink truly large fold changes.
* **WHAT:** Test an empirical Bayes prior width estimator that weights or deconvolves the gene-wise MLE sampling variance, and expand the LFC accuracy benchmark (Figs. S12–S16) to include sparse, heavy-tailed true LFC distributions (e.g., Laplace or $t$/Cauchy mixtures).
* **HOW:** In the quantile-matching step for $\sigma_r^2$ (chunk 2, line 235), compare the current unweighted 95% quantile against a quantile computed after subtracting expected sampling variance (or restricted to genes above median information $J_m(\hat{\beta}_{ir})$), and report separate RMSE/MAE curves for true-null and true-DE gene subsets across heavy-tailed simulation scenarios.

#### 3. Assess Small-Sample Wald Test Calibration and Batch-Adjusted Real-Data Benchmarks
* **WHY:** Comparing the Wald statistic $z_{ir} = \hat{\beta}_{ir} / \text{SE}(\hat{\beta}_{ir})$ to a standard normal $N(0,1)$ treats the MAP dispersion $\alpha_i^{\text{MAP}}$ as known without error, which can yield anti-conservative tail probabilities when residual degrees of freedom $m - p \le 3$ (e.g., 2 vs. 2 replicates). Additionally, omitting the known batch covariate in the Bottomly et al. benchmark (chunk 2, line 6) obscures the performance of GLM-capable methods under their intended usage.
* **WHAT:** Add a $2\text{ vs. }2$ sample-size column to the simulation and Pickrell mock-null FPR benchmarks (Figs. 6–7), evaluate replacing $N(0,1)$ with a $t_{m-p}$ reference distribution in minimal-replicate designs, and include a supplementary panel for Bottomly et al. where the 3-batch covariate is included in the design matrix (`~ batch + strain`).
* **HOW:** Re-run the Pickrell et al. mock sampling at $m = 4$ (2 vs. 2) and the Bottomly et al. evaluation/verification splits with `~ batch + strain` for all GLM-capable methods (`DESeq2`, `DESeq`, `edgeR`, `edgeR-robust`, `DSS`, `limma-voom`) to demonstrate how accounting for known covariates improves both sensitivity and verification precision.
