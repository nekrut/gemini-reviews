### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript presents a large-scale characterization of fungal nucleic acids and staining patterns across human cancers, analyzing 17,401 tissue and blood samples across four cohorts: (1) 1,183 FFPE and frozen tumor, normal adjacent tissue (NAT), and normal breast samples from the Weizmann (WIS) cohort profiled by ITS2 amplicon sequencing and 5.8S rDNA qPCR alongside 104 paraffin-only and 191 extraction negative controls, supplemented by four histological/FISH staining modalities on tissue microarrays (TMAs); (2) 15,512 whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and RNA-seq samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA); and (3) two cell-free plasma WGS cohorts from Hopkins ($n = 537$) and UCSD ($n = 169$). The authors report that fungal DNA is present at lower abundance and richness than bacterial DNA across cancer types, localizes predominantly within cancer cells and macrophages, forms three multi-domain co-occurrence clusters ("mycotypes" F1–F3) associated with host immune subtypes and survival, and enables machine learning (ML) discrimination between cancer types and between cancer patients and healthy controls in tissue and blood (reporting AUROCs up to 96–98% in plasma when combined with bacterial features). Raw amplicon sequences, host-depleted shotgun files, biom tables, and analysis scripts are publicly deposited across NCBI, Qiita, Zenodo, and GitHub.

While the experimental arm in the WIS cohort—combining physical extraction and paraffin controls, mock community benchmarks, technical triplicates, 5.8S qPCR, and multi-modal tissue imaging—provides credible evidence that low-biomass fungal signals exist in subsets of human tumors, the computational and statistical conclusions drawn from the shotgun metagenomic cohorts (TCGA, Hopkins, and UCSD) are undermined by severe methodological flaws. Specifically, the pan-cancer and liquid-biopsy ML pipelines rely on supervised batch correction (Voom-SNM) applied prior to cross-validation using target biological variables (`sample type`, `disease type`, and `treatment response`), paired with negative-control label scrambling performed *after* supervised normalization, which fails to detect target leakage. In addition, the *in silico* decontamination pipeline overrides statistical contaminant calls to retain any fungal species previously reported in the human gut or clinical literature, short-read mapping (median 51 bp) with fractional multi-mapping (`-k 16`) inflates shotgun richness, and confidence intervals for repeated cross-validation treat correlated repeats on the same dataset as independent samples. Resolving these issues requires removing supervised batch correction from the pre-split data matrix, enforcing strict out-of-fold preprocessing, tightening human host depletion against complete telomere-to-telomere (T2T) and pangenome references, and tempering the liquid-biopsy and prognostic claims.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Low-biomass fungal nucleic acids and cellular structures are present in subsets of human tumors and localize to cancer cells and macrophages: [Moderately Supported].**  
  In the WIS cohort, 5.8S qPCR and ITS2 sequencing across 1,183 tissues and 295 physical negative controls demonstrate elevated fungal DNA relative to extraction and paraffin blanks (Figure 1B; Data S1.1), and antibody/FISH staining detects fungal antigens or 28S rRNA in 0–25% of TMA cores across five cancer types (Figure 2). However, the WIS ITS2 pipeline applies a hard per-sample floor of 1,000 reads per ASV followed by multiplication by post-PCR gel dilution factors (5- to 20-fold after 41 PCR cycles; STAR Methods), which distorts quantitative abundance comparisons, and relies on a permissive false discovery rate threshold ($\text{FDR} \le 0.20$) and one-sided $t$-tests.

* **Shotgun sequencing of TCGA tissues and blood reveals rich, cancer-type-specific fungal communities that can be accurately classified by machine learning: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  To build pan-cancer classifiers and overcome poor raw-data discrimination (such as between tumor and NAT; Data S5.8B–G), the authors apply `Voom` (designed for bulk RNA-seq rather than sparse, zero-inflated microbial counts) and Supervised Normalization of Microarrays (`SNM`) across the dataset before 10-fold cross-validation, explicitly passing `sample type` (tumor, NAT, blood) in TCGA and `disease type` and `treatment response` in UCSD as the supervised biological variables (STAR Methods, "TCGA and UCSD cohorts: Batch correction"). Using target labels to supervise feature normalization across all samples leaks class information into the held-out folds and artificially inflates biological variance up to 7.9-fold (Data S5.6A). Crucially, the negative control analyses (scrambling labels or shuffling sample IDs) were executed "just prior to ML model building" (STAR Methods, "All cohorts: Machine learning methods")—*after* Voom-SNM had already baked the unscrambled biological structure into the normalized matrix—invalidating the null baseline in Figures 5 and 6. Furthermore, in TCGA, sequencing center, experimental strategy (WGS vs. RNA-seq), and cancer type are heavily confounded, and short unmapped reads (mean 57.4 bp, median 51 bp, minimum 45 bp) mapped with Bowtie2 `-k 16` and $1/k$ fractional read-splitting artificially inflate TCGA per-sample fungal richness above targeted ITS2 sequencing (Figure 3A).

* **In silico decontamination reliably removes environmental and reagent contaminants in TCGA and Hopkins cohorts: [Fragile].**  
  Although `decontam` was run in frequency mode on TCGA plate-center batches (flagging 57 of 319 fungal species), Step 2 of the decontamination protocol automatically retained any fungal species present in the WIS cohort, the HMP gut mycobiome cohort, or reported in the literature as a human commensal or pathogen (STAR Methods). Because ubiquitous laboratory, reagent, skin, and food contaminants (*Saccharomyces cerevisiae*, *Malassezia restricta*, *Malassezia globosa*, *Candida albicans*, *Aspergillus* spp.) are also human commensals/opportunists, this literature rule overrides statistical contamination signals. Moreover, the Hopkins plasma cohort lacked both negative controls and `decontam` frequency filtering, relying instead on copying the TCGA retained species list.

* **Intratumoral fungal-bacterial-immune "mycotypes" and specific fungal taxa stratify patient survival and therapy response: [Fragile].**  
  The pan-cancer survival associations in TCGA (Figure 4D) and clinical associations in WIS (Figure 6A–G) rest on fragile statistical foundations. In WIS, the survival associations for *Malassezia globosa* in breast cancer ($n = 11$ positive vs. $n = 69$ negative, $p = 0.024$; Figure 6C) and Phaeosphaeriaceae in ovarian cancer ($n = 9$ vs. $n = 45$, $p = 0.015$; Figure 6F) are unadjusted univariate log-rank tests on small positive subgroups without multivariable adjustment for age, stage, or HER2 status—despite Figures 6A–B showing that fungal prevalence in the same breast cohort covaries with patient age and HER2 status. In TCGA, MMvec co-occurrence filtering is described with a self-contradictory consistency rule ("features whose median co-occurrence values were less than the standard mean error (SEM) of their co-occurrence values across centers"; STAR Methods), and the survival separation is largely driven by C3 vs. C4/C5 host immune subtypes already known to stratify TCGA survival.

* **Plasma cell-free fungal and bacterial DNA provides stage-invariant pan-cancer liquid biopsy diagnostics (AUROC 94–98%) and a generalizable 20-fungi signature: [Overstated].**  
  In the Hopkins cohort, the "top 20 fungi" signature was selected from the pan-cancer versus healthy ML model trained on the Hopkins dataset and then evaluated on the *same* Hopkins cohort across individual cancer types (Figure 6I) and stages (Data S6.2B), constituting circular in-sample feature selection. In addition, the 99% confidence intervals reported for the Hopkins and UCSD ROC/PR curves (Figures 6H, 6J, 6K) were computed by treating 10 repeats of 10-fold cross-validation on the exact same dataset as 10 independent observations, which measures only random fold-partitioning jitter rather than sample variance and yields severely anti-conservative confidence bands.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Eliminate Supervised Batch Correction Leakage and Perform Strict Out-of-Fold Preprocessing
* **WHY:** Applying Voom-SNM with `sample type`, `disease type`, or `treatment response` as the supervised biological variable across the full dataset prior to cross-validation leaks test-set labels and feature distributions into the training process. Scrambling labels *after* Voom-SNM fails to control for this leakage.
* **WHAT:** Remove supervised SNM normalization from all ML classification pipelines, and ensure all feature selection, scaling, upsampling, and unsupervised batch adjustment occur strictly inside each training fold.
* **HOW:** Within each 10-fold CV split (or external train/test split), fit any normalization or batch-removal parameters exclusively on the 90% training fold without passing the prediction target (`sample type`, `disease type`, or `response`) as a supervised preservation variable, and project the 10% test fold onto those learned parameters. For negative controls, permute the target labels *before* any normalization, filtering, or batch correction step, and re-run the entire pipeline end-to-end.Similarly, when evaluating the "top 20 fungi" signature inside the Hopkins cohort (Figure 6I, Data S6.2B), select the top 20 features strictly within each training fold rather than globally across the cohort.

#### 2. Harden Host Depletion and Eliminate Multi-Mapping Read Splitting in Shotgun Cohorts
* **WHY:** Mapping short unmapped reads (45–57 bp) after GRCh38.p7 filtering to a microbial database with only 320 fungal genomes using Bowtie2 `-k 16` and $1/k$ fractional assignment allows unannotated human repeats (e.g., ribosomal DNA arrays, centromeric/telomeric repeats absent from GRCh38.p7) and low-complexity sequences to map to eukaryotic fungal genomes and split across up to 16 taxa, inflating apparent species richness and false-positive calls.
* **WHAT:** Upgrade host depletion to include the complete T2T-CHM13 human reference genome and human pangenome/rDNA decoys, enforce a stricter minimum read length and alignment complexity filter, and disable $1/k$ fractional read-splitting for richness and presence/absence analyses.
* **HOW:** Re-map the extracted fungal reads (`1.172 × 10^8` reads) against T2T-CHM13v2.0, human rDNA complete repeating units (U13369.1 / KY962518.1), and common mammalian/plant contaminants using `minimap2` or `Bowtie2`, discarding any read with $\ge 90\%$ identity to host/decoy sequences or low sequence complexity (`fastp --low_complexity_filter`). In Woltka/Bowtie2, evaluate unique genome-specific mappings (or require a minimum per-sample breadth of coverage via `zebra_filter`) rather than summing rounded $1/k$ fractional hits across 16 genomes.

#### 3. Remove Literature Overrides from In Silico Decontamination and Correct WIS Dilution Scaling
* **WHY:** Retaining any species ever reported in HMP gut or clinical literature overrides `decontam` frequency calls for common reagent and skin contaminants (*S. cerevisiae*, *Malassezia* spp., *Candida* spp.). In WIS, multiplying post-PCR amplicon reads by gel dilution factors after 41 PCR cycles and hard-flooring at 1,000 reads conflates end-point PCR saturation with initial template biomass.
* **WHAT:** Report TCGA, Hopkins, and UCSD results using the strict statistical `decontam` output without manual literature exemptions, and analyze WIS ITS2 amplicon data using compositional methods (e.g., CLR/RPCA on un-multiplied reads or normalized by 5.8S qPCR load).
* **HOW:** Generate a sensitivity table and re-run the Figure 5 and Figure 6 ML models excluding all 57 taxa flagged by `decontam` in TCGA and all 32 taxa flagged in UCSD, regardless of whether they appear in HMP or clinical literature. For WIS, replace the post-PCR gel dilution multiplier with compositional log-ratios or spike-in/5.8S qPCR-calibrated absolute abundances, and verify that differential prevalence results in Figure 6 hold at $\text{FDR} \le 0.05$ rather than $\text{FDR} \le 0.20$.

#### 4. Correct Confidence Interval Estimation and Multivariable Survival Modeling
* **WHY:** Computing 99% confidence intervals across 10 repeats of 10-fold CV on the same dataset treats identical data resamples as independent cohorts, severely underestimating variance. Unadjusted Kaplan-Meier log-rank tests on small WIS subgroups ($n = 9$ and $n = 11$) are confounded by age, stage, and molecular subtype.
* **WHAT:** Compute valid out-of-fold confidence intervals (e.g., via DeLong's method on pooled out-of-fold predictions or Nadeau-Bengio variance-corrected $t$-intervals) and fit multivariable Cox proportional hazards models in WIS and TCGA.
* **HOW:** In Figures 6H, 6J, and 6K, replace the 10-repeat SD/CI ribbons with 95% confidence intervals computed via DeLong's test (or 1,000 patient-level bootstrap resamples) on a single out-of-fold prediction vector. For Figures 6C and 6F, fit Cox models adjusting for patient age, tumor stage, and HER2 status (in breast cancer), and report the adjusted hazard ratios, 95% confidence intervals, and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted $p$-values. In TCGA (Figure 4D), fit a Cox model stratified by cancer type that includes both the Thorsson et al. immune subtype (C1–C6) and the fungal F1/F2 log-ratio to test whether fungal log-ratios provide prognostic value independent of host transcriptomic immune subtype.

#### 5. Technical, Mathematical, and Editorial Cleanup
* **WHY:** Several equations and methodological definitions in the STAR Methods contain mathematical typos or inverted inequalities that obscure what was actually computed.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  * **STAR Methods ("WIS cohort: Construction and analysis of the multi-domain interaction networks"):** The equation for Normalized Mutual Information is written as `NMI(X, Y) = I(X, Y) H(X) H(Y)` (a product), which violates the stated bound $[0, 1]$. Correct the formula to $NMI(X, Y) = \frac{I(X, Y)}{\sqrt{H(X)H(Y)}}$ (or $\frac{2 I(X,Y)}{H(X)+H(Y)}$, matching the exact MATLAB implementation).
  * **STAR Methods ("TCGA cohort: Co-occurrence analyses with MMvec"):** Correct the definition of $Q^2$ from `1 - model coefficient of variation [CV] / null model CV` to `1 - model cross-validation error / null model cross-validation error` (in MMvec, CV denotes cross-validation loss, not coefficient of variation).
  * **STAR Methods ("TCGA cohort: Co-occurrence analyses with MMvec"):** Clarify the center-consistency filter currently written as "features whose median co-occurrence values were less than the standard mean error (SEM) of their co-occurrence values across centers." A feature is consistent across centers when its SEM across centers is small relative to the magnitude of its median ($|\text{median}| > \text{SEM}$), not when the median is less than the SEM. State the exact mathematical inequality used in the code.
  * **STAR Methods ("TCGA, Hopkins, and UCSD cohorts: Bioinformatic processing"):** Remove the duplicated text in "suggesting; see below), suggesting that marker-based taxonomy classification methods may be limited in very low-biomass settings."
