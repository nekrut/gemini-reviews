### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript investigates the evolutionary divergence of gene regulation between the house mouse subspecies *Mus musculus musculus* and *M. m. domesticus* and its relationship to asymmetric F1 hybrid male sterility. Using an experimental cross design that pairs intrasubspecific F1 controls (*M. m. musculus* PWK $\times$ CZII and *M. m. domesticus* LEWES $\times$ WSB) to avoid inbreeding depression with reciprocal intersubspecific F1 hybrids (fertile $\text{LEWES}\female \times \text{PWK}\male$ and sterile $\text{PWK}\female \times \text{LEWES}\male$, $n=3$ biological replicates per genotype), the authors sequence whole-testis mRNA (mean 7.5 Gb per sample, 100 bp paired-end reads, NCBI BioProject `PRJNA286765`). Analyzing allele-specific expression (ASE) across 9,851 testable autosomal genes in the fertile hybrid and pure subspecies, they classify regulatory divergence into *cis*-only (24%), *trans*-only (9%), and combined *cis* and *trans* changes (44%), reporting a strong excess of opposing (*cis–trans* compensatory) changes over reinforcing changes ($P < 0.0001$, Table 2) and directional *cis*-upregulation of transcriptional regulator GO terms in *M. m. musculus*. Comparing sterile and fertile F1 males, they confirm widespread overexpression of the *M. m. musculus* X chromosome in sterile hybrids (Fig. 2), identify 63 strictly misexpressed ($>1.25\ \log_2$ fold change) and 202 "aberrantly expressed" ($>1.0\ \log_2$ fold change) autosomal genes in sterile testes, and report that these sterility-associated misexpressed genes are significantly enriched for opposing/compensatory *cis–trans* divergence ($P < 0.0001$, Table 4, Fig. 3).

The experimental cross design—using intrasubspecific outbred F1s (`PWK x CZII` and `LEWES x WSB`) as the parental baseline alongside reciprocal fertile and sterile intersubspecific F1s—is biologically thoughtful and eliminates inbreeding depression and heterosis confounders common in hybrid expression studies. However, the statistical analysis of *cis* and *trans* evolution suffers from a well-known mathematical artifact (Fraser 2019; Zhang and Emerson 2019): because the *trans* effect is estimated as the difference between the parental log-ratio and the hybrid allelic log-ratio ($\widehat{\text{trans}} = \log_2(P_{\text{dom}}/P_{\text{mus}}) - \log_2(H_{\text{dom}}/H_{\text{mus}})$), the shared estimation error in the hybrid allelic ratio $\log_2(H_{\text{dom}}/H_{\text{mus}})$ enters $\widehat{\text{cis}}$ with a positive sign and $\widehat{\text{trans}}$ with a negative sign. Consequently, random sampling variance automatically induces a negative covariance ($\text{Cov}(\hat{c}, \hat{t}) = -\text{Var}(\hat{H}) < 0$), artificially inflating the proportion of genes classified as having "opposing" (*cis* + *trans* opposite, compensatory, and *cis* $\times$ *trans*) changes relative to a naive neutral expectation. Furthermore, pooling reads across the three biological replicates and applying binomial and Fisher's exact tests on downsampled read counts ignores biological overdispersion across mice, inflating false-positive classifications. Both flaws can be corrected directly on the authors' existing RNA-seq dataset by using cross-replicate split estimation and negative-binomial generalized linear models.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Asymmetric overexpression of the X chromosome in sterile ($\text{PWK}\female \times \text{LEWES}\male$) but not fertile ($\text{LEWES}\female \times \text{PWK}\male$) F1 hybrid testes: [Well Supported].**  
  The reciprocal F1 design cleanly separates X-linked misexpression in sterile males from general hybridization effects ($P < 0.0001$, Fig. 2, Supplemental Tables S3–S4), and this result is concordant with earlier microarray studies and stage-sorted germ-cell analyses showing disrupted meiotic sex chromosome inactivation (MSCI).

* **Extensive *cis*-regulatory divergence between *M. m. musculus* and *M. m. domesticus* that replicates across a larger panel of 15 wild-derived inbred lines: [Well Supported].**  
  Pure *cis* changes (24% of testable genes) have larger median effect sizes than pure *trans* changes (0.65 vs. 0.58 $\log_2$ fold change, $P = 0.00019$) and show highly significant overlap and directional concordance with an independent 15-line testis RNA-seq dataset from Phifer-Rixey et al. 2014 ($P = 1.75 \times 10^{-16}$).

* **Widespread compensatory evolution inferred from an excess of opposing over reinforcing *cis* and *trans* changes ($P < 0.0001$): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  Following the Wittkopp/McManus framework, the authors estimate $\text{cis} = H$ (hybrid allelic ratio) and $\text{trans} = P - H$ (parental ratio minus hybrid ratio) using the *same* hybrid replicates. Whenever there is measurement or sampling noise $\epsilon_H$ in the hybrid allelic ratio ($\hat{H} = H + \epsilon_H$), the estimated *cis* effect is $\hat{c} = H + \epsilon_H$ and the estimated *trans* effect is $\hat{t} = P - H - \epsilon_H$. Under pure null noise ($H = P = 0$), $\hat{c} = +\epsilon_H$ and $\hat{t} = -\epsilon_H$ are perfectly negatively correlated, systematically placing noisy genes into the "compensatory" ($P \approx 0, H \ne 0$, where $\hat{c}$ and $\hat{t}$ have opposite signs) and "*cis* + *trans* opposing" categories. This statistical coupling is exacerbated by pooling reads across the 3 biological replicates and running exact binomial tests (Methods, line 7, chunk 2), which treats thousands of sequencing reads as independent Bernoulli trials without accounting for inter-individual biological variance.

* **Genes uniquely misexpressed in sterile hybrids are significantly enriched for opposing/compensatory *cis–trans* regulatory divergence, linking compensatory coevolution to Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibilities: [Fragile].**  
  While biologically plausible, this enrichment test (Table 4, Fig. 3) is confounded by two factors: (1) the compensatory category itself is inflated by the $\text{Cov}(\hat{c}, \hat{t}) < 0$ estimation artifact described above, which disproportionately affects genes with higher expression variance across individuals—the very genes most likely to exceed a fold-change threshold in the sterile hybrid; and (2) as acknowledged in the Discussion, whole-testis RNA-seq in sterile males reflects altered germ-cell stage composition (incomplete pachytene arrest and reduced post-meiotic spermatids). If genes expressed in late spermatogenesis have different expression levels or allelic noise in fertile F1s than somatic/early meiotic genes, stage-composition shifts in sterile testes can create a spurious correlation between apparent sterile "misexpression" and fertile-hybrid ASE categories.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Eliminate the Negative Covariance Artifact Between $\widehat{\text{cis}}$ and $\widehat{\text{trans}}$ Using Independent Replicate Splitting
* **WHY:** Estimating $\widehat{\text{cis}} = \hat{H}$ and $\widehat{\text{trans}} = \hat{P} - \hat{H}$ from the same hybrid RNA-seq samples induces a mathematical negative covariance $\text{Cov}(\hat{c}, \hat{t}) = -\text{Var}(\hat{H})$, which artificially creates "opposing" (*cis* vs. *trans*) and "compensatory" calls under the null hypothesis.
* **WHAT:** Re-estimate $\widehat{\text{cis}}$ and $\widehat{\text{trans}}$ using independent biological replicates of the fertile hybrid so that the measurement errors of $\hat{c}$ and $\hat{t}$ are statistically independent ($\text{Cov}(\hat{c}, \hat{t}) = 0$).
* **HOW:** With $n=3$ fertile hybrid replicates ($H_1, H_2, H_3$), use one subset of hybrid replicates (e.g., $H_1$, or average across all $\binom{3}{1}$ split permutations) to estimate the *cis* divergence $\hat{c} = H_1$, and use a completely non-overlapping subset of hybrid replicates (e.g., $H_2, H_3$) along with the parental replicates to estimate the *trans* divergence $\hat{t} = P - H_{2,3}$. Re-run the directional opposition test in Table 2 and the sterile misexpression enrichment test in Table 4 and Fig. 3 using these cross-replicate independent estimates.

#### 2. Replace Pooled Binomial/Fisher's Exact Tests with Overdispersed Negative-Binomial or Beta-Binomial Models Across Biological Replicates
* **WHY:** In the Methods ("Regulatory divergence", line 7, chunk 2), reads are downsampled and pooled across the three individuals per genotype, and genes are classified using a binomial test on pooled read counts and Fisher's exact test. Pooling reads across animals treats read-level Poisson sampling as the only source of variance and ignores biological variance (overdispersion) among the 3 mice per group, leading to severe anti-conservative $p$-values for highly expressed genes.
* **WHAT:** Re-analyze parental differential expression and hybrid allele-specific expression using a replicate-aware statistical model (such as `DESeq2`, `edgeR`, or a beta-binomial GLM) that models biological variance across the $n=3$ replicates.
* **HOW:** Input the unpooled 3-replicate $\times$ 2-allele count matrices for parents and fertile hybrids into a negative-binomial GLM (`~ genotype + allele + genotype:allele`, where the `allele` main effect in hybrids tests *cis*, and the `genotype:allele` interaction term tests *trans* while propagating replicate-level dispersion), and report the updated proportions of *cis*-only, *trans*-only, and *cis* + *trans* genes in Fig. 1B.

#### 3. Account for Testis Cell-Type Stage Composition in the Sterile Misexpression vs. Compensatory Enrichment Test
* **WHY:** Sterile $\text{PWK}\female \times \text{LEWES}\male$ males have significantly reduced testis weights and lower post-meiotic sperm counts due to partial meiotic arrest (Supplemental Table S16). In whole-testis bulk RNA-seq, reduced abundance of post-meiotic round/elongating spermatids causes post-meiotic transcripts to appear "underexpressed" and somatic Sertoli/Leydig and early spermatogonial transcripts to appear "overexpressed" (which matches the observed significant excess of overexpressed autosomal genes in sterile hybrids, $P = 0.0006$, Supplemental Table S6).
* **WHAT:** Stratify the 63 misexpressed and 202 aberrantly expressed autosomal genes by their primary spermatogenic cell stage (using the flow-sorted germ-cell expression datasets from Campbell et al. 2013 or Chalmel et al. / Namekawa et al. cited in the mouse testis literature) and test whether the enrichment of compensatory *cis–trans* changes (Table 4, Fig. 3) holds within individual spermatogenic cell stages.
* **HOW:** Annotate each of the 9,851 testable autosomal genes by its peak expression stage (somatic, mitotic spermatogonia, pachytene spermatocytes, post-meiotic round spermatids), include stage as a covariate in a logistic regression predicting sterile misexpression from compensatory regulatory status, and verify whether the odds ratio remains significant after controlling for spermatogenic stage.

#### 4. Control for Intrasubspecific Heterozygosity (`PWK/CZII` vs. `LEWES/WSB`) When Mapping and Assigning Subspecies Reads
* **WHY:** Because the "pure subspecies" samples are actually intrasubspecific F1 hybrids ($\text{PWK} \times \text{CZII}$ for *M. m. musculus* and $\text{LEWES} \times \text{WSB}$ for *M. m. domesticus*), each parental individual carries two distinct haplotypes (e.g., PWK and CZII), whereas the intersubspecific F1 hybrid carries only the maternal and paternal haplotypes ($\text{PWK}$ and $\text{LEWES}$). Therefore, in the *cis*/*trans* comparison, the parental expression ratio compares $(\text{PWK}+\text{CZII})/2$ against $(\text{LEWES}+\text{WSB})/2$, whereas the F1 hybrid allelic ratio compares $\text{PWK}$ against $\text{LEWES}$. Any *cis*-regulatory polymorphism segregating *within* a subspecies (between PWK and CZII, or between LEWES and WSB) will cause $P \ne H$ and will be misclassified as a *trans*-regulatory difference between subspecies!
* **WHAT:** Acknowledge and quantify how within-subspecies *cis* polymorphism between PWK and CZII (or LEWES and WSB) affects the $\hat{P} - \hat{H}$ estimator of *trans* divergence.
* **HOW:** Use SNPs distinguishing PWK from CZII (in the $\text{PWK} \times \text{CZII}$ parental samples) and LEWES from WSB (in the $\text{LEWES} \times \text{WSB}$ parental samples) to measure within-subspecies allelic imbalance, or restrict the *cis*/*trans* classification to genes that do not show significant allelic imbalance between PWK and CZII or between LEWES and WSB.
