### 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY & CLAIM AUDIT TABLE (SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT)

This manuscript presents a large-scale meta-scientific evaluation of a de-identified large language model peer-review system (`gemini-4-argon` / `Gemini 3.1 Pro` operating with web search disabled under a 7-pillar, 10-point scoring rubric) across 1,580 full-text reviews of 1,466 unique open-access biomedical papers spanning six experiments: (1) a 200-paper calibration benchmark (50 retracted/refuted papers in Cohort A, 50 verified sound benchmark controls in Cohort B, and 100 contemporary wild papers in Cohort C, split into a 75-paper pilot and a 125-paper held-out expansion); (2) a 30-paper $\times$ 3-wave test-retest repeatability study; (3) an out-of-distribution (OOD) audit of 60 recent papers across ecology, neuroimaging, and AI drug discovery; (4) a peer-review value-added study across 20 matched preprint-to-published pairs and 100 published open-review papers (261 human referees, 363 substantive critiques); (5) a 35-year date-blinded longitudinal audit of 915 verified full-text articles (1991–2025) pooled with contemporary wild papers ($N=1,076$) linked to NIH RePORTER grant portfolios and journal Article Processing Charges (APCs); and (6) a 164-paper blinded self-audit of the senior author's career publications (2004–2026). Full review dossiers, SHA-256 input digests, and Python verification scripts are archived in an open repository.

The study's experimental architecture has clear strengths: deterministic XML scrubbing of author/venue/date metadata directly quantifies the $+0.35$-point prestige halo ($5.92 \to 5.57$) and the $+1.19$-point retraction-banner penalty ($3.46 \to 4.65$); the held-out 125-paper calibration wave confirms strong diagnostic separation ($\text{ROC-AUC} = 0.9824$, $\widehat{\text{Sp}}_{<5.0} = 100\%$); test-retest reliability is formally quantified via Shrout–Fleiss two-way random-effects $\text{ICC}(2,1) = 0.8583$ in wild literature; perceived-decade nostalgia bias is ruled out via partial correlation holding publication year constant ($r = -0.009, p = 0.83$); and the 164-paper self-audit candidly documents severe table copy-paste errors, alignment-gap denominator errors, and multiple-testing fallacies in the authors' own work. However, our forensic audit identified seven methodological, statistical, and numerical consistency issues that require revision before publication: (i) an untested group $\times$ revision interaction in the 20-pair preprint-to-published experiment (committing the Nieuwenhuis et al. difference-in-significance fallacy where the actual two-sample difference in gains yields $t \approx 1.61, p \approx 0.13$); (ii) severe intra-window calendar-year clustering in the 35-year Europe PMC sample where 129 of the 144 papers ($89.6\%$) in the `2016–2020` window came from the single pandemic year 2020 and 80 of 144 in `2006–2010` came from 2010, distorting the decade-level summaries in the main text relative to Table S4; (iii) post-hoc locus conditioning and threshold mixing in the Abstract's "up to 89%" sensitivity claim, alongside a direct set-definition contradiction between the main text and Supplementary Section S1; (iv) unpropagated binomial uncertainty and spectrum bias in the Rogan–Gladen defect prevalence estimator; (v) small-sample ($n=3$ trial pairs) overclaiming in the clinical-trial replication (Table S7) and single-LLM self-adjudication in Stage 2 of the open-review concordance audit; (vi) unadjusted temporal collinearity and AI label-score circularity in the $N=1,076$ covariate comparisons; and (vii) multiple numerical discrepancies between the main text and Supplementary Tables S1–S6 (`1,466` vs. `1,480` manuscripts; `1,580` vs. `1,568` reviews; `160` vs. `161` wild papers; `422` vs. `459` megajournal papers in the same $N=1,076$ cohort; and `650,000` vs. `1,965,335` annual PubMed articles in 2025). All issues can be resolved through statistical re-analysis, multivariable regression, and text/table reconciliation using the existing dataset.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT (MAJOR & MINOR METHODOLOGICAL CRITIQUES)

* **Claim 1 (Calibration Accuracy, Prestige Halo Removal, and Upstream Locus Exclusions): `[Well Supported]` for primary calibration ($\text{ROC-AUC}=0.9688$ full, $0.9824$ held-out, $\widehat{\text{Sp}}_{<5.0}=100\%$); `[Overstated]` for the combined "100% specificity / up to 89% sensitivity" headline and inconsistent 5-vs.-6 upstream locus definitions.**  
  On the complete 200-paper calibration cohort ($N_A=50, N_B=50$), the blinded reviewer achieves $\text{ROC-AUC}=0.9688$ ($0.9824$ on the strictly held-out 125-paper Phase-2 expansion, Table S1), with $\widehat{\text{Sp}}_{<5.0} = 100.0\%$ ($50/50$) and $\widehat{\text{Se}}_{<5.0} = 66.0\%$ ($33/50$). Robustness checks excluding the 13 refutation controls ($\text{ROC-AUC}=0.9635$) or the 7 senior-author controls in Cohort B ($\text{ROC-AUC}=0.9635$, Table S1 lines 419–420) confirm that calibration is not driven by self-authored benchmarks. However, three forensic issues affect the presentation of sensitivity:
  1. **Cutoff mixing in the Abstract and Table 1:** The Abstract states that the reviewer *"passed 100% of verified sound studies at our failure cutoff while catching up to 89% of retracted or refuted papers"* (line 7). At the $<5.0$ failure cutoff where specificity is $100\%$, sensitivity is $66.0\%$ on all 50 Cohort A papers and $75.6\%$ ($34/45$) after excluding 5 upstream-database papers (Table S1 lines 412, 416). The $88.9\%$ ("up to $89\%$") sensitivity figure requires simultaneously relaxing the threshold from $<5.0$ to $<6.0$ (where full-cohort specificity drops to $98.0\%$, or $92.0\%$ in the Phase-1 pilot) *and* restricting Cohort A to the $N_A=45$ subset.
  2. **Post-hoc conditioning on test scores to define `defect_locus_pilot5`:** Line 70 states: *"Looking closely at the five refuted papers that scored above 7.0 under blind review revealed an important boundary of text-only auditing... When we set aside those five upstream-database cases, our blinded reviewer catches 76% of internally flawed papers at the strict $<5.0$ cutoff, 89% at $<6.0$, and 96% at $<6.5$, with a diagnostic accuracy of 0.99."* Defining an exclusion subset after inspecting which Cohort A papers scored $>7.0$ conditions on the outcome variable and inflates $\text{ROC-AUC}$ from $0.9688$ to $0.9926$.
  3. **Direct contradiction between Main Text (line 70) and Supplementary Section S1 (lines 339–341):** Line 70 claims that the 5 excluded upstream papers (`Internal Flaws Only (Excl. 5 Upstream)`, $N_A=45$) consist of *"Three of those papers... applied them to public summary tables from the GIANT genetics consortium \cite{Turchin2012,Berg2014,Field2016}"* plus *"The other two papers... \cite{Nejman2020,Rabosky2014}"* ($3 + 2 = 5$). Yet Supplementary Section S1 (lines 339–341) explicitly defines:
     - `defect_locus_giant_strict` ($N=47$): excludes the 3 GIANT height papers (`A11`, `A23`, `A24`);
     - `defect_locus_pilot5` ($N=45$): excludes the 5 papers (`A07`, `A11`, `A19`, `A24`, `A25`)—which includes only **two** of the three GIANT papers (`A11`, `A24`, omitting `A23`) plus **three** non-GIANT papers (`A07`, `A19`, `A25`);
     - `defect_locus_broad6` ($N=44$): excludes the union of all **six** papers (`A07`, `A11`, `A19`, `A23`, `A24`, `A25`).
     Thus, there are 6 upstream papers total ($3\text{ GIANT} + 3\text{ other}$), and `defect_locus_pilot5` ($N=45$) left one GIANT height paper (`A23`) inside the "Internal Flaws Only" stratum while excluding three non-GIANT papers.
     Finally, line 68 states *"In 95% of the flawed papers it flagged (38 of 40), the blinded AI pinpointed the exact statistical error"*; the denominator $40$ corresponds to $40/45$ ($88.9\%$) at $<6.0$ in `defect_locus_pilot5` (whereas across all $N_A=50$, $33$ papers scored $<5.0$ and $38$ scored $<6.0$), which is not explained where $38/40$ is introduced.

* **Claim 2 (Test-Retest Repeatability Across 30 Papers $\times$ 3 Blind Runs): `[Well Supported]`.**  
  The Shrout–Fleiss two-way random-effects absolute-agreement estimator $\text{ICC}(2,1)$ and consistency estimator $\text{ICC}(3,1)$ (Section S3 Eq. 4, Table S2) are appropriate for multi-run psychometric evaluation. Because pooling extreme calibration groups (Cohort A mean $4.42$ vs. Cohort B mean $8.64$) inflates total between-paper variance $\text{MS}_R$ (yielding pooled $\text{ICC}(2,1) = 0.9671$), the authors properly foreground the within-stratum uncurated wild estimate (Cohort C, $n=10 \times 3$, $\text{ICC}(2,1) = 0.8583$, $\text{ICC}(2,3) = 0.9479$, $\text{SD}_e = 0.630\text{ pts}$, threshold $\kappa = 0.729$). Minor caveats are that $n=10$ papers per stratum yields wide confidence intervals on per-stratum $\text{ICC}$ and Cohen's $\kappa$ (which should be added to Table S2), and Eq. (4) writes $\text{ICC}(2,K) = \frac{K\cdot\text{ICC}(2,1)}{1 + (K-1)\text{ICC}(2,1)}$, which is exact for $\text{ICC}(3,K)$ (Spearman–Brown) and holds for $\text{ICC}(2,K)$ only when between-wave variance equals residual variance ($\text{MS}_C \approx \text{MS}_E$).

* **Claim 3 (Superior Net Diagnostic Accuracy in the Brian et al. 2026 Clinical-Trial Replication): `[Overstated]`.**  
  In Supplementary Section S4 (line 390), the authors astutely critique Brian et al. (2026) because three top-ranked models (`Opus 5.5`, `Fable 5`, `Opus 5`) were evaluated on only $n=3$ initial trials (`N`, `I`, `M`) and omitted from the 55 expanded trials (`T01–T60`). Yet in Take-Home 1 (line 74) and Table S7 (line 629), the authors evaluate `gemini-4-argon` on only **$n=3$ trial pairs** (`T01–T03`, 6 texts total) and claim it achieved *"the highest net diagnostic accuracy of all tested models (Youden's $J = 0.82$)."* Across 3 trials ($3 \times 9 = 27$ major errors and $3 \times 14 = 42$ control error-category checks), the difference between `gemini-4-argon` ($J = +0.816$, sensitivity $84.0\%$, specificity $97.6\%$) and `GPT-6.1 Sol` ($J = +0.789$, sensitivity $92.6\%$, specificity $86.3\%$) is $\Delta J = +0.027$, which corresponds to less than a single error category across 3 trials and carries a standard error of $\sim 0.08$ ($p > 0.70$). Moreover, Table S7 reports `gemini-4-argon` sensitivity as `84.0% (7.56/9)` across $n=3$ trials; because an integer count $k/27$ across 3 single-pass trials can only produce numerators $k/3$ ending in `.00`, `.33`, or `.67` (as seen in all 12 comparison rows of Table S7), the caption or methods must clarify whether `7.56/9` reflects a 3-run ensemble ($68/81$ across 9 runs) or half-credit scoring ($22.68/27$).

* **Claim 4 (Contemporary Wild Literature Failure Rates and Rogan–Gladen True Defect Prevalence): `[Moderately Supported]`.**  
  The empirical gradient across the 160 contemporary wild papers (2020–2025)—ranging from Molecular Evolution ($6.90 \pm 1.51$, $5.0\% <5.0$) down to Single-Cell Transcriptomics ($4.33 \pm 1.56$, $75.0\% <5.0$) and from selective flagships ($7.2 \pm 1.6$, $14\%$ failed) to megajournals ($4.4 \pm 1.5$, $65\%$ failed)—is internally consistent and corroborated by downstream citation rates ($r = +0.45, p = 2.2 \times 10^{-9}$). However, two methodological issues affect this section:
  1. **Unpropagated uncertainty and spectrum bias in the Rogan–Gladen estimator:** Line 89 applies Eq. (3) ($\hat{\pi}_{\text{RG}} = \frac{p_{\text{obs}} + \widehat{\text{Sp}} - 1}{\widehat{\text{Se}} + \widehat{\text{Sp}} - 1}$) with $\widehat{\text{Sp}}_{<5.0} = 1.00$ and $\widehat{\text{Se}}_{<5.0} \approx 0.756$ to project that $55\%\text{–}65\%$ of recent open-access papers suffer from fundamental flaws. First, with $N_B = 50$ controls, $50/50$ specificity has an exact Clopper–Pearson 95% CI of $[0.9289, 1.0000]$ (and in the Phase-1 pilot `ANON_01–75` or with original pilot label `PMC8135569` in Cohort B, $\widehat{\text{Sp}}_{<5.0}$ was $96.0\%$ or $98.0\%$, Table S1 lines 413, 418). Second, Cohort B comprises landmark foundational methods and definitive refutation papers, whereas uncurated wild literature includes routine incremental studies. If effective specificity on sound incremental studies is $0.90\text{–}0.95$ rather than $1.00$, $\hat{\pi}_{\text{RG}}$ shifts downward by $7\text{–}15$ percentage points.
  2. **Unexplained two-stage evaluation of the 60 OOD papers:** Table S3 (lines 465–470) reports two sets of numbers for the 60 OOD papers: `Stage 1 (Single-Pass Evaluation)` ($4.99 \pm 1.92$, $48.3\% <5.0$) and `Stage-1+2 Consensus` ($4.88 \pm 1.88$, $55.0\% <5.0$). The main text (line 91) quotes the `Stage-1+2 Consensus` means and failure rates for the three OOD subfields ($5.2 / 55\%$, $4.9 / 50\%$, $4.6 / 60\%$) while comparing them directly against single-pass in-domain Cohort C subfields, and the Methods never explain what "Stage-1+2 Consensus" entailed for the OOD cohort.

* **Claim 5 (Selective Peer Review Adds Nearly Three Times More Value Than Megajournal Peer Review): `[Fragile]`.**  
  Across all $N=20$ matched preprint-to-published pairs, peer review significantly improved the 8-point Scientific Core by $+0.41$ points ($p = 0.0031$, $16/20$ improved). However, the headline conclusion that selective peer review adds nearly three times more value than megajournal peer review ($+0.59$ Core points, $p=0.013$, $n=10$ vs. $+0.22$ Core points, $p=0.12$, $n=10$; Abstract, Table 1, Take-Home 3 line 108) rests on comparing a significant within-stratum $p$-value ($p=0.013$) against a non-significant within-stratum $p$-value ($p=0.12$) without reporting a direct two-sample interaction test between the two arms ($\Delta_{\text{sel}} - \Delta_{\text{mega}}$). Reconstructing the standard errors from the reported paired $t$-tests on $df = 9$ ($\bar{d}_{\text{sel}} = 0.59, t_{9} \approx 3.09 \implies SE_{\text{sel}} \approx 0.191$; $\bar{d}_{\text{mega}} = 0.22, t_{9} \approx 1.71 \implies SE_{\text{mega}} \approx 0.129$) yields a standard error for the between-group difference of $SE_{\text{diff}} = \sqrt{0.191^2 + 0.129^2} \approx 0.230$. The resulting two-sample Welch $t$-statistic for $\Delta_{\text{sel}} - \Delta_{\text{mega}} = +0.37$ Core points is:
  $$t = \frac{0.59 - 0.22}{0.230} \approx 1.61 \quad (df \approx 15.8,\ \text{two-tailed } p \approx 0.127).$$
  Thus, at $n=10$ pairs per arm, the difference in peer-review gain between selective journals and megajournals is not statistically significant at $\alpha = 0.05$.

* **Claim 6 (100-Paper Open-Review Concordance, 97.9% Unresolved Recall, and 58% Megajournal Referee Anomalies): `[Moderately Supported]`.**  
  The 100-paper open-review experiment provides strong empirical evidence of a quality gap between selective open-review venues ($7.49 \pm 1.38$, $8.0\% <5.0$, $n=50$) and open-review megajournal papers ($5.01 \pm 1.69$, $48.0\% <5.0$, $n=50$, $p = 2.1 \times 10^{-12}$), and the specific forensic examples of human reviewer failures (`OPEN_ANON_011` inverted odds ratios and CI outside point estimate; `OPEN_ANON_036` $121.7\text{ mg/mL}$ melanin in $100\text{ mg/mL}$ tissue; `OPEN_ANON_035` revision-induced $p = -0.006$; `OPEN_ANON_049` pasted LLM prompt in a decision letter) are concrete and verifiable. Two methodological limitations should be explicitly qualified:
  1. **Single-venue megajournal sampling:** All 50 open-review megajournal papers were sampled from a single journal (*PLOS ONE*, line 110), whereas the selective arm spanned five journals (*eLife*, *PLOS Biology*, *PLOS Genetics*, *PLOS Pathogens*, *PLOS Computational Biology*). The $58.0\%$ referee anomaly rate ($38.0\%$ superficial checklists, $12.0\%$ thorough) reflects *PLOS ONE* (2020–2025) specifically and should not be stated as an unweighted estimate across all commercial megajournals (*Scientific Reports*, *Frontiers*, *MDPI*, *PeerJ*) without venue qualification.
  2. **Single-LLM self-concordance grading in Stage 2:** According to the AI Disclosure Statement (line 270), Stage 2 human-concordance audits—which classified whether `gemini-4-argon`'s Stage 1 review recalled each of the 363 human critiques ($97.9\%$ unresolved recall, $78.2\%$ overall) and tallied $385$ "AI-only forensic catches"—were executed by instances of `gemini-4-argon`. Having the evaluated model adjudicate its own recall and count its own uncorroborated critiques without an independent human-blinded audit on a stratified subsample risks inflating partial-match recall and counting benign stylistic critiques among the 385 AI-only catches.

* **Claim 7 (35-Year Secular Decline Driven by Compositional Shifts vs. Modest Within-Journal Drift): `[Moderately Supported]` (Requires Correction of Severe Intra-Window Calendar-Year Clustering).**  
  The date-blinded evaluation of $N=915$ verified full-text papers (after excluding 85 abstract-only XML stubs), the check showing zero partial correlation between AI-perceived decade and score controlling for publication year ($r = -0.009, p = 0.83$, $n=543$), and the within-journal fixed-effect regression across 30 recurring journals ($n=549$, slope $-0.23\text{ pts/decade}$, $p=0.027$) are well-designed controls. However, auditing the sample sizes and means between the main text (line 130) and Supplementary Table S4 (lines 494–500) exposes a critical sampling distortion:
  1. **Severe intra-window calendar-year clustering and mismatch between 5-year windows and calendar decades:** In Table S4 (lines 494–500), the seven 5-year windows have sample sizes $n = 73$ (`1991–1995`), $124$ (`1996–2000`), $142$ (`2001–2005`), $144$ (`2006–2010`), $144$ (`2011–2015`), $144$ (`2016–2020`), and $144$ (`2021–2025`), summing to $915$. In the main text (line 130), the same 915 papers are summarized by calendar decade: 1990s ($n=154$, mean $7.63$), 2000s ($n=249$, mean $6.76$), 2010s ($n=239$, mean $6.56$, $18.4\% <5.0$), and 2020s ($n=273$, mean $5.81$, $34.8\% <5.0$), also summing to $154 + 249 + 239 + 273 = 915$. Because boundary years `2000`, `2010`, and `2020` sit at the end of the `1996–2000`, `2006–2010`, and `2016–2020` 5-year windows but belong to the `2000s`, `2010s`, and `2020s` calendar decades, exact conservation of $N=915$ reveals the number of papers sampled in those boundary years:
     - Year `2000`: $(73 + 124) - 154 = 43$ of the $124$ papers in `1996–2000` ($34.7\%$) came from the single year 2000 (which also explains how the 1990s mean of $7.63$ on $n=154$ exceeds *both* the `1991–1995` mean of $7.61$ and the `1996–2000` mean of $7.47$, and how the 1990s failure rate of $3.9\%$ [$6/154$] is lower than both `1991–1995` [$6.8\%, 5/73$] and `1996–2000` [$4.0\%, 5/124$]);
     - Year `2010`: $(197 + 142 + 144) - (154 + 249) = 80$ of the $144$ papers in `2006–2010` ($55.6\%$) came from the single year 2010;
     - Year `2020`: $273\text{ (2020s)} - 144\text{ (2021–2025)} = \mathbf{129}$ **of the 144 papers in `2016–2020` ($89.6\%$) came from the single pandemic year 2020**, leaving only $144 - 129 = 15$ papers across the four years `2016–2019`!
