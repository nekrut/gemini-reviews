### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript characterizes the low-biomass bacterial microbiome across 1,526 human samples (1,010 solid tumors and 516 normal or normal-adjacent tissues [NAT]) spanning seven cancer types (breast, lung, ovary, pancreas, melanoma, bone, and glioblastoma multiforme [GBM]), alongside 811 negative controls (437 DNA extraction blanks, 206 PCR no-template controls, and 168 tissue-free paraffin block margins). Using 16S rDNA qPCR, a multiplexed 5-region (5R) 16S rDNA amplicon sequencing pipeline reconstructed with SMURF against an RDP-augmented Greengenes database, a six-stage statistical decontamination filter, orthogonal visualization on $>400$ tissue cores (IHC for LPS and LTA, 16S rRNA FISH, and correlative light and electron microscopy [CLEM]), ex vivo fluorescent D-alanine incorporation ($n = 4$), and aerobic/anaerobic culturomics on fresh breast tumors ($n = 5$ tumors, 350 PBS negative control plates), the authors report that solid tumors harbor predominantly intracellular, cell-wall-deficient (L-form-like) bacteria within cancer and CD45+ immune cells, that breast tumors exhibit the highest species richness (mean 16.4 species/tumor), and that PICRUSt2-inferred bacterial pathways correlate with clinical phenotypes (e.g., smoke-chemical degradation in smokers' lung tumors, ER+ status in breast cancer, and immune checkpoint inhibitor [ICI] response in melanoma). Raw sequencing data (PRJNA624822) and Zenodo code repositories (refs. 63, 64) are publicly deposited.

The multi-modal experimental design—combining 811 negative sequencing controls, paraffin-margin controls, multi-center enrichment filters, CLEM, D-alanine metabolic labeling, and 350 control culture plates—represents a serious effort to overcome the notorious contamination pitfalls of low-biomass microbiome research. However, several statistical and biological limitations temper the downstream taxonomic and functional conclusions: (1) SMURF probabilistic reconstruction across five short, discontinuous 16S amplicons mapped to an obsolete Greengenes reference database with computationally imputed species labels (380,000 sequences assigned species taxonomy via RDP) can generate chimeric or over-split species calls ("9,190 bacterial species detected"), which explains why 40.5% of cultured whole-genome-sequenced isolates failed the six bioinformatics filters and 22% (105/474 colonies) could not be matched at the species level; (2) tissue-type-specific survivor bias in Filters 1–6 (where species are filtered independently per tumor type) artificially inflates beta-diversity separation and PCoA clustering between tumor types (Fig. 4A, F); (3) PICRUSt2 functional predictions from fragmented 5R 16S reconstructions are treated as known gene content without propagating phylogenetic imputation error; and (4) widespread use of a permissive false discovery rate threshold ($Q < 0.25$) and unadjusted paired/unpaired proportion tests inflates differential prevalence hits. Addressing these points computationally will tighten the quantitative claims.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Bacterial DNA, 16S rRNA, and LPS are detectable above background controls in solid human tumors and localize intracellularly within cancer and immune cells: [Well Supported].**  
  This core finding is supported by converging lines of evidence: 16S qPCR above the 99th percentile of 437 extraction and 168 paraffin controls ($P < 10^{-10}$, Fig. 1B), colocalized LPS IHC and 16S rRNA FISH staining with negative probe/antibody controls across $>400$ tumor cores (Fig. 1C–D, Fig. 2A–D), CLEM imaging in 4 breast tumors (Fig. 2E), intracellular fluorescent D-alanine incorporation in 4 ex vivo breast tumors (Fig. 3G), and $>1,000$ colony-forming units grown from 4/5 fresh breast tumors vs. only 5 colonies total across 350 PBS control plates (line 59).

* **Each tumor type has a distinct species-level microbiome composition (Fig. 4A, F), with breast tumors harboring an average of 16.4 distinct species out of 9,190 total detected species: [Moderately Supported].**  
  While the higher bacterial load and richness in breast tumors are consistent across rarefaction curves (Fig. 3C–F), two methodological artifacts affect the exact species counts and between-tumor separation:
  1. *Per-condition filtering circularity in beta-diversity (Pillar 2 & 3):* In line 55 and the Fig. 4A/4F captions, the authors state: *"Only bacteria that passed all six filters in a specific cancer type or its NAT were considered to be hits that are present in this cancer or NAT condition."* If a bacterial species is present at low prevalence across five tumor types, but only crosses the statistical enrichment threshold of Filters 2–6 in one tumor type (say, breast cancer, which has the largest sample size in Fig. 1A), its abundance is zeroed out in the other four tumor types. Computing Jaccard similarity and PCoA (Fig. 4A, F) on matrices that have been zero-masked conditioned on the tumor-type label itself mathematically guarantees that samples cluster by tumor type!
  2. *SMURF + Greengenes species inflation (Pillar 3):* Reconstructing full-length 16S profiles from five short PCR amplicons in low-biomass FFPE tissue via SMURF and mapping to Greengenes (augmented with RDP species predictions) is prone to multi-template chimeras and database over-splitting. Notably, in the breast tumor culturomics validation (line 59), only 11 of 37 (29.7%) WGS-verified cultured species were called as filtered "hits" in the 5R cohort, whereas 15 (40.5%) were discarded by the filters.

* **Intratumor bacteria in cancer cells exist in a cell wall–deficient (L-form-like) state: [Fragile].**  
  The authors infer a cell wall–deficient state because classical rods/cocci were seen in only 3/426 cores by FISH, LTA IHC was negative in cancer cells despite Gram-positive 16S reads, and TEM shapes were irregular (line 42). However, in line 61 and Fig. 3G, the authors validate live intracellular bacteria in breast tumor cells using **fluorescent D-alanine incorporation**, explicitly noting that *"D-alanine is used by bacteria to generate peptidoglycan, an essential component of the bacterial cell wall."* Active synthesis of peptidoglycan via D-alanine incorporation in intracellular tumor bacteria is in direct mechanistic tension with the claim that these same intracellular bacteria lack peptidoglycan cell walls (Pillar 3 & 6). Poor LTA epitope preservation in FFPE or lower LTA antibody affinity relative to LPS is a more parsimonious explanation for negative LTA staining in cancer cells.

* **PICRUSt2-predicted bacterial metabolic pathways (e.g., cigarette smoke degradation and plant metabolites in smokers' lungs; arsenate/mycothiol pathways in ER+ breast cancer) and melanoma ICI responder taxa reflect microenvironment selection: [Overstated].**  
  1. *Unpropagated PICRUSt2 imputation uncertainty and Cyanobacteria/chloroplast artifacts (Pillar 3 & 4):* PICRUSt2 infers gene content solely from 16S reference phylogenies; it does not measure metagenomic gene presence. In smokers' lung tumors, the authors find enrichment of "plant-related metabolic pathways... e.g., plant photorespiration" mapped to *Cyanobacteria* (lines 89–91, Fig. 5B, D). In 16S amplicon sequencing, plant chloroplast 16S rDNA (from inhaled tobacco leaf dust in current smokers) amplifies with universal bacterial 16S primers and classifies within the *Cyanobacteria* phylum! Attributing tobacco chloroplast 16S DNA or dead inhaled tobacco-leaf epiphytes to metabolically active intratumoral bacteria selecting for niche survival overstates the evidence.
  2. *Permissive FDR ($Q < 0.25$) and unadjusted confounding (Pillar 4):* Across Figs. 4E, 4G, and 5B–F, significance is claimed at an FDR threshold of $Q < 0.25$ (meaning up to 1 in 4 reported associations are false positives), and in smokers vs. never-smokers (Fig. 5C) *zero* individual bacterial taxa survive FDR correction. Similarly, the melanoma ICI responder ($n = 29$) vs. non-responder ($n = 48$) binomial test (Fig. 5F) does not control for clinical covariates (e.g., tumor mutational burden, prior therapy lines, anatomical biopsy site, or medical center batch).

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Eliminate Condition-Specific Zero-Masking Before Computing Between-Tumor Beta-Diversity (Fig. 4A, F)
* **WHY:** Zeroing out species that did not pass Filters 2–6 *within* each individual tumor type prior to calculating pairwise Jaccard similarities (Fig. 4A, F) introduces supervised label leakage into an unsupervised clustering analysis, especially because sample sizes vary widely across tumor types (e.g., hundreds of breast and lung samples vs. smaller bone/melanoma/GBM cohorts in Fig. 1A).
* **WHAT:** Recompute the Jaccard similarity matrix (Fig. 4A) and PCoA biplot (Fig. 4F) using a **unified global species filter** (all species that pass contamination filters across the study as a whole, without zeroing out species per tumor type) and rarefy to an equal number of samples per tumor type.
* **HOW:** Construct a single decontaminated species-by-sample presence/absence matrix across all 1,526 samples where the same set of globally passing species is retained for every sample regardless of its tumor label. Run PERMANOVA (`adonis2` in R package `vegan`) stratifying by medical center to report the variance explained ($R^2$ and $P$-value) by tumor type vs. collection center.

#### 2. Reconcile the Cell Wall–Deficient (L-Form) Hypothesis with D-Alanine Peptidoglycan Labeling
* **WHY:** Claiming that intracellular bacteria in cancer cells lack a cell wall (line 42) while simultaneously using fluorescent D-alanine incorporation into nascent peptidoglycan cell walls to prove live intracellular bacteria in breast tumor cells (line 61, Fig. 3G) is internally inconsistent.
* **WHAT:** Clarify in the text how D-alanine incorporation (which requires active peptidoglycan transpeptidation) relates to the proposed L-form / cell wall–deficient state, and acknowledge alternative explanations for the absence of LTA IHC staining in cancer cells.
* **HOW:** Revise lines 42 and 61 to note that intracellular bacteria may retain partial or modified peptidoglycan synthesis (sufficient for D-alanine labeling) while shedding or masking surface LTA epitopes, or that LTA IHC has a higher detection threshold than LPS IHC in FFPE sections.

#### 3. Test for Tobacco Chloroplast 16S Cross-Amplification in Smokers' Lung Tumors
* **WHY:** Universal 16S primers (including 5R) amplify plant plastid/chloroplast 16S rRNA genes, which taxonomically classify under the phylum *Cyanobacteria* in Greengenes. The simultaneous enrichment of *Cyanobacteria* and "plant photorespiration / plant metabolite biosynthesis" pathways in current smokers' lungs (lines 89–91, Fig. 5B, D) is a classic signature of inhaled *Nicotiana tabacum* chloroplast DNA or dead tobacco-leaf surface bacteria rather than metabolically active lung-resident bacteria.
* **WHAT:** Screen the *Cyanobacteria* 5R sequences detected in smokers' lung tumors against the *Nicotiana tabacum* plastid genome and report what fraction of the "plant metabolic pathway" signal in Fig. 5B/D is driven by chloroplast or dead tobacco-associated DNA.
* **HOW:** Align the representative 16S sequences assigned to *Cyanobacteria* in Table S11/S12 against NCBI RefSeq plastid genomes (`NC_001879.2`, *Nicotiana tabacum* chloroplast) using BLASTN, and repeat the PICRUSt2 smoker vs. never-smoker comparison after excluding plastid/mitochondrial and *Cyanobacteria* operational taxonomic units.

#### 4. Tighten False Discovery Rate Thresholds ($Q < 0.05$ or $0.10$) and Account for Patient-Level Pairing in Tumor vs. NAT Tests
* **WHY:** Using a two-sample independent proportion $z$-test to compare tumors against their matched NAT from the *same* patients (Fig. 4G) ignores within-patient correlation, and using $Q < 0.25$ across Figs. 4E, 4G, and 5B–F allows a high rate of false positives.
* **WHAT:** Re-analyze matched Tumor–NAT pairs using paired statistical tests (McNemar's test or mixed-effects logistic regression with patient ID as a random effect) and report which taxa and pathways survive standard $Q < 0.05$ and $Q < 0.10$ thresholds.
* **HOW:** For the subset of patients with matched Tumor and NAT samples (Table S1), run McNemar's test (`mcnemar.test` in R) for species prevalence differences, and annotate Figs. 4E, 4G, and 5B–F with standard significance tiers ($Q < 0.05$, $Q < 0.10$, and $Q < 0.25$).
