## 1. Executive Summary & Synthesis
The authors perform a genome-wide scan for positively selected genes (PSGs) along the human and chimpanzee lineages following their divergence from a common ancestor, using the rhesus macaque ($5.1\times$ draft genome) as an outgroup across 13,888 orthologous gene trios. By applying the PAML improved branch-site likelihood ratio test (Test 2) and calibrating base-quality masking ($Q \ge 20$) via the chimpanzee-to-human synonymous substitution ratio ($r = d_S^{\text{chimp}}/d_S^{\text{human}} = 0.985$), the study addresses sequencing-error inflation that affected earlier $4\times$ draft chimpanzee comparisons. The central evolutionary finding—that the chimpanzee lineage harbors significantly more positively selected genes than the human lineage (233 vs. 154 at nominal $P < 0.05$; 59 vs. 2 at 5% false discovery rate [FDR]), while non-PSGs show a slightly lower average $d_N/d_S$ ($\omega$) in chimpanzees ($0.238$) than in humans ($0.252$)—is coherently explained by population genetics theory: a larger long-term effective population size ($N_e$) in chimpanzees increases the efficacy of both adaptive and purifying selection. However, because nearly all downstream functional, tissue-expression, Mendelian disease (OMIM), and chromosomal rearrangement enrichments are conducted on the unadjusted $P < 0.05$ gene sets—where the authors' own FDR calculation implies that $\sim 97\%$ ($152/154$) of the human "PSGs" and $\sim 75\%$ ($174/233$) of the chimpanzee "PSGs" may be false positives under the null—several biological enrichment claims are statistically fragile.

## 2. Direct Appraisal of Core Claims
* **Claim 1: Base-quality masking ($Q \ge 20$) of the $6\times$ chimpanzee assembly eliminates sequencing-error bias, revealing significantly more PSGs in chimpanzees than in humans (233 vs. 154 at $P < 0.05$; 59 vs. 2 at 5% FDR)** — `[Well Supported]`
  The calibration of sequence quality using the synonymous substitution ratio $r = d_S^{\text{chimp}}/d_S^{\text{human}}$ (Table 1) is well conceived: $r$ drops from $1.045$ in the $4\times$ assembly without quality filtering ($Q0$) to $0.996$ ($Q0$) and $0.985$ ($Q20$) in the $6\times$ assembly, while the excess of chimpanzee over human PSGs persists across all thresholds ($\chi^2 = 16.12, P = 5.9 \times 10^{-5}$ at $Q20$; $59$ vs. $2$ at 5% FDR; $21$ vs. $2$ after Bonferroni correction, lines 44–61). The population genetic reconciliation with purifying selection—showing that mean $\omega$ across 13,505 non-PSGs is lower in chimpanzees ($0.238$) than in humans ($0.252$, paired $t$-test $P < 10^{-5}$, lines 98–100)—is theoretically sound and internally consistent with a larger ancestral/long-term $N_e$ in chimpanzees.
* **Claim 2: Human and chimpanzee PSGs are differentially enriched in PANTHER biological processes and molecular functions, and diverge in adult vs. fetal brain expression** — `[Fragile]`
  All functional category comparisons (Figure 1, lines 63–72) and microarray tissue-expression profiles (Figure 2, lines 74–76) are performed on the nominal $P < 0.05$ candidate sets ($154$ human and $233$ chimpanzee genes). However, at $P < 0.05$ across $13,888$ tests, up to $\sim 694$ false positives would be expected under a uniform null (though the mixture null $\frac{1}{2}\chi_0^2 + \frac{1}{2}\chi_1^2$ makes the conservative $\chi_1^2$ test yield fewer rejections). Crucially, the authors report at line 60 that only $2$ human genes and $59$ chimpanzee genes survive a 5% FDR threshold. Consequently, the $154$-gene human set is dominated by statistical noise, and the reported human vs. chimpanzee expression ratio divergence in fetal vs. adult brain ($P = 0.04$, Figure 2D) is not corrected for multiple testing across the 18 tissues and developmental stages examined.
* **Claim 3: Human PSGs are significantly enriched in OMIM human genetic disease genes compared with chimpanzee PSGs** — `[Fragile]`
  In Table 2 and lines 78–83, the authors report that $21.4\%$ ($33/154$) of human PSGs vs. $13.7\%$ ($32/233$) of chimpanzee PSGs are found in OMIM ($P = 0.049$ in the $6\times$ $Q20$ dataset; $P = 0.044$ in the $4\times$ $Q10$ dataset). Beyond the high false-discovery rate within the 154 human PSGs, these $P$-values sit right at the $\alpha = 0.05$ boundary, and in the unmasked $6\times$ $Q0$ dataset the difference is non-significant ($19.4\%$ vs. $15.7\%, P = 0.32$, Table 2). Moreover, an ascertainment bias exists because OMIM catalogs human phenotypes: human genes with functional coding variants or rapid evolution may be more intensively studied in clinical genetics than genes specifically under selection in chimpanzees.
* **Claim 4: Chimpanzee PSGs are enriched on evolutionarily rearranged chromosomes and in regions of higher local recombination ($P = 0.041$)** — `[Moderately Supported]`
  Figure 3A shows that rearranged chromosomes harbor a higher proportion of chimpanzee PSGs than colinear chromosomes ($\chi^2 = 5.65, P = 0.017$), and chimpanzee PSGs have higher local recombination rates than non-PSGs (one-tailed $t$-test $P = 0.041$, lines 85–95). While the Hill–Robertson effect provides a plausible explanation, local recombination rates also drive GC-biased gene conversion (gBGC), which accelerates $W \to S$ (`A/T -> G/C`) substitutions and is known to generate false-positive signals in branch-site likelihood ratio tests of $\omega > 1$ without true adaptive protein evolution.

## 3. Essential Revisions & Methodological Critiques
1. **Assess sensitivity of downstream biological enrichments to FDR control and stricter $P$-value thresholds:**
   - Because only 2 human PSGs survive 5% FDR (line 60), repeat the PANTHER functional analysis (Figure 1), tissue expression profiling (Figure 2), OMIM disease enrichment (Table 2), and chromosomal rearrangement analyses (Figure 3) across a range of stricter nominal cutoffs (e.g., $P < 0.01$ and $P < 0.005$, as well as the 59 FDR-controlled chimpanzee PSGs) to demonstrate that the biological conclusions are not driven by false-positive genes in the $0.01 < P < 0.05$ tail.
   - Apply multiple-testing corrections (Benjamini–Hochberg FDR) to the tissue expression comparisons across 18 tissues in Figure 2.
2. **Test and control for GC-biased gene conversion (gBGC) and multinucleotide mutations:**
   - Given that chimpanzee PSGs are associated with higher recombination rates ($P = 0.041$, line 95), stratify substitutions by weak-to-strong ($A/T \to G/C$) vs. strong-to-weak ($G/C \to A/T$) and AT-to-AT/GC-to-GC changes, or check whether $\omega_{W \to S}$ drives the likelihood ratio test statistics in the 233 chimpanzee PSGs.
   - Report whether closely spaced nonsynonymous substitutions or indels near exon boundaries (which frequently arise from alternative splicing discrepancies or local alignment errors in draft assemblies) cluster within the inferred foreground $\omega_2 > 1$ sites.
3. **Restore missing equation and clarify LRT degrees-of-freedom calibration:**
   - In Materials and Methods (line 138), the $\chi^2$ test formula comparing $x_i$ and $y_i$ in PANTHER categories is missing from the text (blank line between line 137 and 139); insert the explicit mathematical expression.
   - In the branch-site Test 2 description (lines 125–129), explicitly note whether $P$-values were computed using $\chi_1^2$ (conservative) or the $\frac{1}{2}\chi_0^2 + \frac{1}{2}\chi_1^2$ mixture distribution, as this halves all nominal $P$-values and directly affects the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR calculation.
