## 1. Summary of Claims & Epistemic Architecture

This computational mechanobiology manuscript couples a 0D Lotka–Volterra-style ordinary differential equation (ODE) model of the IGF1–AKT–mTOR–FOXO signaling pathway with a 3D transversely isotropic hyperelastic finite element model (implemented in `FEniCSx`) via the multiplicative volumetric growth decomposition ($\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{G}$) and the cumulative stress-unloading framework of Goriely and Ben Amar (2007). The authors simulate 9-week exercise regimens across cylindrical, idealized fusiform, and 12 anatomically realistic human lower-limb muscle geometries (BFLH, ST, TA from the Visible Human dataset) with Stokes-flow-derived fiber architectures.

### Primary Claims Audit
1. **Claim 1:** The computational framework "mechanistically integrates these mechanical and biochemical stimuli" via "bidirectional coupling" between tissue-level continuum mechanics and the IGF1–AKT–mTOR–FOXO pathway to predict exercise-induced skeletal muscle hypertrophy. `[Overstated]`
   - *Epistemic basis:* As the authors candidly admit in Methods line 296 (*"growth is driven directly by the signaling output of the ODE model rather than by local mechanical stimuli such as computed tissue stress or strain. Consequently, aggregate phenomenological parameters governing the whole-muscle exercise stimulus, such as $F_{\max}$, are decoupled from the maximum isometric stress parameter $\sigma_{\text{active}}$"*), **the 3D mechanical stress/strain state during active muscle contraction ($\alpha > 0$) never feeds into the signaling ODEs**. Instead, the ODE receives a globally uniform scalar square-wave input ($I_{\text{ex}} F_{\max}$), and the only feedback from 3D mechanics to the ODE is a global scalar integral—the mid-belly cross-sectional area $A_{CS}(t)$—modulating $k_M(t)$ via an ad hoc Hill function.
2. **Claim 2:** In the Lotka–Volterra signaling model, high-intensity, high-frequency (MWF) training produces the largest increase in myofibril density $N(t)$ via wave summation of mTOR and FOXO, and Sobol global sensitivity analysis identifies FOXO intrinsic growth ($a_F$) and self-inhibition ($b_F$) rates as the dominant parameters controlling net hypertrophy. `[Well Supported]`
   - *Epistemic basis:* Deterministic ODE numerical integration (`RK45`) and a 300,000-evaluation Sobol sensitivity analysis (`SALib`) across $[0.5, 2.0]\times$ parameter ranges.
3. **Claim 3:** In anatomically realistic muscle geometries (Visible Human BFLH, ST, TA), uniform transverse volumetric growth ($\mathbf{G} = G\mathbf{I} + (1-G)\mathbf{a}_0 \otimes \mathbf{a}_0$) produces spatially heterogeneous 3D displacements and consistently yields a larger relative increase in total muscle volume (~8–9%) than in mid-plane cross-sectional area (~3–6%). `[Fragile]`
   - *Epistemic basis:* As shown below in Section 2.1, this discrepancy is a direct mathematical consequence of a missing square-root in the kinematic definition of the transverse stretch multiplier $G(t)$ (Equations 9–10) combined with oblique fiber angles at the midplane and Robin boundary constraints.
4. **Claim 4:** When spatially uncorrelated element-wise parameter noise ($\pm 1\%$ uniform perturbation to all ODE rate constants) is introduced, elastic continuum compatibility acts as a "mechanical buffer" that smooths jagged local volume expansion ($J$) into a continuous macroscopic displacement field, while slightly reducing mean total growth compared to the homogeneous baseline. `[Well Supported]`
   - *Epistemic basis:* Elliptic PDE regularity of hyperelastic equilibrium ($\nabla \cdot \mathbf{P} = 0$) over $n=5$ stochastic mesh realizations (Figure 7).

## 2. Major Methodological & Statistical Flaws

### 2.1 Dimensional / Kinematic Factor-of-2 Error in the Transverse Growth Tensor $\mathbf{G}(t)$
In Equations (9) and (10) (lines 303–319), the authors assume that the myofibril population $N(t)$ is directly proportional to the muscle cross-sectional area, $N(t) = \kappa A_{CS}(t)$, and define the scalar incremental growth multiplier as:
$$G(t) = 1 + \frac{\Delta N}{N(t - \Delta t)}$$
They then substitute $G(t)$ directly into the 3D deformation tensor as the stretch along **both** directions perpendicular to the fiber unit vector $\mathbf{a}_0$:
$$\mathbf{G}(t) = G(t)\mathbf{I} + (1 - G(t))\mathbf{a}_0 \otimes \mathbf{a}_0$$
In an orthonormal basis $(\mathbf{a}_0, \mathbf{e}_1, \mathbf{e}_2)$, the matrix representation of $\mathbf{G}(t)$ is $\text{diag}(1, G(t), G(t))$. Therefore, the local volumetric determinant (and the cross-sectional area dilation ratio in the plane normal to $\mathbf{a}_0$) is:
$$\det(\mathbf{G}(t)) = \frac{A_{CS,\text{local}}(t)}{A_{CS,\text{local}}(t - \Delta t)} = [G(t)]^2 = \left(1 + \frac{\Delta N}{N}\right)^2 \approx 1 + 2\frac{\Delta N}{N}$$
Because the authors used $G(t)$ instead of $\sqrt{1 + \Delta N / N}$ as the linear stretch in each of the two transverse directions, **every incremental change in $N$ is doubled in cross-sectional area and volume ($[G(t)]^2$ instead of $G(t)$)!**
Furthermore, this kinematic error directly explains the "paradox" highlighted in Results lines 22–23 and Discussion line 48 (where volume increased by ~8–9% while midplane CSA increased by only ~4–6%):
- Because $N(t)$ increases by ~4.3% under the MWF protocol with Hill feedback, the local volume element grows by $\det(\mathbf{F}) = \det(\mathbf{G}) \approx (1.043)^2 \approx 1.088$ (+8.8% volume increase!).
- Meanwhile, at the midplane $\Gamma_{CS}$, the cross-sectional area is constrained radially by elastic compatibility with the Robin-sprung ends and sliced along the global PCA axis rather than normal to the curved Stokes-flow fibers. Thus, the 8–9% volume growth vs. 4–6% CSA growth is primarily driven by the $G^2$ area-stretch formulation rather than physiological remodeling.

### 2.2 Non-Commutativity and Frame-Invariance Flaw in the Cumulative Growth Update (Equation 12b)
In Equation (12b) (line 347), the authors write the recursive update for the total growth tensor as:
$$\mathbf{G}^{(k)} = \left(\mathbf{A}^{(k-1)}\right)^{-1} \cdot \mathbf{G}_k \cdot \mathbf{A}^{(k-1)} \cdot \mathbf{G}^{(k-1)}$$
where $\mathbf{G}_k = G_k \mathbf{I} + (1 - G_k)\mathbf{a}_0 \otimes \mathbf{a}_0$ is constructed using the **reference** fiber orientation $\mathbf{a}_0$ (from the undeformed mesh at $t=0$).
Look closely at the tensor mapping: $\mathbf{G}_k$ is pushed/pulled by $\mathbf{A}^{(k-1)}$, which maps between the intermediate stress-free configuration $\mathcal{B}_g^{(k-1)}$ and the current stressed configuration $\mathcal{B}_t^{(k-1)}$. If $\mathbf{G}_k$ is applied in the intermediate stress-free configuration (the green arrow in Figure 2E of Goriely & Ben Amar 2007), then the total growth tensor is simply the multiplicative composition $\mathbf{G}^{(k)} = \mathbf{G}_k \cdot \mathbf{G}^{(k-1)}$, where $\mathbf{G}_k$ uses the fiber direction in the intermediate configuration! Conversely, if $\mathbf{G}_k$ is defined in the current deformed configuration $\mathcal{B}_t^{(k-1)}$ and pulled back via $(\mathbf{A}^{(k-1)})^{-1}$, then $\mathbf{G}_k$ must be constructed using the **current deformed** unit fiber vector $\mathbf{a}^{(k-1)} = \mathbf{F}^{(k-1)}\mathbf{a}_0 / \|\mathbf{F}^{(k-1)}\mathbf{a}_0\|$, not the initial reference vector $\mathbf{a}_0$. Mixing the reference-configuration vector $\mathbf{a}_0$ inside $\mathbf{G}_k$ while sandwiching $\mathbf{G}_k$ between $(\mathbf{A}^{(k-1)})^{-1}$ and $\mathbf{A}^{(k-1)}$ mixes reference and current tangent spaces. Moreover, in the constitutive stress law (Equation 1, line 70), $\mathbf{S}_{\text{aniso}}$ and $\mathbf{S}_{\text{active}}$ are evaluated using $\mathbf{C}$ and $\mathbf{a}_0$ rather than the elastic right Cauchy-Green tensor $\mathbf{C}_e = \mathbf{A}^T \mathbf{A}$.

### 2.3 Disconnected Active Contraction Mechanics and Mesh-Dependent White-Noise Heterogeneity
1. **Unused Active Stress Formulation:** Equations (1)–(2) and Table 1 define an elaborate active and passive hyperelastic fiber stress model ($\sigma_{\text{active}}$, $f_{\text{active}}(\lambda)$, $\lambda_{\text{opt}} = 1.4$), and Figure 4B shows a single static contraction ($\alpha = 1$). However, during all 9-week growth simulations (Figures 4C–F, 6, 7), $\alpha$ is set to $0$ (passive unloading) and the active contraction mechanics play **zero role** in driving or spatially localizing hypertrophy! Because $G(t)$ is spatially uniform across the entire mesh (in Figures 4 and 6), the muscle grows identically in regions of zero mechanical stress and regions of peak tensile stress.
2. **Threshold Clipping Explains the "Drag" in Heterogeneous Simulations:** In Figure 7, perturbing ODE parameters by $\pm 1\%$ reduces mean volume and CSA growth compared to the uniform baseline. The authors attribute this to "mechanical drag" from elastic compatibility. In fact, because the ODE system sits on a razor-sharp threshold ($M^* = 0.469, F^* = 0.434$, Equation 5) where $f(F,M)$ is piecewise rectified at $0$ and clipped at $[N_{\min}, N_{\max}]$ (Equation 6), Jensen's inequality applied to a rectified nonlinear function of noisy parameters shifts the **expected value of the ODE output $E[N(t)]$ itself** downward before any 3D mechanics are even solved (as visible in the gray mean line lying below the blue dashed line in Figure 7A for $N(t)$!).

## 3. Minor / Technical Issues

1. **Unrealistic Stokes-Flow Fiber Architecture for Bipennate Muscles:**
   - As the authors acknowledge in the Discussion (line 50), solving a Stokes flow from origin to insertion produces longitudinal, fusiform-like streamlines even for the tibialis anterior (TA) and biceps femoris long head (BFLH), which in humans are strongly pennate/bipennate muscles where fibers insert into a central aponeurosis at 10–25° angles.
2. **Piecewise Nondifferentiability of $f_{\text{passive}}(\lambda)$ and $f_{\text{active}}(\lambda)$:**
   - In Equation (2b), at $\lambda = 0.6\lambda_{\text{opt}}$, the left limit is $9(0.2)^2 = 0.36$ and the right limit is $1 - 4(0.4)^2 = 0.36$, which is continuous. However, in Equation (5), $f(F,M)$ has negative signs on **all** branches in the typeset table (line 233: `− k_M (M − M*)` on lines 2 and 4), which is a typographical sign error in Equation (5) (since mTOR promotes synthesis, the $k_M(M - M^*)$ term must be positive!).

## 4. Constructive Remediation Plan

1. **Fix the Transverse Stretch Kinematics (Square-Root Formulation) and Sign Typo in Equation 5:**
   - Correct the typographical minus signs on the $+k_M(M - M^*)$ terms in Equation (5).
   - Correct the transverse stretch multiplier in Equation (9)/(10) to $G(t) = \sqrt{1 + \Delta N / N(t - \Delta t)}$ so that the cross-sectional area dilation $\det(\mathbf{G}) = [G(t)]^2 = 1 + \Delta N / N$ matches the 1:1 proportionality $N(t) = \kappa A_{CS}(t)$. Re-run the cohort simulations to check how much of the volume-vs-CSA discrepancy remains once $\det(\mathbf{G})$ is properly scaled.
2. **Clarify the Elastic Tensor Pull-Back and Stress Evaluation:**
   - Explicitly write out how $\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{A})$ and $\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{F}, \mathbf{G})$ are assembled in `FEniCSx` (confirming that elastic stress depends on $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{F}\mathbf{G}^{-1}$ rather than total $\mathbf{F}$, and that $\mathbf{G}^{(k)} = \mathbf{G}_k \mathbf{G}^{(k-1)}$ in the intermediate stress-free configuration).
3. **Separate Jensen's Inequality (ODE Rectification) from Mechanical Buffering in Figure 7:**
   - Compare the spatial mean of the uncoupled 0D ODE ensemble $\overline{N(t)}$ against the 3D mechanically coupled volume change $\overline{J(t)}$ in Figure 7B to quantify how much of the growth reduction is purely biochemical (due to threshold rectification in the 0D ODEs) versus mechanical compatibility (residual stress).
4. **Couple Local Mechanical Strain/Stress to Local Signaling Input $I_{\text{ex}}(\mathbf{X})$:**
   - Even as a proof-of-concept demonstration in one geometry, replacing the spatially uniform $I_{\text{ex}}$ in Equation (7) with the local active fiber stress or stretch computed from the $\alpha = 1$ contraction step (Figure 4B) would achieve true bidirectional mechanochemical coupling and elevate the physiological significance of the framework.
