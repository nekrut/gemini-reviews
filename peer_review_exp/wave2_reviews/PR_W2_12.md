## 1. Summary of Claims & Epistemic Architecture

This manuscript investigates the length distribution and non-adenosine ("mixed tailing") composition of mRNA and ncRNA poly(A) tails across seven herpesviruses (HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, HCMV, KSHV, EBV, and Herpesvirus Saimiri [HVS]) and three non-herpesviruses (SARS-CoV-2, Human Adenovirus 41 [hAdV41], and Mpox virus [MPXV]) using Oxford Nanopore direct RNA sequencing (DRS, SQK-RNA002 chemistry) and the `nanopolish` and `ninetails` packages. The authors report that uninfected human cell lines (A549, MeWo, NHDF) exhibit modal mRNA poly(A) tail lengths of 48–54 nt, whereas herpesviral protein-coding mRNAs across $\alpha$-, $\beta$-, and $\gamma$-herpesvirus subfamilies exhibit extended modal poly(A) tail lengths of 96–150 nt. By contrast, SARS-CoV-2 subgenomic mRNAs (~50 nt), hAdV41 mRNAs (54–73 nt), and MPXV mRNAs (68–85 nt) show shorter modal lengths. Using `ninetails`, the authors show that although non-adenosine incorporation increases during HCMV infection (from 6.5% to 13.3% on host mRNAs and 6.6% to 12.2% on viral mRNAs) and is enriched for G-incorporation on HCMV `RNA2.7` and `UL69`, overall mixed-tailing rates (<25% at maximum across herpesviruses, and 6.1–7.1% in KSHV and VZV) are too low to account for the population-wide extension of herpesviral poly(A) tails. All code and raw fast5 datasets are deposited in GitHub and ENA/SRA.

The empirical dataset is broad and technically informative, and the negative conclusion regarding mixed tailing as the primary driver of pan-herpesvirus poly(A) tail extension is convincing. However, the statistical analysis contains a direct contradiction in the EBV host-vs-virus comparison, treats single sequencing runs as fixed effects without biological replication in the primary GLMM, and relies on steady-state DRS measurements without nascent RNA labeling to dismiss transcript-age effects.

### Assessment of Specific Conclusions
* **Herpesvirus mRNAs generally exhibit longer steady-state poly(A) tail distributions than bulk host mRNAs: [Moderately Supported].**  
  True and substantial for HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, HCMV, KSHV, and HVS (modes 96–150 nt vs. 42–54 nt in host mRNAs during lytic infection), though the claim of uniformity across all seven viruses is contradicted by the authors' own effect size for EBV ($A = 0.46$).
* **EBV mRNAs have longer poly(A) tails than host mRNAs in the same population: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In Results (line 206), the authors claim "for all viruses we observed uniformly longer poly(A) tail length distributions... with differences between human and viral mRNAs in the same population ranging from negligible ($A = 0.46$, EBV) to large ($A = 0.18$, HVS)." In Vargha and Delaney's $A$ statistic, $A = 0.50$ indicates stochastic equality, $A < 0.50$ indicates Group 1 (human) has shorter tails than Group 2 (viral), and $A = 0.46$ is a negligible difference of only 4% from 0.50 (or in the opposite direction if coded identically to other comparisons). Furthermore, with tens of thousands of reads as the unit of analysis, a Wilcoxon test produces $p < 0.05$ for trivial noise.
* **Non-adenosine "mixed tailing" occurs on specific transcripts (e.g., HCMV `RNA2.7`, `UL69`) but is too infrequent (<7–23%) to explain the global upward shift of herpesvirus poly(A) tail distributions: [Well Supported].**  
  Because 77–94% of herpesviral reads lack any detectable non-A incorporation (with synthetic IVT control showing ~1% false positive rate) yet the entire unimodal distribution of viral poly(A) tails is shifted upward by 50–100 nt, mixed tailing mathematically cannot explain the bulk distribution shift.
* **Longer viral poly(A) tails are not explained by viral transcripts being newly synthesized ("younger") relative to the host mRNA pool: [Fragile].**  
  The authors argue against transcript age based on hAdV41 and a cited 10-hour median human mRNA half-life, but herpesviruses actively degrade pre-existing host mRNAs via viral endonucleases (HSV-1/2 vhs *UL41*, KSHV *SOX*, EBV *BGLF5*) and hyper-transcribe viral late genes; without metabolic labeling (e.g., 4sU) or transcription-arrest chases, steady-state poly(A) length remains confounded by the ratio of nascent-to-aged transcripts.

## 2. Major Methodological & Statistical Flaws

### 2.1. Pseudoreplication and Read-Level Wilcoxon Testing in Host vs. Viral Comparisons [Methodologically Flawed]
In the Methods (lines 181–183) and Results (lines 198, 206), two statistical issues undermine the hypothesis tests:
1. **Read-level Wilcoxon tests inflate sample size to $N \sim 10^4\text{--}10^5$ reads:** In line 206, the authors apply a two-sample Wilcoxon rank-sum test pooling all individual sequencing reads from human vs. viral mRNAs within a single sequencing run. Because individual reads are not independent biological replicates (and a handful of hyper-abundant viral transcripts can dominate the read pool), $p < 0.05$ is guaranteed even when the effect size is negligible—as seen in EBV where Vargha and Delaney's $A = 0.46$ (within 0.04 of parity, $0.50$) is still declared a "uniformly longer" distribution.
2. **GLMM specification (`PolyA_Length ~ Run + (1 | Transcript)`) confounds biological condition with technical sequencing run:** For the uninfected cell line comparisons (line 183, 198) and time-course comparisons, each cell line or time point appears to be represented by a single sequencing run (`Run` as fixed effect), with no random effect for biological replicate (`(1 | Biological_Replicate)`). Consequently, run-to-run flowcell/pore variance is statistically inseparable from biological differences when claiming that MeWo tails are "~4% longer than A549 ($p < 0.0001$)."

### 2.2. Uncontrolled Host Shutoff and Transcript Age Confounding Across Virus Families [Fragile]
Alpha- and gammaherpesviruses (HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV, KSHV, EBV, HVS) encode potent host-shutoff endonucleases (vhs/*UL41*, *SOX*, *BGLF5*) and/or aggressively hijack host RNA Pol II, whereas HCMV and SARS-CoV-2 remodel host mRNA decay differently (and notably, host mRNA modal poly(A) tail length jumps to 80 nt in VZV, 94 nt in HCMV, and 170 nt in SARS-CoV-2; lines 208, 221). Notice that in VZV (host mode 80 nt vs. viral mode 150 nt), HCMV (host mode 94 nt vs. viral mode 122 nt), and SARS-CoV-2 (host mode 170 nt vs. viral mode ~50 nt), the host mRNA poly(A) distribution itself shifts dramatically during infection! Comparing viral poly(A) tails to uninfected host tails without accounting for (i) selective degradation of short-tailed vs. long-tailed host transcripts and (ii) the burst of nascent viral transcription at late high-MOI timepoints leaves the mechanistic interpretation ambiguous.

### 2.3. Reference Genome Mismatch for Mpox Virus Alignment [Moderately Supported]
In the Methods (line 121) and Results (line 221), the authors state they infected NHDFs with Mpox virus "strain NL001-2022" (which is further misdescribed on line 221 as "Clade 1b, strain NL1", whereas NL001-2022 isolated at Erasmus MC in 2022 is Clade IIb), and then aligned the reads against "Mpox UK1 (`MT903343.1`)" (line 166). While orthopoxvirus coding sequences are conserved, the strain clade designation in the text should be corrected and verified against the reference genome used.

## 4. Constructive Remediation Plan

### 1. Perform Transcript-Level (Rather Than Read-Level) Comparisons and Qualify the EBV Result
* **WHY:** Pooling tens of thousands of reads in a Wilcoxon test treats each read as an independent observation, allowing highly expressed viral genes to dominate the distribution and yielding statistically significant $p$-values for negligible effects ($A = 0.46$ in EBV).
* **WHAT:** Recompute host vs. viral poly(A) tail length comparisons using per-transcript median poly(A) tail lengths (for transcripts with $\ge 30$ reads) in addition to read-level distributions, and revise the claim that EBV exhibits an extended poly(A) tail distribution.
* **HOW:** Using the existing processed tables in the GitHub repository (`DepledgeLab/polyAtails`), calculate the median poly(A) tail length for each individual host and viral protein-coding transcript, run the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Vargha-Delaney $A$ across transcript medians, and explicitly note in the Results (line 206) that EBV ($A = 0.46$) shows an overlapping overall distribution with host mRNAs rather than a large upward shift.

### 2. Incorporate Biological Replicate Structure into the GLMM
* **WHY:** Modeling `PolyA_Length ~ Run + (1 | Transcript)` on unreplicated flowcell runs conflates technical nanopore run-to-run variability with biological differences between cell lines or timepoints.
* **WHAT:** Where biological replicates exist (e.g., 72 hpi HCMV and siCNOT1 replicates shown in S3/S4 Figs), estimate the between-replicate variance component; where $n = 1$ flowcell per condition was run, report the results as descriptive distributions with effect sizes ($A$) rather than claiming $p < 0.0001$ biological differences for a 4% (2 nt) shift between MeWo and A549.
* **HOW:** Fit `PolyA_Length ~ Condition + (1 | Biological_Replicate) + (1 | Transcript)` on the replicated HCMV datasets to quantify baseline flowcell-to-flowcell variance (in nt), and use this technical variance bound as a minimum threshold for biological significance across the unreplicated comparisons.

### 3. Correct Strain Metadata, Figure Cross-References, and Concentration Typos
* **WHY:** Factual errors in viral clade classification, figure references, and antibiotic concentrations detract from technical precision.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  1. Line 221: Correct "Mpox virus (MPXV; Clade 1b, strain NL1)" to match the Methods (line 121: strain NL001-2022, Clade IIb).
  2. Line 229: Change "(Fig 2c and S1 Table)" to "(Fig 3c and S1 Table)".
  3. Line 240 (Fig 4 caption): Change "RNA2.9 (cytoplasmic)" to "RNA2.7 (cytoplasmic)".
  4. Lines 136 and 141: Change "250 mg/mL G418" to "250 µg/mL G418" and "100 mg/mL hygromycin" to "100 µg/mL hygromycin".
  5. Line 166 / Fig 1h: Specify how OMK (*Aotus*) host mRNAs were aligned and annotated in the HVS experiment.
