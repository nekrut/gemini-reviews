## 1. Summary of Claims & Epistemic Architecture

This manuscript presents a retrospective computational analysis of 596 Rift Valley fever virus (RVFV) complete segment sequences ($L = 173, M = 196, S = 227$) across 13 African countries (1944–2022) retrieved from NCBI Virus, aiming to characterize spatiotemporal amino acid mutation burdens, cross-species transmission dynamics, and codon-level selection pressures.

* **Claim 1:** RVFV "mutation burden" varies significantly across countries and outbreaks (e.g., lowest in 1970s Egypt with mean $2.4$ mutations per isolate; highest in 1987 Mauritania with $18.1$ and 2008–2011 South Africa with $13.4$), with dynamic year-to-year increases and decreases reflecting episodic viral diversification. `[Methodologically Flawed]`
* **Claim 2:** Non-human isolates carry significantly higher protein-coding mutation counts than human isolates in the L ($15$ vs. $13, p < 0.001$) and M ($11$ vs. $9, p < 0.001$) segments, whereas human isolates carry markedly higher mutation counts in the S segment ($18$ vs. $8, p < 0.001$). `[Methodologically Flawed]`
* **Claim 3:** Ancestral host-state reconstruction on the M segment phylogeny demonstrates that livestock lineages are the primary source of human spillovers ($n = 16$ transitions), alongside $n = 178$ within-human evolutionary transitions. `[Methodologically Flawed]`
* **Claim 4:** Seven recurrent nonsynonymous substitutions across five codon sites (L: N277S, N277D, S278N; M: I442S, I442V, V659A; S: N133S) are under host-specific diversifying selection (supported by FUBAR posterior probability $\ge 0.90$), driving polymerase adaptation and immune escape. `[Overstated]`

## 2. Major Methodological & Statistical Flaws

### 2.1 Severe Reference-Bias Artifact in "Mutation Burden" Quantification
The entire spatiotemporal and host-level "mutation burden" analysis (Figs. 1, 2, and 3; lines 88–92 and 129–164) is built on a fundamental evolutionary bioinformatics fallacy: counting amino acid differences per isolate relative to a **single fixed reference genome** (`NC_014395.1`, `NC_014396.1`, `NC_014397.1`).
1. **Identity of the RefSeq genome:** The NCBI RefSeq standard for RVFV (`NC_014395`–`NC_014397`) is the **ZH-501 strain**, isolated in **Egypt in 1977** (Lineage A).
2. **Trivial explanation of the "Egyptian conservation" finding:** Because every isolate in the dataset was compared against the 1977 Egyptian Lineage A reference strain, Egyptian isolates from the 1970s (which belong to Lineage A and are nearly identical to ZH-501) trivially exhibit only $2.4$ amino acid differences! Conversely, isolates from Mauritania (1987), Kenya (2006–2007), or South Africa (2008–2011) belong to different clades (Lineages C, H, I, K, L) that diverged decades earlier from Lineage A and therefore differ by 12–18 amino acids from the Egyptian reference.
3. **Misinterpretation of lineage replacement as "stepwise mutation accumulation and decline":** In Fig. 1b (lines 139–141), the authors compute year-to-year changes in mean distance from the reference genome (e.g., reporting a $+11.3$ increase followed by a $-14.3$ decrease in South Africa between 2008 and 2011) and interpret this as fluctuating "mutation burden." In reality, sampling a clade distant from Lineage A in one year and a clade closer to Lineage A in the next year shifts the Hamming distance to the reference without any change in evolutionary rate or within-outbreak diversification.

### 2.2 Phylogenetic Pseudoreplication and Pipeline Confounding in Segment/Host Comparisons
In Fig. 3 (lines 155–164), the authors apply Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and Kruskal-Wallis tests directly to per-isolate reference-distance counts, claiming that human isolates have higher mutation counts in the S segment (median $18$, IQR $18\text{--}19$) than non-human isolates (median $8$, IQR $7\text{--}9, p < 0.001$).
* **Violation of statistical independence:** Viral sequences sampled during an outbreak share a common phylogenetic history and cannot be treated as independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations in a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. South Africa (2008–2011) and Kenya (2006–2007) account for $76.7\%$ of the dataset; a single densely sampled human outbreak in a lineage whose S segment happens to differ by 18 residues from ZH-501 creates a tight spike at 18 mutations (as seen in the zero-width IQR of $18.0\text{--}19.0$) that drives $p < 0.001$ purely through clonal pseudoreplication.
* **Cross-segment pipeline inconsistency:** Protein-coding mutations in the M segment were called using Genome Detective (DIAMOND + Advanced Genome Aligner), whereas L and S segment mutations were called using a separate Nextflow/MAFFT/Aliview manual-trimming/Biopython pipeline (lines 88–92). Comparing total mutation counts across segments when different aligners, trimming rules, and variant callers were used introduces severe technical confounding.

### 2.3 Biologically Invalid Ancestral Host-State Reconstruction (`ace` with Equal Rates and Midpoint Rooting)
The host-state reconstruction in Fig. 5 (lines 105–111 and 177–185) suffers from three compounding methodological errors:
1. **Midpoint rooting of a time-scaled tree:** In line 107, the authors state that the time-scaled M segment tree from TreeTime *"was rooted at the midpoint"* prior to ancestral state reconstruction. Midpoint rooting overwrites the temporal root inferred by root-to-tip regression in TreeTime, distorting the chronological direction of ancestor-descendant branches near the base of the tree.
2. **Equal Rates (`ER`) continuous-time Markov model:** Using `ape::ace(..., model = "ER")` forces the transition rate from Human $\to$ Livestock and Human $\to$ Vector to be identical to the rate from Livestock $\to$ Human and Vector $\to$ Human. Epidemiologically, humans are primarily dead-end spillover hosts for RVFV (or play a negligible role in infecting livestock), and mosquitoes do not evolve over decades as an independent reservoir lineage without vertebrate amplification.
3. **Unaccounted sampling bias and spurious "human-to-human" transmission:** Because human clinical cases are heavily oversampled during epidemics relative to mosquitoes or wildlife, internal nodes within an outbreak clade are reconstructed as "Human," leading the authors to report **$n = 178$ human-to-human transitions** (line 179; compared to only 16 livestock-to-human and 16 vector-to-vector transitions). RVFV is an arbovirus transmitted by mosquitoes and contact with infected animal tissues; sustained human-to-human transmission does not occur in nature. Reporting 178 "human-to-human" evolutionary transitions without correcting for host sampling bias or vector-borne transmission biology invalidates the transition network.

### 2.4 Flawed Inference of "Host-Specific Diversifying Selection" and Internal Contradictions
The selection pressure analysis (lines 114–119, 188–251, Table 2) contains major statistical and logical flaws:
1. **Internal contradiction on S segment codon 133 (N133S):** In line 214, the text states: *"For the S segment, the mutation N133S at codon 133 was detected in livestock and vector isolates sampled from Kenya, South Africa and Zimbabwe between the 1960s and 2020s. Evidence consistent with diversifying selection at this codon was detected only in human-derived sequences, as supported by FUBAR analysis."* If N133S occurred in livestock and vector isolates, how did it drive a FUBAR diversifying selection signal *only* in the human-derived alignment? Either the host metadata stratification is mismatched or a different substitution/polymorphism occurred at site 133.
2. **Misreporting of FUBAR $\beta$ and $\alpha$ as a ratio:** In Table 2 (line 239) and lines 248–250, the authors report `dN/dS(β/α)` as `14.657/0.793`, `9.771/0.828`, etc. In FUBAR, $\beta$ is the posterior mean nonsynonymous substitution rate and $\alpha$ is the posterior mean synonymous substitution rate; formatting them with a slash without computing the ratio or reporting the credible interval is non-standard, and none of the 5 sites were supported by FEL or SLAC ($p > 0.1$).
3. **Absence of formal differential selection testing:** Running FUBAR separately on human and non-human sequence subsets does not test whether selection pressure differs significantly between hosts. Differences in sample size and tree length between the human and non-human subsets directly alter FUBAR posterior probabilities. Testing for host-specific differential selection requires a joint phylogeny using `Contrast-FEL` (to test $\beta_{\text{human}} \neq \beta_{\text{non-human}}$) or `MEME` (to test episodic selection on spillover branches).
4. **Miscounting of segments vs. protein-coding genes:** In the S segment, RVFV uses an ambisense coding strategy encoding both the nucleoprotein (N) on the genomic strand and the non-structural protein (NSs) on the antigenomic strand (line 69). The Methods (lines 90–118) refer to uploading a single codon-aligned FASTA file for the "S genome segment" to MAFFT, GARD, and Datamonkey, without explaining how the two opposite-sense open reading frames (N and NSs) were separated prior to codon alignment and selection analysis.

## 3. Minor / Technical Issues

1. **Inconsistent TreeTime versions:** Line 102 states that root-to-tip regression was performed using *"TreeTime (version 0.11.4)"* and in the very next sentence states that time-scaled trees were reconstructed using *"TreeTime (version 0.9.4)."*
2. **Segment reassortment ignored:** RVFV is a tripartite bunyavirus known to undergo segment reassortment during co-infections in vectors and livestock. The manuscript analyzes $L=173, M=196, S=227$ sequences independently without linking isolates that share all three segments or testing for inter-segment reassortment.
3. **Truncated amino acid mutation range in Abstract:** The Abstract (line 59) states *"ranging from 2—20 per isolate"*, whereas the Results (line 131) states *"ranging from 0 to > 20 mutations per isolate (range = 8–31 in South Africa; range = 1–5 in Egypt)."*

## 4. Constructive Remediation Plan

1. **Replace reference-based Hamming counts with patristic phylogenetic distances:** Abandon counting amino acid differences relative to the 1977 Egyptian ZH-501 reference genome. Instead, quantify within-outbreak diversity using nucleotide/amino acid pairwise diversity ($\pi$) within each country-year outbreak clade, and measure evolutionary divergence using root-to-tip patristic distances on the time-calibrated maximum-likelihood phylogeny.
2. **Account for phylogenetic non-independence in host and segment comparisons:** Use phylogenetic generalized linear mixed models (`PGLMM` / `MCMCglmm`) or clade-level paired comparisons (comparing human vs. livestock sequences *within the same outbreak clade*) rather than naive Wilcoxon rank-sum tests across pooled isolates. Use an identical alignment and variant-calling pipeline across all three segments (separating the S segment into N and NSs coding sequences, and the M segment into NSm, Gn, and Gc).
3. **Re-run phylodynamic host-transition analysis with asymmetric rates and subsampling:**
   * Do not midpoint-root the time-scaled tree; preserve the temporal root from TreeTime / BEAST.
   * Replace the symmetric Equal Rates (`ER`) model in `ape::ace` with an asymmetric discrete-trait phylodynamic model (e.g., `ARD` in `ape`/`corHMM` or Bayesian structured coalescent / MASCOT in BEAST2) that constrains or accounts for vector-borne transmission biology, and perform tip-date/host subsampling sensitivity analyses to eliminate the artifactual 178 "human-to-human" transitions.
4. **Perform formal branch-site and contrastive selection analyses:** Combine human and non-human sequences into a single per-protein codon alignment (`L`, `Gn`, `Gc`, `N`, `NSs`), label branches by host category, and run `Contrast-FEL` and `MEME` in HyPhy to formally test whether any codon experiences statistically significant differential selection between human and non-human branches.
