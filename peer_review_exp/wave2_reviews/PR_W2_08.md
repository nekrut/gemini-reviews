## 1. Summary of Claims & Epistemic Architecture

This manuscript presents a multiscale computational model of exercise-induced skeletal muscle hypertrophy, coupling a Lotka–Volterra ordinary differential equation (ODE) system for the IGF1–AKT–mTOR–FOXO signaling pathway with a 3D transversely isotropic hyperelastic finite-element model via the Rodríguez–Goriely volumetric growth decomposition ($\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{G}$), applied to idealized geometries and 12 muscle meshes from the Visible Human dataset.

* **Claim 1:** The ODE signaling model acts as an incoherent feedforward loop where exercise bouts trigger fast IGF1/AKT spikes and cumulative mTOR/FOXO wave summation, with Sobol sensitivity analysis identifying FOXO kinetic rates ($a_F, b_F$) as the primary threshold regulators of net myofibril accretion. `[Well Supported]`
* **Claim 2:** Bidirectional mechanochemical coupling between 3D tissue mechanics and biochemical signaling predicts protocol-dependent macroscopic hypertrophy while homeostatically preventing unbounded growth via cross-sectional area (CSA) feedback on the protein synthesis rate $k_M$. `[Overstated]`
* **Claim 3:** In anatomically realistic muscle geometries (Visible Human BFLH, ST, TA), complex 3D fiber architectures drive spatially non-uniform hypertrophy in which total volumetric growth ($\sim 8\text{--}9\%$) consistently exceeds mid-belly CSA growth ($\sim 3\text{--}6\%$). `[Moderately Supported]`
* **Claim 4:** When biochemical signaling rates are perturbed locally across mesh elements, 3D hyperelastic continuum mechanics acts as a "mechanical buffer" that smooths jagged local growth drives into coordinated macroscopic deformation while reducing net mean hypertrophy. `[Fragile]`

## 2. Major Methodological & Statistical Flaws

### 2.1 Absence of Local Mechanotransduction: The Signaling Model Is Driven by a Global Scalar Clock, Not 3D Tissue Mechanics
The central premise of the manuscript (Abstract, line 7; Introduction, lines 18–24) is that scalar compartmental signaling models fail because they *"neglect the complex spatial distribution of stress and strain within the 3D tissue"*, and that the authors' framework *"mechanistically integrates these mechanical and biochemical stimuli."* However, inspecting the mathematical governing equations (Equations 4–10, lines 52–101) reveals that **local 3D mechanical stress, strain, and fiber stretch never enter the biochemical signaling equations at all**:
1. **Forward stimulus (Eqs. 6–7):** During an exercise bout, the IGF1 stimulus $a_I(t) = \eta I_{\text{ex}} F_{\text{max}}$ and the AKT stimulus $a_A(t)$ are purely prescribed **global scalar functions of time $t$** and fixed scalar constants ($I_{\text{ex}} \in \{0.2, 0.8\}$, $F_{\text{max}}$). Neither the local second Piola–Kirchhoff stress $\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{X})$, the von Mises stress, nor the local fiber stretch $\lambda(\mathbf{X}) = \sqrt{I_4}$ computed in Fig. 4B is ever passed into the ODE system!
2. **Feedback loop (Eq. 10):** The "mechanical feedback" $k_M(t) = 2k_{M0}\left(1 - \frac{(A_{\text{CS}}^*)^n}{K_A^n + (A_{\text{CS}}^*)^n}\right)$ depends solely on $A_{\text{CS}}^*(t) = A_{\text{CS}}(t)/A_{\text{CS}}(0)$, which is a **single global scalar** (the integrated area of a single midplane slice $\Gamma_{\text{CS}}$, Eq. on line 126).
3. **Consequence for Results Sections 1–3 (Figs. 3–6):** Because $a_I(t)$, $a_A(t)$, and $A_{\text{CS}}^*(t)$ are spatially uniform scalars, in every single simulation in Figs. 3, 4, 5, and 6, the ODE variables $(I, A, F, M, N)$ and the incremental growth multiplier $\mathcal{G}(t)$ (Eq. 8) are **100% identical at every point in the 3D mesh**! The active contraction simulation shown in Fig. 4B ($\alpha$ from $0$ to $1$) is completely decoupled from the 9-week growth simulations (where $\alpha = 0$ and a spatially uniform transverse expansion $\mathcal{G}(t)$ is simply projected onto the static reference fiber vector field $\mathbf{a}_0(\mathbf{X})$). Claiming that this model mechanistically couples local 3D tissue mechanics to intracellular signaling is a substantial overstatement of the mathematical formulation.

### 2.2 Mathematical Inconsistency in the Cumulative Growth Update and Fiber Orientation Kinematics
In Equations (11a–c) (line 106), the authors write the recursive cumulative growth update of Goriely and Ben Amar as:
$$\mathbf{F}^{(k)} = \mathbf{A}^{(k)}\mathbf{G}^{(k)}, \quad \mathbf{G}^{(k)} = (\mathbf{A}^{(k-1)})^{-1} \cdot \mathbf{G}_k \cdot \mathbf{A}^{(k-1)} \cdot \mathbf{G}^{(k-1)}, \quad \mathbf{A}^{(k)} = \mathbf{A}_k \cdot \mathbf{A}^{(k-1)}$$
where $\mathbf{G}_k = \mathcal{G}_k \mathbf{I} + (1 - \mathcal{G}_k)\mathbf{a}_0 \otimes \mathbf{a}_0$ (Eq. 9).
1. **Reference-frame mismatch of the fiber vector $\mathbf{a}_0$:** By substituting $\mathbf{G}^{(k)}$ and $\mathbf{A}^{(k)}$, the total deformation gradient is $\mathbf{F}^{(k)} = \mathbf{A}_k \cdot \mathbf{G}_k \cdot \mathbf{F}^{(k-1)}$. In continuum mechanics, if $\mathbf{G}_k$ operates on the current deformed configuration $\mathbf{F}^{(k-1)}\Omega_0$, the local fiber direction in that configuration is $\mathbf{a}_{k-1} = \frac{\mathbf{F}^{(k-1)}\mathbf{a}_0}{\|\mathbf{F}^{(k-1)}\mathbf{a}_0\|}$, **not** the initial reference vector $\mathbf{a}_0$! Conversely, if $\mathbf{G}_k$ is defined in the intermediate stress-free virtual configuration (where fibers remain aligned with $\mathbf{a}_0$ under purely transverse growth), then the multiplicative decomposition is $\mathbf{F}^{(k)} = \mathbf{A}^{(k)} \mathbf{G}_k \mathbf{G}^{(k-1)}$ (so $\mathbf{G}^{(k)} = \mathbf{G}_k \mathbf{G}^{(k-1)}$), which does not conjugate $\mathbf{G}_k$ by $\mathbf{A}^{(k-1)}$. Conjugating $\mathbf{G}_k(\mathbf{a}_0)$ by $\mathbf{A}^{(k-1)}$ mixes the material reference frame with the spatial frame whenever $\mathbf{A}^{(k-1)}$ contains shear or rotation (as occurs in the curved Visible Human meshes).
2. **Inconsistency in Eq. (8) vs. $N(t) = \kappa A_{\text{CS}}(t)$:** In line 92, the authors assume $N(t) = \kappa A_{\text{CS}}(t)$ and define the scalar growth multiplier as $\mathcal{G}(t) = 1 + \frac{\Delta N}{N(t - \Delta t)}$. However, for transverse growth with $\mathbf{G} = \mathcal{G}\mathbf{I} + (1 - \mathcal{G})\mathbf{a}_0 \otimes \mathbf{a}_0$, the local cross-sectional area element perpendicular to $\mathbf{a}_0$ scales as $\det(\mathbf{G}) = \mathcal{G}^2$, not $\mathcal{G}$! Replacing $\sqrt{1 + \Delta N / N}$ with $1 + \Delta N / N$ doubles the first-order volumetric growth rate ($J_G = \det(\mathbf{G}) = \mathcal{G}^2 \approx 1 + 2\frac{\Delta N}{N}$) relative to the myofibril increase $\Delta N / N$.

### 2.3 Dimensional/Scaling Errors in the Active–Passive Force–Length Equations (Eqs. 2a–b)
In line 38, the manuscript states:
> *"The force-length relationships, $f_{\text{passive}}$ and $f_{\text{active}}$, depend on the fiber stretch $\lambda$ and follow a standard normalized model 43 where the maximal active force is generated at an optimal fiber length, $\lambda_{\text{ofl}} = 1.4$ (Figure 2A)."*

Inspecting Equations (2a) and (2b) reveals a mathematical contradiction:
* In Eq. (2b), on the main active branch $0.6\lambda_{\text{ofl}} < \lambda \le 1.4\lambda_{\text{ofl}}$, $f_{\text{active}}(\lambda) = 1 - 4(1 - \lambda/\lambda_{\text{ofl}})^2$. The peak of this parabola ($f_{\text{active}} = 1$) occurs at $\lambda / \lambda_{\text{ofl}} = 1$, i.e., $\lambda = \lambda_{\text{ofl}}$, and active force drops to zero at $\lambda = 0.4\lambda_{\text{ofl}}$ and $\lambda = 1.6\lambda_{\text{ofl}}$.
* In Blemker et al. (ref. 43) and Röhrle et al. (ref. 18), $\lambda_{\text{ofl}}$ is the optimal fiber stretch (typically $\lambda_{\text{ofl}} = 1.0$ or $1.2\text{--}1.4$), and the piecewise bounds for the normalized stretch $\tilde{\lambda} = \lambda / \lambda_{\text{ofl}}$ are $0.4 \le \tilde{\lambda} \le 1.6$, while the linear passive branch begins at $\lambda = 1.4$ (or $\tilde{\lambda} = 1.4$). By setting $\lambda_{\text{ofl}} = 1.4$ *and* multiplying $\lambda_{\text{ofl}}$ by $1.4$ and $1.6$ in the inequality bounds of Eqs. (2a–b), the upper limit of active force generation becomes $\lambda = 1.6 \times 1.4 = 2.24$ ($124\%$ engineering strain!) and the linear passive transition occurs at $\lambda = 1.4 \times 1.4 = 1.96$.

### 2.4 Mesh-Dependent White-Noise Perturbation in the Spatial Heterogeneity Analysis
In Section 2.6 (line 75) and Section 3.4 (line 174, Fig. 7), spatial heterogeneity in signaling is modeled by drawing $\delta(\mathbf{x}) \sim \mathcal{U}[-\Delta, \Delta]$ **independently for each tetrahedral mesh cell**.
* Sampling independent, discontinuous uniform random numbers per finite-element tetrahedron is not a well-posed continuum random field: its spatial correlation length equals the local mesh element size $h$. As the mesh is refined ($h \to 0$), independent element-wise noise averages out by homogenizing over adjacent elements, making the macroscopic damping and residual stress predictions in Fig. 7 an artifact of the arbitrary `fTetWild` discretization rather than a biological length scale (such as myofiber length or motor unit territory).
* Furthermore, the drop in mean hypertrophy under $\pm 1\%$ parameter noise (Fig. 7B) is a direct mathematical consequence of **Jensen's inequality** applied to the rectified threshold piecewise function $f(F, M)$ in Eq. (5) (where cells pushed into the $F \ge F^*$ degradation regime lose myofibrils while cells with $F < F^*$ are clipped at 0), rather than a continuum mechanical effect.

## 3. Minor / Technical Issues

1. **Discrepancy between total volume increase and mid-belly CSA increase (Fig. 6C–D):** Because $\det(\mathbf{G}) = \mathcal{G}^2$ is spatially uniform across the entire muscle in Fig. 6, and the hyperelastic material is nearly incompressible ($\det(\mathbf{A}) = 1$, so $\det(\mathbf{F}) = \det(\mathbf{G}) = \mathcal{G}^2$ everywhere!), the total muscle volume increase $\Delta V / V_0 = \mathcal{G}^2 - 1$ is strictly fixed by the ODE output and is **identical** across the cylinder, the fusiform muscle, and all 12 Visible Human muscles (except for tiny secondary changes from $A_{\text{CS}}^*(t)$ feedback on $k_M$)! The reason mid-belly CSA increased by only $3\text{--}6\%$ while volume increased by $8\text{--}9\%$ in the Visible Human meshes is that the muscles **elongated longitudinally** along their curved/pennate fiber tracks because the traction-free lateral boundaries offered less resistance than radial expansion, or because the PCA midplane slice $\Gamma_{\text{CS}}$ cuts obliquely across curved Stokes-flow fibers ($\mathbf{a}_0 \nparallel \mathbf{N}_{\text{PCA}}$). This kinematic explanation should be explicitly quantified by reporting muscle length change $\Delta L / L_0$ alongside $\Delta V / V_0$ and $\Delta A_{\text{CS}} / A_{\text{CS},0}$.
2. **Inconsistent perturbation amplitude between Methods and Results:** Line 75 (Methods) states `e.g. Δ = 0.1 for ±10% variation`, whereas line 174 (Results) states `perturbed by ±1% using a uniform random distribution`.

## 4. Constructive Remediation Plan

1. **Clarify or implement genuine local mechanotransduction coupling:**
   * Explicitly state in the Abstract, Introduction, and Methods that in the current formulation, exercise stimulation ($a_I, a_A$) and homeostatic feedback ($k_M(A_{\text{CS}}^*)$) are global muscle-level scalars rather than functions of local 3D stress/strain tensors.
   * Better yet, demonstrate true local mechanochemical coupling by replacing the global constant $I_{\text{ex}} F_{\text{max}}$ in Eq. (6) with the local active fiber stress or strain energy density computed from an active contraction solve ($\alpha = I_{\text{ex}}$), and replacing global $A_{\text{CS}}^*$ in Eq. (10) with the local area growth invariant $J_G = \det(\mathbf{G}(\mathbf{X}))$.
2. **Fix the quadratic area scaling in Eq. (8) and the cumulative growth frame in Eq. (11b):**
   * Because transverse area scales as $\mathcal{G}^2$, set $\mathcal{G}(t) = \sqrt{1 + \frac{\Delta N}{N(t - \Delta t)}}$ so that $\det(\mathbf{G}) = 1 + \frac{\Delta N}{N}$ matches the proportional relation $N(t) = \kappa A_{\text{CS}}(t)$.
   * Clarify the reference frame of $\mathbf{a}_0$ in Eq. (11b) and verify whether $\mathbf{G}^{(k)} = \mathbf{G}_k \mathbf{G}^{(k-1)}$ (in the intermediate stress-free configuration) yields identical or cleaner kinematics.
3. **Correct the stretch bounds in Eqs. (2a–b) and report longitudinal elongation:** Verify the value of $\lambda_{\text{ofl}}$ ($1.0$ vs. $1.4$) in Eqs. (2a–b), and report the longitudinal length change ($\Delta L / L_0$) of the 12 Visible Human meshes to explain why $\Delta V / V_0 > \Delta A_{\text{CS}} / A_{\text{CS},0}$ under incompressible $\det(\mathbf{A}) = 1$ kinematics.
4. **Replace element-wise white noise with a spatially correlated random field:** Generate $\delta(\mathbf{x})$ using a Gaussian random field with a defined physical correlation length $\ell_c$ (e.g., $\ell_c = 5\text{ mm}$) so that the heterogeneity analysis in Fig. 7 is mesh-independent.
