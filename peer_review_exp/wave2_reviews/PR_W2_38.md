## 2. Executive Summary & Editorial Verdict

### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript describes `amr.watch` (`https://amr.watch`), an automated, continuously updated web platform built on the `Pathogenwatch` infrastructure to track genotypic variants and antimicrobial resistance (AMR) mechanisms across 15 WHO priority bacterial pathogens from public whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data. Polling the European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) every four hours for paired-end Illumina WGS runs ($\ge 20\times$ coverage) with decodable collection year ($\ge 2010$) and country, the pipeline assembles reads with `SPAdes v3.15.3`, verifies species/serovars (`Speciator v4.0.0`, `SISTR v1.1.1`), assigns lineage nomenclature (7-gene MLST, `GPSC` for *S. pneumoniae*, `LIN` clonal groups for *K. pneumoniae*, and `GenoTyphi` for *S. Typhi*), and calls curated AMR determinants using `AMRFinderPlus`. Evaluating the database as of 31 March 2025, the authors analyze **620,700 passing genomes** while transparently documenting the exclusion of **605,154 genomes** lacking geotemporal metadata and **12,348 genomes** with discordant species labels. They quantify severe global disparities (89.6% of genomes originate from high-income countries—43.8% USA and 22.0% UK—versus 0.9% from low-income countries, with 89 countries contributing zero genomes) and ascertainment biases (e.g., 52.4% of *S. Typhi* genomes sequenced in the non-endemic UK; 60.5% carbapenemase carriage among sequenced *K. pneumoniae* vs. 28.7% estimated clinical prevalence), while demonstrating how the platform captures regional dominance of high-risk clones (ST11 vs. ST258/ST147) and carbapenemase classes ($\text{bla}_{\text{KPC-2}}$, $\text{bla}_{\text{NDM-1}}$, $\text{bla}_{\text{NDM-5}}$, $\text{bla}_{\text{OXA-48}}$). All processed metadata and accession lists are downloadable as CSVs via the web application.

This is a well-engineered, highly useful public health bioinformatics resource distinguished by its unusually candid and quantitative assessment of sampling, travel, and resistance-enrichment biases in public INSDC repositories. The primary technical limitations are: (1) reliance on an outdated `AMRFinderPlus` database version (`2021-12-21.1`, over three years old at the March 2025 analysis cutoff) and the 2017 rather than 2024 WHO priority pathogen list; (2) strict exclusion of Oxford Nanopore and PacBio long-read sequencing datasets, which disproportionately excludes recent LMIC and plasmid-focused AMR surveillance; (3) absence of `BioProject` / study-level clustering indicators or binomial confidence intervals when plotting annual country proportions; and (4) minor typographical errors in species and drug names (`"S. flexnen"`, `"cefedericol"`, `"E. coll"`).

---

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Automated ingestion, assembly, species verification, and AMR/lineage typing of 620,700 public bacterial genomes in `amr.watch`: [Well Supported].**  
  The pipeline applies community-standard tools (`SPAdes`, `Speciator`, `SISTR`, `PubMLST`, `GPSC`, `LIN`, `GenoTyphi`, `AMRFinderPlus`) with explicit species-specific contiguity and contamination filters (Supplementary Table 1), successfully catching 12,348 misannotated ENA entries and resolving the *E. coli* / *Shigella* taxonomic boundary.
* **Public INSDC bacterial WGS repositories exhibit severe geographic skew (89.6% high-income vs. 0.9% low-income countries), metadata incompleteness (605,154 excluded genomes), travel ascertainment (*S. Typhi*), and AMR sequencing bias: [Well Supported].**  
  The authors' audit of 1.24 million ENA entries (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 3) provides a rigorous, quantitative benchmark of global genomic surveillance gaps and resistance pre-selection bias.
* **Geotemporal visualizations in `amr.watch` accurately recover known regional high-risk clones (e.g., *K. pneumoniae* ST11 in China vs. ST258/ST147 in the USA) and shifting carbapenemase epidemiology ($\text{bla}_{\text{NDM-5}}$ expansion): [Moderately Supported].**  
  The macro-epidemiological trends align well with published multicenter surveillance studies (Figs. 4–5). However, because genomes within a country-year bin are aggregated across all ENA `BioProject` submissions without study-level deduplication or uncertainty intervals, single-center outbreak investigations can transiently skew annual proportions in countries with low baseline sequencing volume, and the `2021-12-21.1` `AMRFinderPlus` database misses AMR alleles described after late 2021.

## 3. Forensic Technical Audit & Constructive Remediation Plan

### Major Methodological & Statistical Flaws

#### 3.1. Outdated `AMRFinderPlus` Database (`2021-12-21.1`) in an "Always-On" 2025 Surveillance Platform
In the Materials and Methods ("Identification of mechanisms associated with AMR", line 52), the authors state that AMR genes and point mutations are called using **`AMRFinderPlus v3.10.23, database version 2021-12-21.1`**. For a platform analyzed through **31 March 2025** and intended for ongoing situational awareness, using a reference database frozen in December 2021 (>3.25 years old) is a notable technical vulnerability:
* Between 2022 and 2025, numerous clinically critical $\beta$-lactamase/carbapenemase variants (including novel $\text{bla}_{\text{KPC}}$ alleles conferring ceftazidime-avibactam resistance, $\text{bla}_{\text{NDM}}$, and $\text{bla}_{\text{OXA}}$ variants), mobile tigecycline/colistin resistance determinants, and species-specific chromosomal point mutations were added to the NCBI Pathogen Detection Reference Gene Catalog (`AMRFinderPlus v3.12+` / `v4.0`).
* Similarly, while the Introduction (line 16) cites the updated **2024 WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List** (ref. 3), the platform scope and drug-pathogen combinations remain anchored to the **2017 WHO list** (lines 30, 52).

#### 3.2. Exclusion of Long-Read (ONT and PacBio) Sequencing Data (`instrument_platform = "ILLUMINA"` Filter)
In the Methods (line 37), ENA ingestion is restricted to `library_layout="PAIRED"` and `instrument_platform="ILLUMINA"`. While this simplifies short-read assembly with `SPAdes`, Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) and PacBio HiFi sequencing have become major platforms for bacterial AMR genomics since 2021—particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where portable ONT MinION/GridION sequencers have lower infrastructure barriers, and in plasmid epidemiology studies tracking carbapenemase dissemination. Filtering out all non-Illumina runs exacerbates the observed LMIC surveillance gap (89 countries with zero passing genomes).

#### 3.3. Unadjusted Study-Level (`BioProject`) Clustering and Missing Uncertainty Intervals on Country-Year Proportions
In Figures 4 and 5 and the interactive web charts, variant and AMR mechanism frequencies are displayed as raw percentages of genomes per year (`proportion of genomes`). When a country has only a modest number of sequenced isolates in a given year (or when a single research group deposits 300 isolates from a single hospital ICU outbreak under one `study_accession`), the raw percentage fluctuates sharply without reflecting population-level trends. Displaying sample denominators ($n/N$), binomial confidence intervals, or the number of distinct contributing ENA `study_accession` / `BioProject` IDs per year would prevent misinterpretation of focal outbreak dumps as national secular trends.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Upgrade `AMRFinderPlus` to a Current 2025/2026 Database Release and Document Database Versioning Policy
* **WHY:** An "always-on" AMR surveillance platform should not run on a December 2021 reference catalog (`2021-12-21.1`) that predates 3+ years of newly characterized resistance alleles.
* **WHAT & HOW:** Update the `Pathogenwatch` / `amr.watch` backend to a current `AMRFinderPlus` release (or document scheduled annual/biannual database refreshes alongside Supplementary Table 2 curation), display the active `AMRFinderPlus` database version date in the `amr.watch` web footer/about page, and note in the Discussion how transition from the 2017 to the 2024 WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List will be handled.

#### 2. Quantify or Discuss the Volume of Excluded ONT/PacBio Bacterial Genomes in ENA
* **WHY:** Readers and policymakers need to know how much of the apparent LMIC data gap is due to excluding non-Illumina (`ONT` / `PacBio`) submissions.
* **WHAT & HOW:** Query the ENA Portal API using the authors' existing metadata retrieval script without the `instrument_platform="ILLUMINA"` constraint to report in the Results (line 72) how many post-2010 priority pathogen runs in ENA were sequenced on `OXFORD_NANOPORE` or `PACBIO_SMRT`, and note whether future `amr.watch` releases will ingest long-read or hybrid assemblies.

#### 3. Add `study_accession` (`BioProject`) Diversity Metrics or Confidence Intervals to Temporal Trend Interpretations and Fix Typos
* **WHY:** Single-study outbreak uploads can distort annual resistance proportions in countries with small sample sizes.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  * Include the `study_accession` / `BioProject` identifier in the downloadable `amr.watch` CSV files (if not already present) and note in the Results/Discussion that users should check whether temporal spikes in country-level ST or AMR proportions are supported across multiple independent `study_accession` submissions.
  * Provide a public GitHub/GitLab link for the `amr.watch` web application code in the Methods/Data Sharing section.
  * Fix the three typos: `"S. flexnen"` $\to$ `"S. flexneri"` (line 30), `"cefedericol"` $\to$ `"cefiderocol"` (line 85), and `"E. coll"` $\to$ `"E. coli"` (line 96).
