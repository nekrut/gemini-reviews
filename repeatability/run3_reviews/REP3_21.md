### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript introduces linkage disequilibrium (LD) Score regression, a statistical method to separate test statistic inflation in genome-wide association studies (GWAS) caused by true polygenic architecture from inflation driven by confounding biases such as population stratification and cryptic relatedness. Using only summary-level $\chi^2$ statistics and reference LD scores computed from 378 phased European individuals in the 1000 Genomes Project ($M \approx 1.1\text{ million}$ HapMap 3 variants retained for regression), the authors derive the linear expectation $E[\chi^2 | \ell_j] = N h^2 \ell_j / M + N a + 1$, where the intercept $1 + Na$ isolates confounding bias and the slope scales with SNP heritability $h^2$. Across extensive simulations of unstructured polygenicity, continental and national population stratification (using 7 Psychiatric Genomics Consortium cohorts), cryptic relatedness (Framingham Heart Study), and ascertained binary liability traits, the authors show that the LD Score regression intercept tracks true null inflation while remaining near 1.0 under pure polygenicity. Applying the method to summary statistics from 24 GWAS traits, they demonstrate that polygenicity accounts for the majority of $\chi^2$ inflation in large studies and that genomic control ($\lambda_{\text{GC}}$) is overly conservative. Command-line Python software and reference European LD scores are made publicly available.

This is a mathematically rigorous and practically useful contribution to statistical genetics. The derivations, weighted least-squares formulation accounting for correlated test statistics and heteroskedasticity, block-jackknife standard errors, and disattenuation corrections for noisy LD score estimates are thoughtfully constructed. The primary limitations are minor: (1) several mathematical and typographical errors in the main text equations and Online Methods (e.g., the per-allele effect variance exponent in the Introduction and Results, and the bound $r_{\text{adj}}^2 < 1$ instead of $< 0$ in Online Methods); (2) sensitivity of the intercept to reference-target population LD mismatch (up to $\pm 8\%$ mean LD score difference between Finnish and Southern European cohorts) and strong negative selection where causal variants are exclusively rare ($\text{MAF} < 1\%$); and (3) a few broken cross-references to supplementary tables. All of these issues can be addressed through text and equation corrections without new analyses.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **The LD Score regression intercept accurately separates confounding bias from polygenic signal under infinitesimal and moderate frequency-dependent architectures: [Well Supported].**  
  Simulations across 1,000 Swedish individuals, 7 PGC European cohorts with continental and national stratification, and ~22,000 WTCCC2 individuals with simultaneous polygenicity (causal SNPs on first halves of chromosomes) and stratification (null SNPs on second halves) demonstrate that the regression intercept matches the empirical mean $\chi^2$ of true null SNPs (Figure 1, Supplementary Tables 3–5). Liability-threshold simulations confirm invariance to case-control ascertainment (Supplementary Table 6).

* **Polygenicity accounts for the majority of test statistic inflation across >20 complex trait GWAS, rendering $\lambda_{\text{GC}}$ overly conservative: [Well Supported].**  
  Across all 24 studies in Table 1, the regression slope is significantly positive and the intercept is consistently lower than $\lambda_{\text{GC}}$ (mean difference 0.11; e.g., for the trait in row 4 with $N \approx 70,000$, mean $\chi^2 = 1.613$, $\lambda_{\text{GC}} = 1.484$, and intercept $= 1.070 \pm 0.010$). Block-jackknife standard errors over 2,000-SNP blocks properly account for spatial LD correlation.

* **A single pan-European 1000 Genomes reference panel ($N = 378$) is adequate for LD Score regression across European-ancestry GWAS: [Moderately Supported].**  
  The authors transparently quantify intra-European LD Score variation across CEU (109), GBR (104), FIN (117), and TSI (102) relative to combined EUR (110). However, their worst-case calculation in Online Methods shows that applying the combined EUR panel to a homogeneous Finnish or Southern European cohort can bias the estimated proportion of confounding inflation by up to $\pm 10\%$, and noisy reference mismatch systematically biases the intercept upward.

* **The slope of LD Score regression provides a general estimator of genome-wide SNP heritability: [Fragile].**  
  As the authors acknowledge in the Results ("Frequency-Dependent Genetic Architectures"), the derivation assumes that variance explained per SNP is uncorrelated with LD Score. When causal variants are concentrated at low frequencies ($\text{MAF} < 1\%$) due to strong purifying selection, the slope frequently becomes negative and the intercept exceeds the mean $\chi^2$ (Supplementary Figure 7). Thus, heritability estimation from the slope requires strong assumptions about the MAF-effect size relationship.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Correct Mathematical Notation for Per-Allele Effect Size Variance and Unbiased $r^2$
* **WHY:** In the Introduction (line 15), the text states that effect sizes are drawn with variance proportional to $p(1-p)^{-1/2}$, and in the Results (line 46), per-allele effect sizes are written as drawn from $N(0, h^2 / (2p(1-p))^{-1/2} / M)$. For per-normalized-genotype effect sizes to have constant variance $h^2/M$ (so that $2p(1-p)\beta_{\text{allele}}^2 = h^2/M$), the per-allele variance must be proportional to $[2p(1-p)]^{-1}$, or the standard deviation proportional to $[2p(1-p)]^{-1/2}$. Additionally, in Online Methods (line 136), the text states "it is possible to have $\hat{r}_{\text{adj}}^2 < 1$, which is a mathematically necessary feature of any unbiased estimator of $r^2$." This is a typo for $\hat{r}_{\text{adj}}^2 < 0$.
* **WHAT:** Fix the exponents in the variance expressions in the Introduction and Results, and change $< 1$ to $< 0$ in the unbiased correlation estimator discussion.
* **HOW:** Replace $p(1-p)^{-1/2}$ with $[2p(1-p)]^{-1}$ for variance (or specify standard deviation $\propto [2p(1-p)]^{-1/2}$) on lines 15 and 46, and update $\hat{r}_{\text{adj}}^2 < 1$ to $\hat{r}_{\text{adj}}^2 < 0$ on line 136 while keeping the subsequent sentence that total LD Score $\hat{\ell}_j$ (which includes the self-term $r_{jj}^2 = 1$) can consequently fall below 1.

#### 2. Provide Subpopulation-Matched Reference Panel Sensitivity Analyses for Table 1 Traits
* **WHY:** Several meta-analyses in Table 1 include substantial Finnish or Southern European cohorts, where a 7–8% systematic shift in reference LD Score can shift the intercept by up to $7 \times \text{slope}$.
* **WHAT:** Quantify how much the intercepts in Table 1 change when using GBR/CEU-only versus ancestry-weighted EUR LD Scores.
* **HOW:** Re-run the Table 1 regressions using the CEU/GBR reference LD Scores and report the maximum absolute change in the intercept across the 24 phenotypes in a supplementary table column.

#### 3. Formalize the Linked Selection Bias Bound on the Slope and Intercept
* **WHY:** In simulations with pure population stratification (lines 51–53), the regression slope was slightly positive on average (Supplementary Tables 3c, 4c), which the authors attribute to linked/background selection inducing a correlation between $F_{\text{ST}}$ and LD Score, yet they do not provide an explicit analytical expression for this bias in the main text.
* **WHAT:** Add the covariance term $\text{Cov}(F_{\text{ST}}, \ell_j)$ to the expected $\chi^2$ equation under stratification so readers can see directly when background selection inflates the slope vs. underestimates the intercept.
* **HOW:** Expand Equation (1) in the Methods or Discussion to show $E[\chi_j^2 | \ell_j] \approx 1 + N a_0 + N (\frac{h^2}{M} + b \frac{\text{Cov}(F_{\text{ST}}, \ell)}{\text{Var}(\ell)}) \ell_j$ and report the empirical magnitude of $\text{Cov}(F_{\text{ST}}, \ell_j) / \text{Var}(\ell_j)$ observed across the 7 PGC cohorts.
