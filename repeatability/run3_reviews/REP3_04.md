### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This benchmarking study evaluates how biological replication ($n_r = 2, \dots, 40$) and expression fold-change thresholds ($T = |\log_2\text{FC}| \in \{0, 0.3, 1, 2\}$) govern the sensitivity and false-positive control of 11 widely used RNA-seq differential gene expression (DGE) tools (`baySeq`, `cuffdiff`, `DEGSeq`, `DESeq`, `DESeq2`, `EBSeq`, `edgeR` [exact and GLM], `limma`, `NOISeq`, `PoissonSeq`, and `SAMSeq`). To avoid relying on synthetic simulations or heterogeneous inter-individual cohorts, the authors generated a dedicated RNA-seq dataset of 48 biological replicates per condition in *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* (wild-type BY4741 vs. $\Delta\text{snf2}$ chromatin-remodeling mutant) using a randomized balanced block design across four library batches and seven Illumina HiSeq 2000 lanes with ERCC spike-in controls, yielding 42 WT and 44 $\Delta\text{snf2}$ clean replicates ($\sim 889\text{M}$ aligned reads). Using 100-iteration subsampling without replacement against tool-specific full-dataset gold standards, hierarchical clustering against five nonparametric/parametric reference tests, and WT-vs.-WT null resampling, the authors show that at $n_r = 3$, nine of the 11 tools recover only $20\%\text{--}40\%$ of all full-dataset differentially expressed genes (rising to $>85\%$ for genes with $|\log_2\text{FC}| \ge 2$), whereas recovering $>85\%$ across all fold changes requires $n_r > 20$. All raw sequencing reads are deposited in the European Nucleotide Archive (`PRJEB5348`) and all benchmarking code is publicly available on GitHub (`https://github.com/bartongroup/profDGE48`).

The experimental design, execution, and open-data/open-code transparency are exemplary, and the authors appropriately frame low-variance clonal yeast cultures ($R > 0.97$ between replicates) as an upper bound on DGE tool performance in more complex eukaryotic transcriptomes. Two methodological issues should be addressed to strengthen the statistical interpretation. First, throughout the Abstract, Results, and Figure 4, the manuscript conflates the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate ($\text{FDR} = \mathbb{E}[\text{FP}/(\text{FP}+\text{TP})]$) with the false positive rate ($\text{FPR} = \text{FP}/(\text{FP}+\text{TN})$, Equation 2). In the WT-vs.-WT null resampling experiment (Figure 4), where no genes are truly differentially expressed ($\text{TP} = 0$), calling up to $5\%$ of all 7,126 genes ($\sim 356$ genes) as significantly differentially expressed at an adjusted $P \le 0.05$ is treated as adequate "5% FPR/FDR control," when in reality a well-calibrated BH procedure under the complete null controls the family-wise error rate (the probability of calling *even one* gene out of 7,126) at $\le 5\%$. Second, because $60\%\text{--}75\%$ of the yeast transcriptome is called differentially expressed in the $\Delta\text{snf2}$ full dataset (line 40), the core assumption of endogenous count normalizations (TMM, DESeq median-of-ratios) that most genes are invariant is violated; leveraging the ERCC spike-ins already sequenced in every sample would provide an independent check on normalization bias.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **With $n_r = 3$ biological replicates per condition, well-calibrated DGE tools detect $>85\%$ of high-fold-change genes ($|\log_2\text{FC}| \ge 2$) but only $20\%\text{--}40\%$ of all differentially expressed genes identified at $n_r \ge 42$: [Well Supported].**  
  The subsampling curves (Figures 1B–C and 2A–F; Supplemental Figures S2–S12) clearly demonstrate the steep drop-off in statistical power for small-to-moderate fold changes ($|\log_2\text{FC}| < 1$) at $n_r = 3$, and Figure 3 confirms that the full-dataset calls for `DESeq2`, `edgeR` (exact), `limma`, `DESeq`, `EBSeq`, and `cuffdiff` converge closely with distribution-free permutation, Mann–Whitney, and bootstrap tests.

* **Nine of the 11 evaluated tools successfully control their false discovery rate at $\lesssim 5\%$ across all replicate numbers, as demonstrated by the WT-vs.-WT null test (Figure 4): [Overstated].**  
  The manuscript equates a $5\%$ Benjamini–Hochberg FDR cutoff ($q \le 0.05$) with calling fewer than $5\%$ of all 7,126 yeast genes ($\sim 356$ false positives) in the artificial WT-vs.-WT null comparison (Figure 4, red line at $y = 0.05$). Mathematically:
  1. In two-condition WT-vs.-$\Delta\text{snf2}$ subsampling (Equation 2, Figure 2), $\text{FPR} = \text{FP}/(\text{FP}+\text{TN})$ is the specificity complement, whereas $\text{FDR} = \text{FP}/(\text{FP}+\text{TP})$.
  2. In the WT-vs.-WT null comparison (Figure 4), every gene obeys $H_0$ ($\text{TP} = 0$), so any run with $\text{FP} \ge 1$ has a realized $\text{FDR}$ of $100\%$. Under the complete null, BH adjustment at $\alpha = 0.05$ requires $P_{(k)} \le 0.05 \cdot k / 7126$, which guarantees that across 100 independent null runs, at least 95 runs should return *zero* SDE genes ($\text{FP} = 0$), not up to 356 SDE genes ($\text{FPR} = 0.05$). While `DESeq`, `EBSeq`, `baySeq`, `edgeR`, `DESeq2`, and `limma` have median WT-vs.-WT false-positive fractions near zero, `cuffdiff`, `PoissonSeq`, and `SAMSeq` frequently return dozens to hundreds of false positives under the complete null and therefore fail to control FDR at $5\%$.

* **`DEGSeq` and `NOISeq` exhibit severe false-positive inflation across replicate counts: [Well Supported].**  
  Both the full-dataset comparison ($>80\%$ of genes called SDE) and the WT-vs.-WT null resampling (Figure 4, where `DEGSeq` calls a median of $\sim 50\%$ of all WT genes as false positives at every $n_r$) conclusively demonstrate that `DEGSeq` (which lacks biological overdispersion modeling) and `NOISeq` fail to control Type I error.

* **Full-dataset gold standards (60%–75% of all yeast genes called SDE) reflect true biological differential expression under endogenous normalization: [Moderately Supported].**  
  While agreement with nonparametric tests at $n = 42\text{ vs. }44$ supports statistical significance of count-ratio shifts, Materials and Methods line 146 correctly notes that default endogenous normalizations assume that the majority of genes do not change expression between conditions. In a global chromatin-remodeling mutant ($\Delta\text{snf2}$) where $60\%\text{--}75\%$ of genes change and total extracted RNA varied from $30.3$ to $126.9\ \mu\text{g}$ (line 131), global composition shifts can induce apparent small-fold-change DGE ($|\log_2\text{FC}| < 0.3$) in invariant genes unless calibrated against the ERCC spike-in controls included in the experiment.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Distinguish False Discovery Rate (FDR), False Positive Rate (FPR), and Complete-Null Family-Wise Error Rate
* **WHY:** Using "FDR" in the Abstract and Discussion to describe $\text{FPR} = \text{FP}/(\text{FP}+\text{TN})$ (Equation 2) and interpreting the $5\%$ fraction-of-genes line in Figure 4 as nominal BH-FDR control confuses three distinct statistical quantities.
* **WHAT:** Compute and report the empirical False Discovery Rate ($\widehat{\text{FDR}} = \text{FP}/(\text{FP}+\text{TP})$) alongside $\text{FPR}$ in the WT-vs.-$\Delta\text{snf2}$ subsampling analyses, and evaluate the WT-vs.-WT null experiment (Figure 4) against the exact BH complete-null expectation (the proportion of the 100 bootstrap iterations returning $\ge 1$ false-positive gene, which should be $\le 5\%$).
* **HOW:** Query the existing SQLite database of bootstrap outputs (`profDGE48`) to calculate: (i) $\text{FDR}(n_r, T) = \text{FP}(n_r, T)/[\text{FP}(n_r, T) + \text{TP}(n_r, T)]$ for Figure 2, and (ii) for the WT-vs.-WT runs in Figure 4, tabulate for each tool and each $n_r$ both the fraction of iterations with $\text{FP} > 0$ (empirical FWER, nominal target $\le 0.05$) and the distribution of raw $P$-values against a $\text{Uniform}(0,1)$ quantile-quantile reference.

#### 2. Cross-Check the Endogenous Normalization Gold Standard Against the Sequenced ERCC Spike-In Controls
* **WHY:** When $60\%\text{--}75\%$ of the yeast transcriptome is called SDE in $\Delta\text{snf2}$, median-ratio (`DESeq`/`DESeq2`) and TMM (`edgeR`) scaling factors can be shifted by asymmetric global transcriptional changes, turning truly unchanged genes into apparent small-fold-change SDE calls at $n_r = 42$.
* **WHAT:** Compare the endogenous DESeq size factors against size factors derived from the 92 ERCC spike-in transcripts sequenced in Mix 1 ($\Delta\text{snf2}$) and Mix 2 (WT) (Materials and Methods, line 136).
* **HOW:** Align reads to the ERCC reference sequences, extract counts for the constant-ratio (1:1) ERCC spike-in subgroup across the 42 WT and 44 $\Delta\text{snf2}$ clean libraries, and compute spike-in normalization factors. Report the log2 offset between endogenous DESeq normalization and ERCC normalization, and verify whether the transition fold-change threshold ($T \approx 0.15\text{--}0.3$, Figure 1C) corresponds to the magnitude of normalization uncertainty.

#### 3. Evaluate Split-Half / Disjoint Gold Standards to Eliminate Sample Overlap Bias at High $n_r$
* **WHY:** In Figure 1 and Figure 2, the $n_r$-replicate bootstrap subsamples are drawn from the same 42 WT and 44 $\Delta\text{snf2}$ replicates used to define the gold standard. As $n_r \to 40$, the subsample and gold-standard datasets share $>90\%$ of their observations, guaranteeing algebraic convergence of TPR to $1.0$ and FPR to $0.0$.
* **WHAT:** Add a disjoint split-sample validation curve for $n_r \in \{2, 3, \dots, 20\}$ where each $n_r$-replicate subsample is evaluated against a gold standard computed on the *remaining disjoint* $(42 - n_r)$ WT and $(44 - n_r)$ $\Delta\text{snf2}$ replicates (or a fixed held-out set of 22 WT vs. 22 $\Delta\text{snf2}$ replicates).
* **HOW:** For `edgeR` (exact), `DESeq2`, `DESeq`, and `limma`, partition the clean dataset into a 20-replicate discovery pool and a disjoint 22 WT / 24 $\Delta\text{snf2}$ reference pool across 50 random splits, and plot the disjoint TPR and FDR for $n_r = 2, \dots, 20$ alongside Figure 1B.

#### 4. Clarify why `edgeR` (GLM) Clusters Separately from `edgeR` (Exact) in Figure 3
* **WHY:** In Figure 3 (line 68), `edgeR (exact)` clusters tightly with `DESeq2` and `limma` in Cluster 3, whereas `edgeR (GLM)` falls into the outlier Cluster 2 alongside `DEGSeq`, `NOISeq`, and `baySeq`, yet the Discussion (line 90) recommends running the top tools in GLM mode to adjust for covariates.
* **WHAT:** Explain in the Results and Materials and Methods why `edgeR (GLM)` diverged from `edgeR (exact)` on the identical two-group dataset (e.g., whether tagwise vs. common/trended dispersion estimation was omitted in the GLM call script) and verify that `edgeR (GLM)` with `estimateGLMTagwiseDisp` matches `edgeR (exact)`.
* **HOW:** Inspect the `edgeR` GLM execution script in the `profDGE48` repository, confirm whether `estimateGLMCommonDisp` + `estimateGLMTrendedDisp` + `estimateGLMTagwiseDisp` were all called prior to `glmFit`/`glmLRT`, and update Figure 3 and Supplemental Figure S3 accordingly.

#### 5. Technical, Mathematical, and Editorial Cleanup
* **WHY:** A minor contradiction exists between the Results and Materials and Methods regarding how bootstrap iterations were aggregated.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  1. **Results (line 42) vs. Materials and Methods (line 155):** Reconcile the description of how TPR/FPR were computed. Line 42 states that the median adjusted $P$-value across all 100 bootstrap iterations was calculated first for each gene and then thresholded at $\le 0.05$ to compute TPR/FPR, whereas line 155 and the Figure 1/2 captions state that TP, TN, FP, and FN were counted *within each individual bootstrap run* and then averaged across the 100 runs. Update line 42 so that it matches the exact calculation in line 155.
  2. **Materials and Methods (line 199):** Standardize the symbol for degrees of freedom in Equation 5 (`\nu` vs. `v` on the same line).
