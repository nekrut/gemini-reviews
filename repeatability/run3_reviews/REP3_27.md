### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript analyzes the genome sequence of the novel coronavirus (`2019-nCoV`, GenBank `MN908947`) alongside 271 coronavirus genomes downloaded from GenBank to infer its evolutionary origin, recombination history, and putative wildlife reservoir. Using maximum-likelihood phylogenetics (`RAxML` GTR+G), sliding-window similarity/bootscan plots (`SimPlot`), and squared Euclidean distance of relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) profiles (`CodonW`) across `2019-nCoV`, `bat-SL-CoVZC45`, and eight candidate vertebrate species (*Bungarus multicinctus*, *Naja atra*, *Rhinolophus sinicus*, *Gallus gallus*, *Marmota*, *Homo sapiens*, *Manis javanica*, and *Erinaceus europaeus*), the authors conclude that: (1) `2019-nCoV` is sister to bat SARS-like coronaviruses `bat-SL-CoVZC45` and `bat-SL-CoVZXC21` (Clade A); (2) `2019-nCoV` arose via homologous recombination within the spike glycoprotein gene between a Clade A bat coronavirus and an "origin-unknown coronavirus"; and (3) snakes (*B. multicinctus* and *N. atra*) are the most likely wildlife animal reservoir of `2019-nCoV` because their RSCU profiles have the smallest squared Euclidean distance ($13.54$ and $16.69$) to `2019-nCoV`.

While the basic phylogenetic placement of `2019-nCoV` within *Betacoronavirus* sister to `bat-SL-CoVZC45`/`ZXC21` is correct, the manuscript's two primary novel claims—that snakes are the intermediate animal reservoir and that the spike sequence divergence proves homologous recombination with an unknown virus—are methodologically invalid and biologically unfounded. First, RSCU distance across a handful of unrepresentative GenBank entries (38 CDSs for *B. multicinctus*, 64 CDSs for *N. atra*, and only 10 CDSs for the bat host *R. sinicus*, compared against 28,947 to 49,453 whole-genome CDSs for the other mammals/birds) is severely biased by massive sampling skew and ignores the well-established fact that coronavirus A/U-rich codon bias is driven by intrinsic viral mutational pressure (and ZAP-mediated CpG dinucleotide depletion) rather than host translational co-adaptation. Second, a drop in nucleotide similarity in the Spike S1 receptor-binding region relative to `bat-SL-CoVZC45` on a 3-clade `SimPlot` does not distinguish homologous recombination from accelerated diversifying selection or lineage divergence without an identified second parental lineage or formal mosaic breakpoint test. Third, no betacoronavirus has ever been shown to infect ectothermic reptiles (snakes).

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **`2019-nCoV` has the most similar codon usage bias to snakes (*Bungarus multicinctus* and *Naja atra*), indicating that snakes are the likely wildlife reservoir: [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  This conclusion rests on three compounding methodological and biological errors:
  1. **Severe ascertainment and sample-size skew in CDS selection (Section 2.3, line 50):** For *Erinaceus europaeus*, *Marmota*, *Manis javanica*, *Gallus gallus*, and *Homo sapiens*, the authors computed genome-wide RSCU across 28,947 to 49,453 coding sequences ($16.7\text{M}\text{--}40.7\text{M}$ codons). In contrast, for *Rhinolophus sinicus* (the natural horseshoe bat host of SARS-related coronaviruses), they used only **10 CDSs** (8,081 codons), and for the two snakes they used only **38 CDSs** (*B. multicinctus*, 5,381 codons) and **64 CDSs** (*N. atra*, 9,587 codons)—small, heavily biased sets of partial submissions in GenBank (predominantly venom-gland toxin and mitochondrial/housekeeping transcripts) that do not represent the snake genome-wide codon usage.
  2. **Failure of RSCU Euclidean distance as a viral host-prediction metric:** All coronaviruses exhibit strong intrinsic A+U compositional bias and extreme CpG suppression (`CCG`, `GCG`, `UCG`, `ACG` all have RSCU $\le 0.26$ in Table 1), regardless of whether their natural host is a bat, bird, pig, camel, or human. As shown in Table 1, `bat-SL-CoVZC45`—a known bat virus isolated directly from *Rhinolophus sinicus*—has virtually identical RSCU values to `2019-nCoV` across every codon and is therefore also much closer in Euclidean RSCU distance to the 38 *B. multicinctus* genes than to its own true host *R. sinicus*! Had the authors used `bat-SL-CoVZC45` as a positive control for their method, it would have falsely predicted that a known bat virus is a snake virus.
  3. **Biological implausibility:** *Betacoronavirus* species infect exclusivamente endothermic mammals; there is zero virological or receptor-binding evidence that sarbecoviruses can replicate in ectothermic reptiles.

* **`2019-nCoV` is a recombinant virus between `bat-SL-CoVZC45`/`ZXC21` and an "origin-unknown" coronavirus within the spike glycoprotein: [Overstated].**  
  In Figure 2, the `SimPlot` compares `2019-nCoV` against only three grouped curves: Clade A (`bat-SL-CoVZC45`/`ZXC21`), Clade B (`BM48-31`/`BtKY72`), and Clade C (all other 267 coronaviruses). In the Spike 5' region (`1–783 bp` and S1 domain), sequence similarity to Clade A drops below Clade B/C or falls to low levels, and a NCBI BLAST search of positions `1–783` found no close match in GenBank (line 150). A localized trough in sequence identity between two relatives in a hypervariable surface antigen (Spike S1) is the expected signature of rapid positive selection, indels, or ancestral polymorphism—not proof of homologous recombination. Establishing homologous recombination via bootscanning requires demonstrating statistically significant phylogenetic incongruence where the recombinant region clusters strongly with a second identifiable parental clade (`RDP4`, $\chi^2$ max-chi, or Shimodaira–Hasegawa topology tests), which is absent here ("Bootscanning plot analysis (data not shown)", line 150).

* **`2019-nCoV` clusters within *Betacoronavirus* as a sister lineage to bat SARS-like coronaviruses `bat-SL-CoVZC45` and `bat-SL-CoVZXC21`: [Well Supported].**  
  The maximum-likelihood phylogenetic reconstruction (`RAxML` GTR+G with 1,000 bootstrap replicates; Figure 1) correctly places `2019-nCoV` (`MN908947`) in a monophyletic lineage sister to the Zhoushan bat coronaviruses `bat-SL-CoVZC45` and `bat-SL-CoVZXC21`, distinct from Clade B (`BM48-31`/`BtKY72`) and classic human `SARS-CoV`.

* **`2019-nCoV` causes only mild pneumonia and has limited capability for person-to-person spread due to spike recombination: [Overstated].**  
  In the Introduction (line 24) and Discussion (line 154), the authors claim that *"Unlike SARS-CoV, the 2019-nCoV appeared to initially cause a mild form of viral pneumonia and have limited capability for person-person spread. This might be due to the recombination occurred within the receptor-binding glycoprotein."* Making definitive epidemiological and phenotypic claims of "mild pneumonia" and "limited capability for person-person spread" from sequence data alone—when the authors' own Introduction (line 20) notes that 7 of the first 27 cases (26%) and 11 of 44 cases (25%) were critically ill and 9 of 217 had already died—contradicts the clinical data presented in the paper.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Validate RSCU Host Prediction Against Known Coronavirus-Host Pairs and Use Complete Reference Genomes
* **WHY:** Computing RSCU from 10 CDSs for *Rhinolophus sinicus* and 38–64 CDSs for snakes while using $>28,000$ whole-genome CDSs for mammals/birds introduces severe sampling artifact, and the authors never tested whether minimum RSCU Euclidean distance correctly identifies *R. sinicus* as the host of `bat-SL-CoVZC45`.
* **WHAT:** Replace the 10-CDS and 38/64-CDS GenBank subsets with whole-genome coding annotations for *Rhinolophus sinicus*, *Bungarus multicinctus*, and *Naja atra* (or matched core orthologous gene sets across all species), and benchmark RSCU Euclidean distance across a control panel of coronaviruses with known hosts (`bat-SL-CoVZC45` $\to$ *R. sinicus*, `SARS-CoV` $\to$ *R. sinicus* / *Paguma larvata* / *Homo sapiens*, `MERS-CoV` $\to$ *Camelus dromedarius*, avian `IBV` $\to$ *Gallus gallus*).
* **HOW:** Extract all annotated CDSs from the whole-genome assemblies of *R. sinicus* (NCBI RefSeq `GCF_001888835.1`) and available colubrid/elapid snake genome assemblies (e.g., *Ophiophagus hannah* or *Notechis scutatus*), recompute Table 1 and the squared Euclidean distances in Figure 3B, and verify whether `bat-SL-CoVZC45` clusters with its true bat host. Since `bat-SL-CoVZC45` and `2019-nCoV` cluster together away from all vertebrate hosts due to viral background G+C content ($\sim 38\%$) and CpG dinucleotide depletion, retract the claim that RSCU implicates snakes as the reservoir.

#### 2. Perform Formal Recombination Breakpoint and Phylogenetic Incongruence Testing
* **WHY:** A drop in pairwise nucleotide similarity in Spike S1 relative to `bat-SL-CoVZC45` in `SimPlot` (Figure 2) does not differentiate homologous recombination from rapid sequence evolution or lineage sorting when no second parent is present in the alignment.
* **WHAT:** Run formal recombination detection algorithms (`RDP4` suite: RDP, GENECONV, BootScan, MaxChi, Chimaera, SiScan, and 3Seq) and construct separate maximum-likelihood trees for the putative non-recombinant backbone (`ORF1ab`), the putative recombinant region (`Spike 1–783 bp`), and the remainder of the genome.
* **HOW:** Show the bootscan plot (currently omitted as "data not shown" on line 150) alongside separate ML trees with bootstrap support for the Spike S1 region vs. `ORF1ab`. If `2019-nCoV` remains sister to `bat-SL-CoVZC45`/`ZXC21` (or forms an outgroup to them) without switching topological position into a distinct known clade, reframe the observation as "localized sequence divergence in Spike S1 (potentially reflecting positive selection or ancestral recombination with an unsampled lineage)" rather than asserting homologous recombination as an established fact.

#### 3. Reconcile Sequence Counts and Correct Factual Errors in Geographic Origin
* **WHY:** There are multiple numerical and factual errors in the Methods and Results:
  1. **Sequence count mismatch:** Section 2.1 (line 32) states that *"271 genome sequences (>19 000 bp in length) were used in this study together with the above-described Beta-coronavirus (2019-nCoV, MN908947)"* = **272** sequences (which matches the Figure 1 caption: *"272 near-complete genome sequences"*, line 45, and Discussion line 150). However, Section 3.1 (line 58) states *"Phylogenetic analysis of 276 coronavirus genomes..."* and sums $1\text{ (2019-nCoV)} + 2\text{ (Clade A)} + 2\text{ (Clade B)} + 267\text{ (Clade C)} = \mathbf{272}$, not 276.
  2. **CDS count for coronavirus genomes:** Section 2.3 (line 50) states that `2019-nCoV` (`MN908947`) and `bat-SL-CoVZC45` each have `"1CDS's, 9672 codons"` and `"1CDS's, 9680 codons"`. The coronavirus genome contains multiple open reading frames (`ORF1ab`, `S`, `ORF3a`, `E`, `M`, `ORF6`, `ORF7a`, `ORF7b`, `ORF8`, `N`), not a single concatenated CDS.
  3. **Geographic origin of `bat-SL-CoVZC45` and `bat-SL-CoVZXC21`:** Lines 39 and 58 state that these two viruses were *"obtained from the city of Nanjing, China."* According to their GenBank records (`MG772933` and `MG772934`), they were collected from *Rhinolophus sinicus* bats in **Zhoushan** city, Zhejiang province (not Nanjing; the submitting institute was Nanjing Command).
* **WHAT & HOW:** Change `276` to `272` on line 58; compute coronavirus RSCU across the properly annotated individual viral coding sequences (correcting for the `ORF1ab` ribosomal frameshift); and correct the sampling location of `bat-SL-CoVZC45` and `bat-SL-CoVZXC21` from Nanjing to Zhoushan, Zhejiang.
