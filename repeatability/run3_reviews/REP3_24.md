### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This multicenter cross-sectional study profiles the gut microbiota via V3–V4 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing of stool samples collected on the first day of admission from 374 pulmonary and critical care medicine (PCCM) inpatients across three Chinese hospitals (comprising 81 airway disease, 104 lung infection, 37 respiratory failure, 128 non-small cell lung cancer [NSCLC], and 24 other cases) compared against 105 community healthy controls. Using QIIME 1.9.1 with 97% OTU clustering against GreenGenes 13.8, the authors report reduced $\alpha$-diversity (ACE, Chao1, Shannon) in inpatients, modest $\beta$-diversity separation (ANOSIM $R = 0.0371$, $p = 0.001$), an inpatient-exclusive *Enterococcaceae* enterotype (present in 7.3–15.4% of inpatients across the three hospitals), 15 differentially abundant bacterial families, distance-based redundancy analysis (db-RDA) correlations with age, medical costs, and hospital stay, and a genus-level Random Forest classifier distinguishing inpatients from healthy controls with $\text{AUC} = 0.75$ (95% CI $0.69\text{--}0.80$). Raw sequencing data are deposited in the GSA (`CRA012416`).

While the sample size ($N = 479$) and multi-hospital collection on admission day are strengths, the study's core conclusions regarding the "gut–lung axis" and "specific gut microbiota signatures in patients with different respiratory diseases" are undermined by severe confounding, internal contradictions, and unvalidated machine learning: (1) the inpatient and healthy control groups differ dramatically in baseline smoking history (53.5% vs. 28.6%, $p < 0.01$), pack-years ($42.8 \pm 25.7$ vs. $19.2 \pm 20.4$, $p < 0.01$), and BMI ($21.37$ vs. $23.26\text{ kg/m}^2$, $p < 0.01$), yet the core diversity, Wilcoxon rank-sum, ANOSIM, enterotype, and Random Forest analyses do not control for smoking or BMI; (2) the four heterogeneous disease categories (airway disease, lung infection, respiratory failure, NSCLC) show virtually identical microbiome shifts (Figure 6 and Figure 7), indicating a general hospitalization/illness/smoking effect rather than disease-specific "gut–lung axis" mechanisms; (3) the Random Forest classifier ($\text{AUC} = 0.75$) is trained and evaluated without an independent hold-out test set or cross-hospital external validation; and (4) the text contradicts itself on basic counts (e.g., claiming "four enterotypes" in the Abstract and Discussion while the Results and Figure 3C–D explicitly identify three enterotypes).

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **PCCM inpatients exhibit lower gut $\alpha$-diversity and altered community structure compared to healthy controls, driven by the gut–lung axis: [Overstated].**  
  While $\alpha$-diversity is lower in inpatients (Figure 1) and Bray–Curtis ANOSIM is statistically significant due to $N = 479$, the effect size of community separation is tiny ($R = 0.0371$, $R^2 = 0.035$; Figures 2–3). More importantly, Table 1 reveals massive baseline confounding between inpatients and healthy controls in smoking prevalence (53.5% vs. 28.6%), smoking pack-years (42.8 vs. 19.2), and BMI ($21.37$ vs. $23.26\text{ kg/m}^2$). Although the Methods (line 76) state *"the analyses were further adjusted for age and smoking history,"* the actual tests reported throughout the Results are unadjusted non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, Kruskal–Wallis tests, one-way ANOVAs, and ANOSIM—none of which adjust for covariates. Furthermore, inpatients with acute lung infections ($n=104$) or respiratory failure ($n=37$) frequently receive pre-admission outpatient or emergency department antimicrobials or supportive care, and grouping metastatic/inpatient NSCLC ($n=128$) with acute pneumonia under a single "gut–lung axis" umbrella conflates systemic illness, cachexia, and smoking with pulmonary-specific cross-talk.

* **Different respiratory diseases (airway disease, lung infection, respiratory failure, NSCLC) possess specific gut microbiota signatures: [Overstated].**  
  Despite the section heading *"Specific gut microbiota signature in patients with different respiratory diseases"* (line 178), Figures 6 and 7 demonstrate the exact opposite: there are no meaningful differences *between* the four respiratory disease groups. All four groups show the same reduction in Shannon diversity (Figure 6A), the same lack of phylum-level differences (Figure 7A), and the same increase in *Enterococcaceae* and decrease in *Lachnospiraceae* and *Prevotellaceae* relative to healthy controls (Figure 7B). Thus, the signature is a non-specific marker of being a hospitalized patient rather than a specific signature of any respiratory disease.

* **A Random Forest classifier using 15 genera accurately distinguishes PCCM inpatients from healthy controls ($\text{AUC} = 0.75$): [Fragile].**  
  The Methods (line 76) and Results (line 175) provide no description of train/test splitting, nested cross-validation, or handling of the 3.5:1 class imbalance ($n = 374$ inpatients vs. $n = 105$ healthy controls). Performing feature selection (retaining the top 15 genera by variable importance) on the full dataset before plotting the ROC curve leaks test information and inflates the apparent AUC. Even though the study collected samples across three independent hospitals, the authors did not perform leave-one-hospital-out validation.

* **Four distinct enterotypes are present in the study population, with the *Enterococcaceae* enterotype exclusive to PCCM inpatients: [Moderately Supported].**  
  PAM clustering on family-level Jensen–Shannon distance identifies an *Enterococcaceae*-dominated cluster present in 7.3–15.4% of inpatients across all three hospitals and 0% of healthy controls (Figure 3C, D), which is consistent with known *Enterococcus* overgrowth in hospitalized patients. However, the manuscript contradicts itself repeatedly on the number of enterotypes: the Abstract (line 21) and Discussion (line 201) claim *"Four enterotypes, including the exclusive Enterococcaceae enterotype in inpatients, were identified,"* whereas the Results (line 146) and Figure 3C–D explicitly state and plot *"three distinct enterotypes... Lachnospiraceae (Enterotype 1), Enterobacteriaceae (Enterotype 2) and Enterococcaceae (Enterotype 3)."*

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Control for Confounding by Smoking, BMI, Age, and Center Using Multivariable Models
* **WHY:** Unadjusted Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, Kruskal–Wallis tests, and ANOSIM cannot separate disease status from the nearly two-fold higher smoking prevalence and significantly lower BMI in the inpatient group (Table 1).
* **WHAT:** Re-analyze $\alpha$-diversity, $\beta$-diversity, and differential family/genus abundance using multivariable models that explicitly include smoking status, pack-years, BMI, age, sex, and hospital center as covariates.
* **HOW:**
  * For $\alpha$-diversity: Fit linear regression models $\text{Diversity} \sim \text{Group} + \text{Age} + \text{Sex} + \text{BMI} + \text{Smoking\_Status} + \text{Pack\_Years}$ and report the adjusted $\beta$ and $p$-value for `Group`.
  * For $\beta$-diversity: Run multivariable PERMANOVA (`vegan::adonis2` with `by = "margin"`) including `BMI`, `Smoking`, `Age`, `Hospital`, and `Group` to quantify the unique variance ($R^2$) explained by inpatient status after accounting for confounders.
  * For taxon differential abundance: Use `MaAsLin2` or `ANCOM-BC2` with `Age`, `Sex`, `BMI`, and `Smoking` as fixed effects and `Hospital` as a random effect, replacing the unadjusted Wilcoxon tests and compositional ANOVA in Figures 4, 5, and 7.

#### 2. Validate the Random Forest Classifier Across Hospitals with Nested Feature Selection
* **WHY:** Feature selection on the full cohort followed by in-sample or unnested ROC evaluation overfits the data, and failing to leverage the three-hospital design misses a natural external validation test.
* **WHAT:** Implement leave-one-hospital-out cross-validation (training and selecting features on two hospitals, testing on the third) and compare the microbiome classifier against a baseline clinical classifier using only `Age + Sex + BMI + Smoking`.
* **HOW:** Train the Random Forest (performing feature selection strictly inside each training fold) on Hospital 1 + Hospital 2 and evaluate out-of-sample AUC on Hospital 3 (repeating for all three hospital hold-outs), and report whether the 15 microbial genera add statistically significant predictive power (DeLong test) over `BMI + Smoking` alone.

#### 3. Reconcile Internal Contradictions and Correct the Direction of the Simpson Index
* **WHY:** Several direct contradictions between text and figures undermine technical credibility:
  1. **Enterotype count:** Abstract (line 21) and Discussion (line 201) state "Four enterotypes," while Results (line 146) and Figure 3C–D show three enterotypes.
  2. **Simpson diversity direction:** In ecology and QIIME, the Simpson dominance index ($D = \sum p_i^2$) increases as diversity *decreases*. The Results (line 124) correctly note that *"the Simpson index was significantly higher than that of healthy control,"* but the Discussion (line 199) contradicts this by stating *"The lower alpha diversity indices (ACE, Shannon, Chao, and Simpson) observed in inpatients..."*
  3. **Gender percentage in Table 1:** In Table 1 (line 98), 252 males out of 374 total patients is **67.4%**, not `69.6%` as printed ($252 / 374 = 0.6738$).
* **WHAT & HOW:** Correct "Four enterotypes" to "Three enterotypes" in the Abstract and Discussion; clarify in the Discussion that the classical Simpson dominance index was *higher* (reflecting lower evenness/diversity) in inpatients; and fix the male percentage in Table 1 from `69.6%` to `67.4%` (or clarify if 12 patients had missing sex data).

#### 4. Reframe Claims Around Hospitalization-Associated Dysbiosis Rather Than Disease-Specific Gut–Lung Axis Causality
* **WHY:** Because the cross-sectional samples show nearly identical taxonomic profiles across NSCLC, acute pneumonia, COPD/asthma, and respiratory failure (Figures 6–7), the data reflect general respiratory hospitalization and comorbidity/smoking status rather than disease-specific gut–lung axis mechanisms.
* **WHAT:** Temper the title, Abstract, and section headings to reflect shared dysbiosis across hospitalized PCCM patients rather than "Specific gut microbiota signature in patients with different respiratory diseases."
* **HOW:** Rename the section on line 178 to *"Shared gut microbiota alterations across different respiratory disease categories in PCCM inpatients"* and explicitly discuss pre-admission outpatient medication use, dietary changes during acute illness, and smoking as primary drivers of the observed shifts.
