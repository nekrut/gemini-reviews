### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript introduces the Mixed Effects Model of Evolution (`MEME`), a phylogenetic codon-substitution method designed to detect both episodic and pervasive diversifying positive selection at individual amino acid sites without requiring *a priori* specification of foreground branches. Building on branch-site random-effects phylogenetic models, `MEME` treats the site-specific synonymous substitution rate ($\alpha$) as a fixed effect and models the site-specific nonsynonymous rate ($\beta$) on each branch as an independent draw from a two-category discrete distribution: a constrained category ($\beta^- \le \alpha$, weight $q^-$) and an unrestricted category ($\beta^+$, weight $q^+ = 1 - q^-$). By exploiting branch independence, the authors marginalize over all $2^B$ branch rate assignments exactly in a single Felsenstein pruning pass using a branch-level mixture transition matrix, and test $H_0: \beta^+ \le \alpha$ using a likelihood ratio test (LRT) calibrated against a $0.33\chi^2_0 + 0.30\chi^2_1 + 0.37\chi^2_2$ asymptotic mixture distribution derived under the worst-case neutral boundary ($\beta^- = \beta^+ = \alpha$). Through extensive neutral and purifying null simulations across synthetic and TreeBase phylogenies (up to 325 taxa), 27 episodic/pervasive power scenarios, and 16 empirical protein-coding alignments, the authors show that `MEME` controls Type I error, matches the Fixed Effects Likelihood (`FEL`) test under pervasive selection, dramatically outperforms `FEL` when positive selection is restricted to a subset of lineages (identifying 237 sites vs. 63 by `FEL` across the 16 empirical datasets, including 56 sites where `FEL` infers overall purifying selection), and mitigates the sequence-sampling pathology whereby adding more taxa masks site-level selection. The method is openly implemented in the `HyPhy` package and `Datamonkey` web server, with full simulation and site-level tables provided in the Supporting Information.

This is a foundational methodological contribution characterized by mathematical elegance, rigorous boundary-null calibration, symmetric benchmarking, and exemplary scientific candor regarding what can and cannot be inferred from molecular alignments. In particular, the authors' explicit quantification of the "selection inference uncertainty principle"—showing via simulation that Empirical Bayes identification of *individual branches* at a single site is inherently noisy because it rests on a single realization of the substitution process ($N = 1$)—sets a high standard for statistical honesty. Only minor methodological caveats warrant discussion: specifically, the sensitivity of single-hit branch-site mixtures to multi-nucleotide mutations (MNMs) or localized alignment errors along single branches, and the effect of fixing relative branch lengths under a homogeneous global codon model in Step 1.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Exact marginalization over $2^B$ branch-specific selective configurations at a site is computationally tractable in $O(B)$ operations via mixture transition matrices and yields well-controlled Type I error under the worst-case neutral null: [Well Supported].**  
  Equations 1 and 2 establish that when branch rate categories are independent conditional on site parameters $(\alpha, \beta^-, \beta^+, q^-)$, Felsenstein's pruning recursion over the mixture transition matrix $P_b(t_b) = q^- e^{Q(\alpha,\beta^-)t_b} + (1 - q^-) e^{Q(\alpha,\beta^+)t_b}$ exactly sums over all $2^B$ branch configurations. The $0.33\chi^2_0 + 0.30\chi^2_1 + 0.37\chi^2_2$ boundary mixture distribution closely tracks parametric bootstrap quantiles under strict neutrality ($\omega = 1$, Figure S1) and maintains empirical false-positive rates at or below the nominal $5\%$ level across $N = 8$ to $325$ taxa (Tables S1–S2).

* **`MEME` achieves substantially higher statistical power than lineage-constant site models (`FEL`) under episodic selection while retaining comparable power under pervasive selection: [Well Supported].**  
  Across all 27 variable-selection simulation regimes on three empirical tree topologies (Table 1) and the constant-selection regimes (Table S3), `MEME` uniformly dominates `FEL` whenever $q^+ < 1$ (especially when $q^+ \in \{0.10, 0.25\}$ against a purifying background $\omega^- \le 0.4$, where `FEL` has $<3\%$ power and `MEME` reaches $30\%\text{--}99\%$ power) and sacrifices at most $\sim 5\%$ power at unadjusted nominal $p = 0.05$ under strictly constant $\omega$ (recovering a slight power edge when both tests are size-calibrated to an exact $5\%$ FPR).

* **Episodic diversifying selection is widespread across empirical datasets, and modeling lineage heterogeneity resolves the taxon-sampling paradox in vertebrate rhodopsin and influenza H3N2 hemagglutinin: [Well Supported].**  
  Across the 16 empirical datasets (Table 2), `MEME` recovers $51/63$ `FEL` sites (with the remaining 12 borderline at $p = 0.06\text{--}0.08$) plus 186 additional sites (including 56 classified as negatively selected by `FEL`, such as rhodopsin site 210, where $\Delta\text{LRT} = 21.6, p = 10^{-5}$). In vertebrate rhodopsin, `MEME` detects three experimentally verified spectral-tuning ($\lambda_{\max}$) sites missed by `FEL` and detects monotonically increasing numbers of selected sites as alignment breadth expands from squirrelfish (1 site) to all fish (4 sites) to all vertebrates (19 sites; Table S20).

* **Empirical Bayes (EB) identification of specific branches under selection at an individual site has low power and substantial false-discovery risk ("selection inference uncertainty principle"): [Well Supported].**  
  Rather than overselling branch-site localization, the authors explicitly simulate five selected branches on the rhodopsin phylogeny (Figures S2–S3, lines 150–152) and show that even at $\text{EBF} \ge 20$, at least one background branch is falsely flagged at $24\%$ of selected sites under strong purifying background ($\omega^- = 0.1$) and $66\%$ under neutral background ($\omega^- = 1.0$), justifying their recommendation to use branch-level EBFs strictly for exploratory visualization.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Quantify Sensitivity to Multi-Nucleotide Substitutions (MNMs) Along Individual Branches
* **WHY:** Like standard Muse–Gaut/Goldman–Yang codon models, `MEME`'s instantaneous rate matrix (line 36) permits only single-nucleotide substitutions ($Q_{ij} = 0$ when codons $i$ and $j$ differ at $>1$ position). When a tandem double- or triple-nucleotide mutation occurs in a single mutational event along one short branch (yielding 2–3 instantaneous nucleotide changes that are nonsynonymous), a single-hit codon model must explain the change as 2–3 rapid sequential nonsynonymous substitutions on a branch of length $t_b \ll 1$, driving $\hat{\beta}^+ \to \infty$ with small $\hat{q}^+$ and potentially triggering a significant `MEME` LRT from a single non-adaptive mutational event.
* **WHAT:** Evaluate the false-positive rate of `MEME` when a small realistic fraction ($1\%\text{--}2\%$) of substitutions occur as instantaneous multi-nucleotide mutations under neutral/purifying evolution, and provide diagnostic guidance for single-branch multi-hit codons.
* **HOW:** Simulate neutral/purifying alignments on the vertebrate rhodopsin and HIV-1 *rt* trees incorporating a $1.5\%$ double-nucleotide mutation rate (e.g., following Whelan & Goldman 2004 / Lucaci et al.), measure the resulting `MEME` FPR at $p \le 0.05$, and flag empirical sites where the entire signal of $\beta^+ > \alpha$ is driven by a single branch carrying a 2- or 3-nucleotide codon change.

#### 2. Assess the Impact of Empirical Bayes Shrinkage or Uncertainty in Site-Specific Synonymous Rates ($\alpha_s$)
* **WHY:** `MEME` estimates a separate unconstrained synonymous rate $\alpha_s$ at every site (fitting 4 free parameters per site: $\alpha, \beta^-, q^-, \beta^+$). In small or shallow alignments (e.g., $N < 20$ or mean divergence $\le 0.05$, such as *Salmonella recA* or H3N2 HA in Table 2), a site that happens by chance to have zero observed synonymous substitutions ($\hat{\alpha}_s \to 0$) and 1–2 nonsynonymous substitutions on a single branch could yield an inflated $\hat{\beta}^+/\hat{\alpha}_s$ ratio if $\hat{\alpha}_s$ is noisy.
* **WHAT:** Test whether very low or zero site-specific synonymous counts destabilize the boundary LRT in shallow alignments, and compare unconstrained site-wise $\hat{\alpha}_s$ against a random-effects or shrunk synonymous rate prior.
* **HOW:** Stratify the false-positive rates in the TreeBase neutral simulations (Table S1) by the number of inferred synonymous substitutions at each site ($S = 0, 1, 2, \ge 3$) to verify that sites with $\hat{\alpha}_s \approx 0$ do not exhibit elevated Type I error, or report a comparison with `MEME` holding $\alpha_s = 1$ fixed.

#### 3.Clarify the Effect of Step-1 Global Codon Model Branch Lengths on Extremely Diversifying Sites
* **WHY:** In Step 1 (line 67), relative branch lengths $t_b$ are estimated under a single alignment-wide $\omega$ and held fixed (up to site-level scaling) during Step 2 and Step 3.
* **WHAT:** Briefly note in the Methods whether fixing the *relative* branch-length proportions $t_b / \sum_k t_k$ from the global model can bias inference when a specific clade undergoes a massive burst of adaptive evolution across many sites simultaneously.
* **HOW:** In the simulated episodic datasets with high $q^+ = 0.5$ and $\omega^+ = 36$ (Table 1), compare `MEME` power and FPR using the Step-1 estimated branch lengths versus the true simulated branch lengths.

#### 4. Expand Guidance on Multiple-Testing Correction ($q$-Values vs. Site-Wise $p$-Values) in the Main Text
* **WHY:** While the Discussion (line 233) carefully explains why Benjamini–Hochberg $q$-values are much larger than nominal $p$-values (and provides $q$-values in Tables S4–S19), Table 2 and the Abstract emphasize the 237 sites detected at nominal site-wise $p \le 0.05$ across 4,745 total codons tested across the 16 alignments.
* **WHAT:** Add a column or summary count to Table 2 showing how many sites in each of the 16 datasets remain significant at an FDR threshold of $q \le 0.10$ or $q \le 0.20$ for both `MEME` and `FEL`.
* **HOW:** Aggregate the $q$-value counts already computed in Tables S4–S19 and display them alongside the $p \le 0.05$ counts in Table 2 so readers can directly compare `MEME` and `FEL` under both site-wise ($p \le 0.05$) and alignment-wide FDR ($q \le 0.10$) criteria.
