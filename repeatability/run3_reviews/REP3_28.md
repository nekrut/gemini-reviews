### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This software platform paper summarizes the architectural, algorithmic, and modeling advances introduced in BEAST 2 (up to version 2.5) over the four years following its initial release. The authors review extensions from binary rooted time trees to generalized phylogenetic structures—including sampled-ancestor trees, multi-type structured trees, bacterial clonal-frame ancestral recombination graphs (`BACTER`), and reticulate species networks (`SpeciesNetwork`)—and catalog 34 packages across substitution/site models (`bModelTest`, `PoMo`), molecular clocks (`FLC`), phylodynamic birth-death and structured coalescent models (`BDSKY`, `BDMM`, `EpiInf`, `PhyDyn`, `BASTA`, `MASCOT`, `SCOTTI`, `BadTrIP`), multispecies coalescent (MSC) and isolation-with-migration frameworks (`StarBEAST 2`, `STACEY`, `SNAPP`, `DENIM`, `AIM`), marginal likelihood estimation (`MODEL SELECTION`, `NS`), and posterior predictive simulation (`MASTER`, `TreeModelAdequacy`). To illustrate these capabilities, the manuscript presents four empirical case studies: (1) `bModelTest` reversible-jump nucleotide model averaging on 906 bp of mitochondrial DNA across 36 mammalian species (Figure 2), (2) a `bdsky` + sampled-ancestor (`SA`) analysis of 811 West African Ebola virus coding genomes sampled through October 2015 (Figure 3), (3) an `AIM` isolation-with-migration analysis of 100 nuclear orthologous genes across five Lake Tanganyika Princess cichlid species (`Neolamprologus savoryi` complex) and outgroup `Metriaclima zebra` (Figure 5), and (4) a `TreeModelAdequacy` posterior predictive comparison of stochastic birth-death SIR versus deterministic coalescent SIR models on 100 North American 2009 H1N1 influenza genomes (Figure 6). Source code is available under the LGPL license, and all XML specifications, alignments, log files, and post-processing scripts for the four empirical demonstrations are deposited in Zenodo and GitHub.

This is a well-engineered, modular, and openly documented software ecosystem paper that transparently discusses the mathematical assumptions and computational trade-offs among competing evolutionary models (such as exact versus approximate structured coalescent formulations and concatenation versus MSC). The primary limitations are confined to the illustrative empirical vignettes and third-party package governance: the Ebola `bdsky` analysis applies an unstructured panmictic skyline model across a geographically structured three-country epidemic where late-stage spatial isolation biases sampling proportion estimates; the cichlid `AIM` analysis fixes the species tree root height to a point estimate (9.2 Mya) without propagating calibration uncertainty; the H1N1 model adequacy vignette relies on a single summary statistic (root height) without reporting formal posterior predictive $p$-values or MCMC convergence diagnostics (ESS); and third-party package verification remains informal. All of these issues can be addressed through fuller reporting of existing log outputs, brief sensitivity analyses, and minor corrections to table and figure legends.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **BEAST 2.5 provides a modular, reproducible Bayesian platform unifying generalized time-calibrated phylogenetic structures, model averaging, and simulation-based adequacy testing: [Well Supported].**  
  The core mathematical hierarchy—factoring the joint posterior across phylogenetic likelihoods $P(D \mid T, \theta)$, tree/network generative priors $P(T \mid \theta)$ (Equations 1–3), and hyperpriors $P(\theta)$—is coherently presented and backed by 34 peer-reviewed or preprinted packages (Table 1). Reproducibility is directly supported by side-by-side versioned package installation in the Package Manager and complete Zenodo/GitHub archival of the XML and log files for Figures 2, 3, 5, and 6.

* **Reversible-jump model averaging in `bModelTest` integrates over nucleotide substitution model uncertainty without committing to a single ad hoc model: [Well Supported].**  
  In the 36-mammal mitochondrial dataset (Figure 2; Zenodo `10.5281/zenodo.1475369`), `bModelTest` demonstrates that posterior mass is spread across an eight-model 95% credible set rather than concentrated on a single canonical model, properly propagating site-model uncertainty into the marginal posterior tree distribution (Figure 2b).

* **Birth-death skyline (`bdsky`) with sampled ancestors (`SA`) recovers the temporal trajectory of $R_e$ and the December 2013 index-case origin of the 2013–2016 West African Ebola epidemic from 811 genomes: [Moderately Supported].**  
  The inferred origin time ($t_0$) and the rise and fall of $R_e$ across 20 intervals (Figure 3b) align with epidemiological surveillance records and the September–October 2014 turning point. However, as the authors acknowledge in the text, the monthly sampling proportion $p = \psi / \delta$ is overestimated during peak transmission and underestimated in the final two months (Figure 3c) because `bdsky` assumes a single unstructured population. Applying a panmictic model across Guinea, Liberia, and Sierra Leone violates homogeneous mixing and sampling assumptions, particularly during late-stage localized transmission chains, even though structured packages (`bdmm`, `MASCOT`) are highlighted in the same manuscript.

* **The `AIM` isolation-with-migration model jointly infers species tree topologies and directional hybridization (Bayes factor > 10) across 100 nuclear loci in Lake Tanganyika Princess cichlids: [Moderately Supported].**  
  The analytical integration of migration histories allows MCMC exploration of ranked species tree topologies and directional gene flow in the `Neolamprologus savoryi` complex (Figure 5). However, two modeling choices limit the robustness of the biological inference: (1) the species tree height was fixed to a point value of 9.2 Mya rather than assigned a calibration prior, suppressing age uncertainty across all internal nodes and time-scaled migration rates; and (2) backwards-in-time gene flow rates were assumed to be inversely proportional to species co-existence time with an exponential prior mean of 0.05 on the rate scaler, without reporting prior sensitivity or effective sample sizes (ESS) across the multi-modal ranked topologies (Figure 5a–d).

* **Posterior predictive simulations in `TreeModelAdequacy` show that the stochastic birth-death SIR model fits the 2009 North American H1N1 root height better than the deterministic coalescent SIR model: [Moderately Supported].**  
  Figure 6 illustrates how `MASTER` and `TreeModelAdequacy` generate posterior predictive root-height distributions, showing that the empirical root height falls well inside the stochastic birth-death SIR predictive distribution but in the tail of the deterministic coalescent SIR distribution. However, because the two models differ simultaneously in two components—stochastic versus deterministic population dynamics *and* birth-death (conditioning on sampling intensity) versus coalescent (conditioning on tip sampling times)—the discrepancy in root height cannot be uniquely attributed to early-pandemic demographic stochasticity without also testing the stochastic coalescent SIR model (which is available in the same `phylodynamics` package; Table 1, ref. [27]) and reporting formal two-tailed posterior predictive $p$-values.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Disentangle Stochasticity from Sampling Conditioning in the H1N1 Model Adequacy Vignette (Figure 6)
* **WHY:** Figure 6 compares a *stochastic birth-death* SIR model [28] against a *deterministic coalescent* SIR model [27] and attributes the improved root-height adequacy to early-stage stochasticity. This comparison confounds demographic stochasticity with the difference between birth-death and coalescent tree priors (where birth-death models treat sampling times as informative data governed by $\psi$, whereas coalescent models condition on sampling times).
* **WHAT:** Report quantitative posterior predictive $p$-values for Figure 6, and clarify—or directly test—the role of the stochastic coalescent SIR model.
* **HOW:** Using the existing `TreeModelAdequacy` script (`10.5281/zenodo.1473852`), compute and report in the text and Figure 6 caption the exact posterior predictive $p$-values (and 95% predictive intervals) for the empirical root height under both models, plus at least one complementary tree shape or branching-time summary statistic (e.g., Colless imbalance or lineage-through-time $\gamma$/Pybus-Harvey statistic). If feasible, include the stochastic closed SIR coalescent model (already implemented in `phylodynamics` [27]; Table 1) to isolate demographic stochasticity from the birth-death vs. coalescent sampling assumption.

#### 2. Report MCMC Convergence Diagnostics (ESS, Chain Lengths, Runtimes) Across All Four Empirical Vignettes
* **WHY:** While the manuscript emphasizes computational performance and MCMC mixing improvements across BEAST 2.5 packages, the main text does not report MCMC chain lengths, burn-in percentages, minimum Effective Sample Sizes (ESS), or wall-clock runtimes for the four showcase analyses in Figures 2, 3, 5, and 6.
* **WHAT:** Add a compact summary paragraph or table listing the dataset dimensions, chain length, minimum ESS, and runtime for the `bModelTest`, `bdsky` (Ebola), `AIM` (cichlids), and `TreeModelAdequacy` (H1N1) analyses.
* **HOW:** Extract these statistics directly from the archived log files on Zenodo/GitHub using `LogAnalyser` (already part of BEAST 2) and report them in the respective figure captions or a small summary table. For the `AIM` analysis (Figure 5), also report the posterior probabilities of the four top ranked species trees (Figure 5a–d) so readers can gauge topological uncertainty.

#### 3. Discuss the Impact of Unmodeled Geographic Structure in the Ebola `bdsky` Analysis and Fixed Root Age in the Cichlid `AIM` Analysis
* **WHY:** In Figure 3c, the authors note that late-stage isolation of transmission chains likely biases the unstructured `bdsky` sampling proportion estimates downward, and in Figure 5, fixing the cichlid root height to 9.2 Mya ignores calibration variance when inferring time-dependent migration rates.
* **WHAT:** Explicitly connect the Ebola model misspecification to the structured phylodynamic packages (`bdmm`, `MASCOT`) presented in the next subsection, and note the effect of fixing the root height in `AIM`.
* **HOW:** In the "Tree models for unstructured populations" section, add 1–2 sentences noting how structured birth-death (`bdmm`) or approximate structured coalescent (`MASCOT`) models can resolve the spatial-isolation bias observed in the late-2015 Ebola sampling proportion estimates. In the "Isolation with migration" section, state how placing a normal/lognormal calibration prior around 9.2 Mya (rather than a point constraint) would affect the credible intervals of speciation times while preserving the relative ranking of topologies in Figure 5.

#### 4. Formalize Minimum Validation Standards for Third-Party BEAST 2 Packages
* **WHY:** In "Validation, testing and quality management," the authors candidly state that third-party package validation has been informal and recommend that "each package be evaluated independently by users, based on the published documentation." Placing the burden of MCMC operator correctness verification on end users risks silent inference errors from improperly balanced Hastings ratios or broken likelihood caching.
* **WHAT:** Outline concrete, automated quality gates that the CBAN package repository can enforce or display for contributed packages.
* **HOW:** Expand the "Validation, testing and quality management" section to specify a standard checklist for packages listed on CBAN: (1) automated continuous integration (CI) unit tests for likelihood calculations, (2) prior-sampling consistency checks (verifying that running MCMC without data recovers the analytical prior), and (3) well-calibrated simulation coverage tests (confirming ~95% coverage of 95% HPD intervals on data simulated under the prior).
