### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This comparative evolutionary genomics study tests whether more protein-coding genes have undergone positive Darwinian selection in the human lineage than in the chimpanzee lineage since their divergence 6–7 million years ago. Analyzing 13,888 orthologous human–chimpanzee–macaque gene trios after filtering codons with chimpanzee Phred base quality $<20$ (Q20 dataset, covering $17,995,887\text{ bp}$), the authors apply PAML's improved branch-site likelihood ratio test (Test 2) separately to the human and chimpanzee branches. At a nominal $P < 0.05$, they identify 233 positively selected genes (PSGs) in chimpanzees versus 154 in humans ($1.51$-fold excess in chimpanzees, $P < 10^{-4}$), and after 5% false discovery rate (FDR) correction, 59 chimpanzee PSGs versus 2 human PSGs ($29.5$-fold excess). Conversely, the genome-wide mean $d_N/d_S$ ($\omega$) across all genes ($0.259$ vs. $0.245$) and across the 13,508 non-PSGs ($0.252$ vs. $0.238$) is significantly higher in humans than in chimpanzees ($P < 10^{-4}$). The authors interpret both patterns through population genetics theory: a smaller long-term effective population size ($N_e$) in the human lineage reduced the efficacy of both positive selection (fewer adaptive fixations) and purifying selection (more slightly deleterious nonsynonymous fixations). Gene lists and summary statistics are provided in the Supporting Information, although the processed sequence alignments are not deposited in a public repository.

The study is well designed, replacing the distant rodent outgroup used in earlier studies with the rhesus macaque, calibrating chimpanzee Phred quality thresholds against the human/chimpanzee synonymous substitution ratio ($r = 0.985 \pm 0.008$ at Q20), validating candidate chimpanzee PSGs against the 6$\times$ chimpanzee assembly, and verifying branch-site test behavior via lineage-specific simulations. Two methodological vulnerabilities warrant attention. First, while only 2 of the 154 nominal human PSGs survive 5% FDR correction (implying an estimated false discovery proportion $>95\%$ among the 154 human genes at $P < 0.05$), all downstream analyses of functional divergence (Figure 1), tissue expression (Figure 2), and Mendelian disease enrichment (Table 2, Fisher's exact $P = 0.049$) are conducted on the unadjusted $P < 0.05$ gene lists. Second, the jump in the chimpanzee-to-human PSG ratio from $1.51\times$ at $P < 0.05$ to $29.5\times$ ($59$ vs. $2$) at 5% FDR is too extreme to be explained by a $2\text{--}3\times$ difference in $N_e$ alone and points to residual gene-model/exon-boundary annotation errors or local frame-shifted segments in the Ensembl v36 draft chimpanzee gene predictions that pass base-level Q20 filtering. Both issues can be evaluated directly on the existing alignments.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Chimpanzees have undergone positive selection in significantly more protein-coding genes than humans since their divergence, whereas humans exhibit a higher genome-wide $d_N/d_S$ in non-PSGs due to relaxed purifying selection in a smaller effective population size ($N_e$): [Well Supported].**  
  The joint observation of fewer adaptive sweeps and higher background $\omega$ ($0.252$ vs. $0.238$ in non-PSGs) in humans than in chimpanzees—while synonymous substitution counts are slightly lower in the Q20 chimpanzee data ($29,644$ vs. $30,083$, Table 1)—provides internally consistent support for the nearly neutral and selection-efficacy predictions of population genetics theory, further corroborated by the higher background macaque $\omega$ required for human PSGs to reach detection threshold ($0.294$ vs. $0.278$, line 56).

* **At a 5% false discovery rate, chimpanzees have 29.5-fold more PSGs than humans (59 vs. 2 genes; Table 1): [Fragile].**  
  While Q20 filtering removes single-base sequencing errors in the 4$\times$ chimpanzee assembly, the alignments were generated by aligning Ensembl v36 predicted protein sequences with `CLUSTALW` and back-translating to DNA (line 117). In draft genomes (even at 4$\times$–6$\times$ coverage), ab initio gene annotations frequently substitute an adjacent intronic segment for a missing exon or introduce compensatory indels that create short stretches of consecutive high-quality (Q$\ge$20) apparent nonsynonymous substitutions. Because the branch-site likelihood test is highly sensitive to clustered nonsynonymous changes on the foreground branch, even 30–40 such misannotated exon segments in chimpanzee gene models would heavily inflate the extreme tail ($P < 10^{-4}$, 5% FDR) while leaving the genome-wide synonymous ratio ($r = 0.985$) unaffected.

* **Human PSGs are significantly enriched for OMIM Mendelian disease genes compared to non-PSGs ($9.7\%$ vs. $6.1\%$, $P = 0.049$) and compared to chimpanzee PSGs ($4.7\%$, $P = 0.044$; Table 2): [Fragile].**  
  This conclusion rests on 15 disease genes out of the 154 nominal ($P < 0.05$) human PSGs,yielding a borderline unadjusted Fisher's exact $P = 0.049$. Because only 2 of those 154 human genes survive 5% FDR correction (Table 1), the vast majority of the 154 genes are expected under the null distribution to be false positives. Furthermore, the Fisher's exact test in Table 2 does not control for coding sequence length or background substitution rate: longer genes have both greater statistical power to reach $P < 0.05$ in the branch-site test and a higher probability of harboring known Mendelian disease mutations in OMIM.

* **PSGs are not enriched on rearranged chromosomes relative to colinear chromosomes ($P = 0.10$ in human, $P = 0.055$ in chimp) or in brain-expressed genes ($P = 0.19$): [Moderately Supported].**  
  The absence of enrichment on inverted/fused chromosomes (Figure 3) and across 20 human tissue expression groups (Figure 2) argues against widespread coding-sequence acceleration in brain-expressed or rearranged-chromosome genes, though the high false-discovery fraction in the nominal $P < 0.05$ PSG sets dilutes statistical power to detect subtle real enrichments.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Screen the 59 FDR-Significant Chimpanzee PSGs for Exon-Boundary and Local Alignment Artifacts
* **WHY:** Base-quality filtering (Q20) eliminates sequencing base-call errors but does not remove Ensembl v36 ab initio splice-junction misannotations or compensatory frameshifts in the draft chimpanzee genome, which produce clustered nonsynonymous substitutions that drive extreme branch-site LRT statistics ($29.5$-fold chimpanzee excess at 5% FDR).
* **WHAT:** Audit the 59 FDR-significant chimpanzee PSGs (and 233 nominal PSGs) for clustered lineage-specific substitutions within short sliding windows and verify syntenic splicing against the human reference transcript.
* **HOW:** Flag any alignment window of 10 codons containing $\ge 3$ chimpanzee-specific or human-specific nonsynonymous substitutions, map the chimpanzee genomic DNA directly to the human coding sequence using `Exonerate` or `BLAT` (bypassing Ensembl v36 chimpanzee protein predictions), and re-run PAML branch-site Test 2 after masking non-homologous or frame-shifted exon segments.

#### 2. Control for Gene Length and Expression Level in the Mendelian Disease Enrichment Analysis
* **WHY:** Longer genes accumulate more substitutions, giving the branch-site test higher power to achieve $P < 0.05$, and longer genes are also substantially overrepresented in OMIM, confounding the $2 \times 2$ Fisher's exact test in Table 2 ($P = 0.049$).
* **WHAT:** Re-test the association between PSG status and OMIM Mendelian disease status while controlling for alignment length (codons), background substitution rate ($\omega_{\text{macaque}}$), and tissue expression breadth.
* **HOW:** Fit a logistic regression predicting OMIM disease gene status (`is_OMIM ~ is_human_PSG + log10(alignment_length) + omega_macaque + dS`) across the 13,888 genes, and also perform a length-matched permutation test (sampling 10,000 sets of 154 genes matched to the exact codon-length deciles of the 154 human PSGs). Report the length-adjusted odds ratio and $P$-value alongside Table 2.

#### 3. Estimate the True Proportion of Positively Selected Genes ($\pi_1$) Using the Full $P$-Value Distribution
* **WHY:** Thresholding at $P < 0.05$ mixes true positives with hundreds of false positives (as shown by only 2 human genes passing 5% FDR), whereas thresholding at 5% FDR has low sensitivity when per-gene branch lengths are short ($d_S \approx 0.006$ per lineage).
* **WHAT:** Estimate the overall proportion of true non-null genes ($\hat{\pi}_1 = 1 - \hat{\pi}_0$) on the human and chimpanzee branches directly from the empirical distributions of the 13,888 branch-site likelihood ratio test statistics.
* **HOW:** Using the simulated null distribution of the branch-site LRT statistic from SI Tables 3–4 (or Storey's $q$-value / mixture-model estimation accounting for the $50{:}50$ point-mass-at-zero and $\chi^2_1$ null mixture), estimate $\hat{\pi}_1$ and its bootstrap $95\%$ confidence interval for both the human and chimpanzee lineages across all 13,888 genes and within each PANTHER/tissue/chromosomal category.

#### 4. Temper Claims Based on the Unadjusted $P < 0.05$ Human PSG Set
* **WHY:** Because only 2 of the 154 human genes with nominal $P < 0.05$ survive 5% FDR correction, treating all 154 genes as confirmed "human positively selected genes" in functional and disease comparisons overstates the certainty of individual gene calls.
* **WHAT:** Explicitly state the expected false discovery proportion ($\sim 85\text{--}95\%$) of the nominal $P < 0.05$ lists when introducing the PANTHER, tissue-expression, and OMIM analyses, and qualify the borderline $P = 0.049$ OMIM disease association accordingly.
* **HOW:** Add a caveat in the sections "Similarities and Differences Between Human and Chimp PSGs" (lines 61 and 77) and the Abstract (line 8) noting that the nominal $P < 0.05$ candidate lists contain a substantial fraction of false positives under multiple testing and that the OMIM disease enrichment ($P = 0.049$) is suggestive prior to gene-length adjustment.
