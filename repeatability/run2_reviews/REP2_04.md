### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript investigates the variation of raw codon usage frequencies across 1,484 reference Proteobacterial genomes (both genome-wide and in a 13-gene subset of highly expressed translational/chaperone genes) and examines its relationship with genomic GC content, genome size, tRNA gene copy number, and the presence/absence of tRNA anticodon modification enzymes (`tsaB`, `tsaC/C2`, `tsaD`, `tsaE` for $\text{t}^6\text{A}$; `gluQ` and `tgt` for $\text{GluQ}$; `tilS` for $\text{k}^2\text{C}$; `tusE` and `mnmA` for $\text{s}^2\text{U}$). The authors report that certain codons (such as `AAC`, `CGA`, `AGG`, and `GGA`) show restricted absolute frequency variation ("low variability codons") across Proteobacteria regardless of genomic GC%, and that the presence of genes encoding $\text{t}^6\text{A}$ and $\text{GluQ}$ modification enzymes correlates with shifts in synonymous codon frequencies and higher genomic GC%. All Perl scripts and summary tables are publicly archived at the Universidad de Chile repository (`https://doi.org/10.34691/UCHILE/AYDRZL`).

While the curated dataset of 1,484 complete Proteobacterial genomes and RPS-BLAST domain profiles is a useful resource, the manuscript's statistical and evolutionary inferences suffer from three major methodological flaws: (1) complete omission of phylogenetic comparative controls when testing associations across 1,484 evolutionary related species (treating species as independent replicates in Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney, Kruskal–Wallis, and OLS regression tests); (2) reliance on unnormalized raw codon usage frequencies rather than relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) or amino-acid-normalized codon bias, which mathematically conflates mean codon rarity and proteome amino acid composition with evolutionary variance constraint; and (3) circular variable selection when screening 25 extreme high-GC vs. 25 extreme low-GC genomes for enriched genes (`tsaB`, `gluQ`) and then testing whether those same genes predict GC% in the broader dataset. All three issues can be resolved computationally using the authors' existing dataset and phylogenetic tree.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **A specific subset of codons (`AAC`, `CGA`, `AGG`, `GGA`, `ATG`, `TGG`, `TGT`, `TGC`) exhibits restricted evolutionary variability across Proteobacteria independent of genomic GC%: [Fragile].**  
  The authors deliberately compute raw codon frequencies (percentage of all codons in the genome) without normalizing by the frequency of the encoded amino acid, arguing that Codon Usage Bias (CUB/RSCU) "may distort data interpretation" (Section 2.1). However, raw codon frequency is bounded below by zero, so its standard deviation ($\text{SD}$, Figure 2) and interdecile range ($\Delta_{90-10}$, Supplementary Figure 4) are mathematically coupled to the **mean frequency** of the codon and the proteome-wide abundance of its amino acid. A rare codon like `CGA` or `AGG` (mean frequency $\sim 0.1$–$0.2\%$) cannot physically exhibit an absolute SD of $2.0\%$ the way an abundant codon like `GGC` or `AAT` (mean frequency $3$–$5\%$) can, even if its relative synonymous preference fluctuates by 10-fold across GC regimes. Likewise, single-codon amino acids (`ATG` Met, `TGG` Trp) and rare reactive amino acids (`TGT`/`TGC` Cys) reflect amino acid proteome conservation, not codon decoding constraint.

* **The presence of tRNA anticodon modification enzymes (`tsaB`, `gluQ`, `tusE`) significantly alters codon usage frequencies across Proteobacteria ($p < 0.00005$, Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  In Figure 3 and Supplementary Figures 7–14, the authors divide the 1,484 Proteobacterial genomes into two groups based on the presence or absence of an RPS-BLAST motif and apply standard nonparametric two-sample Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney tests (using $p < 0.00005$). Bacterial genomes share hierarchical phylogenetic ancestry (`CVTree` phylogeny, Section 4.3); loss of non-essential tRNA modification genes (`tsaB`, `tsaE`, `gluQ`, `tusE`) is concentrated in a small number of clades undergoing reductive genome evolution (e.g., obligate intracellular endosymbionts and host-restricted Gammaproteobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria, and Betaproteobacteria with genomes $< 2$ Mbp and GC $< 40\%$; Section 2.4). Treating 1,484 tips of a phylogenetic tree as independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) observations violates phylogenetic independence and inflates test statistics by orders of magnitude.

* **Genes involved in $\text{t}^6\text{A}$ (`tsaB`) and $\text{GluQ}$ (`gluQ`) formation impact the GC content of Proteobacterial genomes (Figure 4, Kruskal–Wallis $p < 0.00005$): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  This analysis is both circular and confounded by genome reduction and phylogeny. In Section 2.2, `tsaB` and `gluQ` were initially discovered *because* the authors selected 25 genomes with GC $> 67\%$ and 25 genomes with GC $< 32\%$ and ran `Roary`/`Scoary` to find genes associated with extreme GC%. Re-testing `tsaB` and `gluQ` against GC% in Figure 4 (in a dataset that includes those extreme GC clades) is circular. Moreover, `Roary` with a 40% BlastP identity cutoff across divergent Proteobacterial classes systematically fails to cluster rapidly evolving AT-rich orthologs from endosymbionts, and small AT-rich genomes ($< 2$ Mbp) lose accessory genes (`tsaB`, `gluQ`) as a consequence of Muller's ratchet and drift rather than `tsaB`/`gluQ` causally driving genomic GC%.

* **Low variability of codon usage is independent of cognate tRNA gene copy number: [Moderately Supported].**  
  The observation in Section 2.6 (`tRNAscan-SE 2.0` analysis, Supplementary Figure 24) that `AAC` and `AAT` are both decoded by a single $\text{tRNA}^{\text{Asn}}_{\text{GTT}}$ (since $\text{tRNA}^{\text{Asn}}_{\text{ATT}}$ is absent in most Proteobacteria) yet display very different sensitivity to genomic GC% is biologically sound and aligns with known wobble vs. Watson–Crick decoding asymmetry ($G_{34}:U_3$ vs. $G_{34}:C_3$ pairing combined with queuosine $Q_{34}$ modification).

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Control for Phylogenetic Non-Independence Using Phylogenetic Comparative Methods
* **WHY:** Treating 1,484 related bacterial species as independent points in Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney and Kruskal–Wallis tests conflates shared clade history (e.g., AT-rich endosymbiont lineages losing `tsaB`/`gluQ`) with independent evolutionary convergence.
* **WHAT:** Replace i.i.d. Wilcoxon and Kruskal–Wallis tests in Figures 3 and 4 with phylogenetic generalized linear models (`phylolm` / PGLS) and phylogenetic ANOVA using the tree already inferred in Section 4.3.
* **HOW:** Using the 1,484-taxon tree in R (`ape` and `phylolm` packages), fit `phylolm(Codon_Freq ~ Enzyme_Presence + GC_content + log(Genome_Size), data = df, phy = tree, model = "lambda")` to test whether `tsaB`, `gluQ`, and `tusE` retain a statistically significant association with codon usage after accounting for Pagel's $\lambda$, genomic GC%, and genome size.

#### 2. Decouple Mean Frequency Scaling and Amino Acid Abundance from Synonymous Codon Variability
* **WHY:** Raw codon usage standard deviation ($\text{SD}$) scales strongly with mean codon frequency; rare codons (`CGA`, `AGG`) have small SD simply because their mean frequency is near zero.
* **WHAT:** Compute and compare three complementary dispersion metrics across the 61 sense codons: (1) raw frequency SD (current Figure 2), (2) the coefficient of variation ($\text{CV} = \text{SD} / \text{Mean}$) of raw codon frequency, and (3) the SD of Relative Synonymous Codon Usage ($\text{RSCU}_{ij} = n_i x_{ij} / \sum_{k=1}^{n_i} x_{ik}$).
* **HOW:** Using the existing codon tables (Supplementary Tables 7–8), add two panels to Figure 2 showing $\text{CV}$ and $\text{SD}(\text{RSCU})$, and verify which codons (`AAC`, `CGA`, `AGG`, `GGA`) remain genuinely low-variance after controlling for amino acid usage and mean frequency.

#### 3. Eliminate Circularity in the GC% Enrichment Screen and Control for Genome Size
* **WHY:** Selecting `tsaB` and `gluQ` via a high-GC vs. low-GC screen (Section 2.2) and then testing whether they predict GC% (Section 2.4, Figure 4) is post-hoc circular, and `Roary` at 40% BlastP identity is unsuitable for phylum-wide orthology across classes separated by $>2$ billion years.
* **WHAT:** Present the relationship between `tsaB`/`gluQ`, GC%, and genome size strictly as a descriptive co-variation driven by genome reduction ($< 2$ Mbp), and repeat the Figure 3 codon-usage comparisons stratified within narrow GC% and genome-size bins.
* **HOW:** Filter the 1,484 genomes to free-living Proteobacteria with genome size $\ge 2.5$ Mbp and matched GC% intervals (e.g., $40$–$55\%$ GC), and test within each GC bin whether the presence of `gluQ` or `tsaB` still shifts `GAC`/`GAT` or `AAT`/`AAC` ratios.
