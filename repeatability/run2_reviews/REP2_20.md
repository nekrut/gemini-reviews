### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This study presents a purpose-built experimental and statistical benchmark of 11 differential gene expression (DGE) tools (`baySeq`, `cuffdiff`, `DEGSeq`, `DESeq`, `DESeq2`, `EBSeq`, `edgeR` exact and GLM, `limma`, `NOISeq`, `PoissonSeq`, and `SAMSeq`) alongside five classical parametric and nonparametric tests ($t$-test, log-ratio $t$-test, Mann–Whitney, permutation, and Studentized bootstrap) using 48 biological replicates per condition of *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* wild-type (BY4741) versus a $\Delta snf2$ chromatin-remodeling mutant. Following a randomized balanced block library preparation across four batches and seven HiSeq 2000 lanes (totaling $\sim 889$ million aligned reads across 42 WT and 44 $\Delta snf2$ clean biological replicates across 7,126 annotated genes), the authors evaluate sensitivity (TPR) and specificity (1$-$FPR) across subsampled replicate sizes ($n_r = 2, \dots, 40$, $i = 100$ iterations) and fold-change thresholds ($|\log_2 \text{FC}| = T \in \{0, 0.3, 1, 2\}$), cross-tool convergence at full sample size (`pvclust`), and empirical false-positive rates on mock null comparisons constructed strictly within the 42 WT replicates. They show that at $n_r = 3$, the nine tools that control FDR $\lesssim 5\%$ recover only 20%–40% of all differentially expressed genes ($T = 0$)—though $>85\%$ of genes with $|\log_2 \text{FC}| \ge 2$—and recommend at least 6 biological replicates per condition in general (with `edgeR` or `DESeq2`), rising to $\ge 12$ replicates when detecting small-fold-change genes is required. Raw sequencing reads are deposited in ENA (`PRJEB5348`) and all pipeline code is openly available on GitHub (`bartongroup/profDGE48`).

This is an exemplary experimental design and benchmarking study. By generating 48 genuine biological replicates per group under a randomized block design across library batches and sequencing lanes, the authors provide both a clean empirical within-condition null test (sampling $n_r$ vs. $n_r$ WT replicates without replacement, which avoids synthetic distribution assumptions) and a high-power reference regime where parametric negative-binomial methods (`DESeq2`, `edgeR`, `limma`) can be directly verified against distribution-free permutation and Mann–Whitney tests. Two minor methodological caveats merit discussion: (1) deleting the catalytic subunit `SNF2` of the SWI/SNF global transcriptional activator alters expression of 60%–75% of the yeast genome at $n_r \ge 42$, which violates the core normalization assumption (used by default TMM/`DESeq` size factors) that the majority of genes are non-differentially expressed; and (2) in Figures 1 and 2, measuring TPR and FPR against each tool's own full-dataset call set ("tool-specific gold standard") rewards internal consistency rather than ground-truth accuracy for tools that over-call DGE at all sample sizes (such as `DEGSeq`), although the authors appropriately supplement this with the tool-independent clustering (Fig. 3) and within-WT null test (Fig. 4).

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **With $n_r = 3$ biological replicates, FDR-controlled DGE tools recover only 20%–40% of all differentially expressed genes ($T = 0$), whereas $>85\%$ of genes with $\ge 4$-fold change ($T = 2$) are detected: [Well Supported].**  
  Across 100 random subsamplings without replacement at $n_r = 3$ (Fig. 1B–C, Fig. 2A,E), all nine FDR-controlling tools exhibit low overall power (20%–40%) when no fold-change cutoff is applied, but exceed 85% sensitivity for $|\log_2 \text{FC}| \ge 2$. Furthermore, the authors explicitly highlight that because yeast cultures grown under controlled laboratory conditions have very high inter-replicate concordance ($R > 0.97$, compared to $R = 0.64\text{--}0.95$ in outbred mouse and human cohorts), these power curves represent an optimistic upper bound for complex eukaryotic experiments.

* **Mock null comparisons within the 42 WT replicates demonstrate that `DESeq`, `DESeq2`, `edgeR`, `limma`, `EBSeq`, and `baySeq` control false positives well below 5%, whereas `DEGSeq`, `NOISeq`, and `SAMSeq` exhibit inflated false positive rates: [Well Supported].**  
  Drawing two disjoint groups of $n_r \in \{3, \dots, 20\}$ biological replicates exclusively from the 42 WT samples provides an assumption-free empirical null where any gene called at $\text{FDR} \le 0.05$ is a definitive false positive (Fig. 4). Under this test, `DEGSeq` yields a median FPR of $\sim 50\%$ across all $n_r$, while `SAMSeq` and `NOISeq` frequently exceed the 5% threshold. In contrast, `DESeq`, `EBSeq`, `baySeq`, `DESeq2`, `edgeR`, and `limma` maintain median empirical FPR near zero across all replicate numbers.

* **At full sample size (42 WT vs. 44 $\Delta snf2$), `DESeq2`, `edgeR (exact)`, and `limma` converge tightly with assumption-free nonparametric tests (Mann–Whitney, permutation, and bootstrap): [Well Supported].**  
  Hierarchical clustering of binary significance vectors across all 7,126 genes (`pvclust` in Fig. 3) groups `DESeq2`, `edgeR (exact)`, and `limma` into a single tight cluster (Cluster 3, AU support 97%–100%) alongside the two-sample $t$-test, log-ratio $t$-test, Mann–Whitney test, permutation test, and Studentized bootstrap test, while separating `DEGSeq`, `NOISeq`, `baySeq`, and `edgeR (GLM)` into an outlier cluster (Cluster 2). Because the rank-based Mann–Whitney and permutation tests make no parametric negative-binomial or variance-shrinkage assumptions, this convergence confirms that the Cluster 1/3 tools capture genuine statistical differences at high $n_r$.

* **Default read-count normalizations across 60%–75% transcriptome-wide remodeling in $\Delta snf2$ accurately calibrate absolute fold-change thresholds ($T$): [Moderately Supported].**  
  In Materials and Methods (line 136), the authors note that ERCC spike-in mixes 1 and 2 were added to all 96 libraries specifically to allow external calibration, and on line 146 they note that default count normalizations assume the majority of genes do not change between conditions. However, when 60%–75% of all 7,126 yeast genes are called SDE between WT and $\Delta snf2$ (line 40), global median-of-ratios (`DESeq`) or TMM (`edgeR`) normalization centers the global fold-change distribution near zero, which can shift modest unidirectional global transcriptional down-regulation in a chromatin-remodeling mutant (`Δsnf2`) into apparent up-regulation of non-target genes. While this does not affect the within-WT FPR benchmark (Fig. 4) or relative tool concordance under symmetric normalization (Fig. 3), some low-fold-change calls ($T < 0.3$) at $n_r = 42$ may reflect composition bias rather than absolute per-cell mRNA changes.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Compare ERCC Spike-In Normalization Against Default Endogenous Gene Normalization at $n_r = 42$
* **WHY:** Because the $\Delta snf2$ mutation disrupts global SWI/SNF chromatin remodeling and alters 60%–75% of yeast genes, endogenous library-size normalizations (TMM, DESeq size factors) can convert a global shift in total mRNA per cell into apparent small fold changes ($|\log_2 \text{FC}| < 0.3$) in housekeeping genes. Since the authors thoughtfully included ERCC spike-ins in all 96 libraries (line 136), they can directly test how much of the low-fold-change SDE cohort ($T = 0$ vs. $T = 0.3$) is sensitive to normalization choice.
* **WHAT:** Quantify the shift in reference $\log_2 \text{FC}$ and total SDE gene count at $n_r = 42$ when size factors are estimated from the ERCC spike-in controls (accounting for the Mix 1 / Mix 2 known ratios) versus endogenous yeast genes.
* **HOW:** Compute `DESeq2` size factors from the ERCC control counts (corrected for known Mix 1/Mix 2 concentrations), re-run `DESeq2` on the 42 WT vs. 44 $\Delta snf2$ dataset, and report the global $\log_2 \text{FC}$ offset and overlap with the endogenous gold standard.

#### 2. Add a Consensus Nonparametric Gold Standard Comparison for Figure 2
* **WHY:** In Figure 2, each tool's subsampled TPR and FPR are evaluated against *that same tool's* 42v44 call set. As a result, a tool that calls >80% of the genome SDE (`DEGSeq`, `PoissonSeq`, `NOISeq`) is judged on its ability to reproduce its own inflated full-data calls, whereas a conservative tool (`DESeq`) is judged against a smaller denominator.
* **WHAT:** Supplement the self-referential TPR/FPR curves in Figure 2 with a benchmark evaluated against a single shared, tool-independent reference set: the consensus SDE call set of the nonparametric permutation and Mann–Whitney tests at $n_r = 42\text{ vs. }44$.
* **HOW:** Define the ground-truth positive set $G_{\text{NP}}$ as genes with BH-adjusted $P \le 0.05$ in the 42v44 permutation test (which relies on zero parametric distribution assumptions), and plot a supplementary version of Figure 2 where all 11 tools are scored against $G_{\text{NP}}$.

#### 3. Investigate and Document Why `edgeR (GLM)` Diverged from `edgeR (exact)` in Figure 3
* **WHY:** For a simple two-group comparison without covariates (WT vs. $\Delta snf2$), `edgeR`'s exact test and negative-binomial GLM likelihood-ratio / quasi-likelihood test should produce nearly identical $P$-values when configured with the same dispersion estimation (`estimateCommonDisp` + `estimateTagwiseDisp`). Yet in Figure 3, `edgeR (exact)` clusters tightly in Cluster 3 while `edgeR (GLM)` clusters with the outlier tools (`DEGSeq`, `NOISeq`, `baySeq`) in Cluster 2.
* **WHAT:** Check the pipeline script for `edgeR (GLM)` to verify whether tagwise dispersions (`estimateGLMTagwiseDisp`) were omitted (leaving only common dispersion) or whether a design-matrix specification caused the discrepancy.
* **HOW:** Inspect the `edgeR (GLM)` function call in the `profDGE48` repository, confirm whether `estimateGLMCommonDisp` $\to$ `estimateGLMTrendedDisp` $\to$ `estimateGLMTagwiseDisp` were all executed prior to `glmFit`/`glmLRT`, and note the exact cause of the divergence between `edgeR (exact)` and `edgeR (GLM)` in the text.
