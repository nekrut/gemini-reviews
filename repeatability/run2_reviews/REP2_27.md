### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This study analyzes the first released genome sequence (`MN908947`) of the novel coronavirus (`2019-nCoV` / SARS-CoV-2) alongside 271–275 other coronavirus sequences from GenBank using maximum-likelihood phylogenetics (`RAxML` GTR+G), sliding-window similarity plotting (`SimPlot`), and relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) squared Euclidean distances across eight vertebrate species (*Bungarus multicinctus*, *Naja atra*, *Rhinolophus sinicus*, *Gallus gallus*, *Marmota*, *Homo sapiens*, *Manis javanica*, and *Erinaceus europaeus*). The authors conclude that `2019-nCoV` is a sister lineage to bat coronaviruses `bat-SL-CoVZC45` and `bat-SL-CoVZXC21`, that homologous recombination occurred in the spike glycoprotein gene with an "origin-unknown coronavirus," and that snakes (*B. multicinctus* and *N. atra*) are the most likely wildlife animal reservoir because they exhibit the smallest squared Euclidean distance in RSCU to `2019-nCoV`.

While the whole-genome phylogenetic placement of `MN908947` as sister to the Zhoushan bat sarbecoviruses (`ZC45`/`ZXC21`) among the sequences available at the time is valid, the manuscript's primary claims—that snakes are the intermediate reservoir host of `2019-nCoV` based on RSCU similarity and that a localized SimPlot drop in Spike proves homologous recombination—are methodologically invalid and biologically implausible. First, the RSCU analysis suffers from severe ascertainment and sample-size bias: whereas whole-genome CDS annotations (28,947 to 49,453 CDSs; 16.7 million to 40.6 million codons) were used for hedgehog, marmot, pangolin, chicken, and human, only **10 CDSs** (8,081 codons) were used for the bat *Rhinolophus sinicus*, **38 CDSs** (5,381 codons) for *Bungarus multicinctus*, and **64 CDSs** (9,587 codons) for *Naja atra* (Section 2.3, line 50), and `MN908947` itself was analyzed as "1 CDS's, 9672 codons" (concatenating or misparsing the multi-ORF coronavirus genome). Second, general genomic RSCU similarity between a virus and a vertebrate does not identify reservoir hosts: RNA viruses (and coronaviruses in particular) have strong genome-wide AU-rich mutational biases driven by viral replication/deamination rather than host translational selection, and all known *Betacoronavirus* hosts are exclusively mammals (never reptiles). Third, a localized sequence divergence dip between `2019-nCoV` and `Clade A` (`ZC45`/`ZXC21`) in Spike on a 3-clade SimPlot without a second parental lineage crossing over does not establish homologous recombination at `2019-nCoV` Spike (especially as the claimed bootscanning plot is cited as "data not shown," line 150). These fatal flaws cannot be remedied without removing the snake reservoir claim, using complete host transcriptomes/genomes with appropriate null models for viral compositional bias, and applying formal recombination tests with identified parental lineages.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **2019-nCoV has the most similar codon usage bias (RSCU) to snakes (*Bungarus multicinctus* and *Naja atra*), indicating that snakes are the most probable wildlife reservoir (lines 8, 24, 74, 156, Figure 3, Table 1): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  This conclusion rests on three compounding methodological errors:
  1. **Extreme sampling bias across host genomes (line 50):** For five species (*Erinaceus europaeus*, *Marmota*, *Manis javanica*, *Gallus gallus*, *Homo sapiens*), the authors computed RSCU across 28,947 to 49,453 genes (16.7M–40.7M codons), yielding stable genome-wide mammalian/avian averages. In contrast, for *Bungarus multicinctus* (38 CDSs, 5,381 codons), *Naja atra* (64 CDSs, 9,587 codons), and *Rhinolophus sinicus* (10 CDSs, 8,081 codons), the authors downloaded only tiny, non-random sets of individual genes from GenBank (for venomous snakes, GenBank nucleotide entries are heavily enriched for short, AT-rich venom gland toxin transcripts), even though complete reference genomes for both *R. sinicus* and *Ophiophagus hannah* / *Notechis scutatus* / *Protobothrops* were publicly available.
  2. **Failure to account for coronavirus intrinsic AU-bias:** `2019-nCoV` (`MN908947`) has an ~62% AU genome composition and strongly prefers U/A-ending codons across almost all amino acids (Table 1: e.g., Phe UUU = 1.41, Leu UUA = 1.64, Leu CUU = 1.75, Val GUU = 1.95, Ser UCU = 1.96, Pro CCU = 1.94, Thr ACU = 1.78, Ala GCU = 2.19). Comparing raw RSCU vectors via squared Euclidean distance without controlling for background nucleotide composition (e.g., $\text{ENC}$-plot vs. GC3s or neutrality plots) merely groups `2019-nCoV` with whichever host gene subset happens to have the lowest GC3 content in the biased GenBank query. Notably, in Table 1 and Figure 3A, the bat coronavirus `bat-SL-CoVZC45` has virtually identical RSCU values to `2019-nCoV` and is therefore also closer in RSCU Euclidean distance to the 38 snake CDSs than to the 10 *Rhinolophus sinicus* CDSs—even though `bat-SL-CoVZC45` was isolated directly from *Rhinolophus sinicus* bats! This internal negative control directly refutes the authors' premise that RSCU Euclidean distance identifies the natural reservoir host.
  3. **Biological implausibility:** Every known member of the genus *Betacoronavirus* (and subgenus *Sarbecovirus*) infects mammals exclusivamente; no betacoronavirus has ever been shown to infect or replicate in ectothermic reptiles (snakes).

* **Homologous recombination occurred within the viral spike glycoprotein (1–783 bp) between bat coronaviruses (Clade A) and an "origin-unknown coronavirus" (lines 8, 24, 63, 150, Figure 2): [Fragile].**  
  In Figure 2, the authors plot sequence similarity of `2019-nCoV` against three composite groups: Clade A (`ZC45`/`ZXC21`), Clade B (`BM48-31`/`BtKY72`), and Clade C (267 other coronaviruses). Demonstrating homologous recombination requires showing statistically significant phylogenetic incongruence (e.g., via RDP4, GARD, or Bootscan with a specific donor sequence) where the query shifts topological affinity from Parent 1 to Parent 2. In Figure 2, Clade A remains the closest match or drops toward background noise in the N-terminal domain / RBD of Spike without Clade B or Clade C rising above Clade A to act as a donor parent, and the authors state that BLAST of Spike bases 1–783 found "no similar sequence... in the database" and refer to bootscanning as "(data not shown)" (line 150). While sarbecoviruses do undergo frequent recombination in nature, a drop in similarity to `ZC45`/`ZXC21` in hypervariable regions of Spike can arise from accelerated diversifying selection or lineage divergence as well as recombination with an unsampled sarbecovirus, and cannot be proven by a 3-line SimPlot alone.

* **Phylogenetic placement of 2019-nCoV as a sister lineage to bat-SL-CoVZC45 and bat-SL-CoVZXC21 within Betacoronavirus (lines 58, 150, Figure 1): [Well Supported].**  
  Among the sequences included in the alignment, `2019-nCoV` forms a well-supported clade (100% bootstrap) sister to the Zhoushan bat sarbecoviruses `bat-SL-CoVZC45` and `bat-SL-CoVZXC21`, distinct from SARS-CoV and the European/African bat sarbecoviruses (`BM48-31` and `BtKY72`).

* **Inconsistent genome counts and erroneous single-CDS parsing of the coronavirus genome (lines 32, 45, 50, 58, 150): [Methodologically Flawed].**  
  The manuscript reports three different numbers for the total coronavirus genomes analyzed: Section 2.1 (line 32) says `271 genome sequences (>19 000 bp in length) were used in this study together with the above-described Beta-coronavirus (2019-nCoV, MN908947)` (= 272); Figure 1 caption (line 45) and Discussion (line 150) say `272 near-complete genome sequences`; but Results Section 3.1 (line 58) says `Phylogenetic analysis of 276 coronavirus genomes` and sums Clade A (2) + Clade B (2) + Clade C (267) + 2019-nCoV (1–5?) = 272 or 276. Furthermore, Section 2.3 (line 50) states that RSCU was calculated on `2019-nCoV genome (1CDS's, 9672 codons)` and `bat-SL-CoVZC45 genome (1CDS's, 9680 codons)`, treating the ~29.9 kb multi-ORF polycistronic coronavirus genome (which contains ORF1ab with a $-1$ ribosomal frameshift plus S, ORF3a, E, M, ORF6, ORF7a, ORF7b, ORF8, N, and ORF10) as a single continuous CDS.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Retract the Snake Reservoir Claim and Re-Run Codon Usage Analysis Using Complete Annotated Proteomes and Compositional Controls
* **WHY:** Using only 10 CDSs for *Rhinolophus sinicus* and 38–64 biased GenBank CDSs for snakes while using >28,000 whole-genome CDSs for mammals, and ignoring the fact that bat coronavirus `bat-SL-CoVZC45` (isolated from *R. sinicus*) shows the exact same RSCU distance pattern as `2019-nCoV`, renders the snake reservoir inference an artifact of GenBank query bias and viral AU mutational pressure.
* **WHAT:** Re-compute host RSCU tables using complete RefSeq coding transcriptomes for all candidate species (including the full *Rhinolophus sinicus* and *Rhinolophus ferrumequinum* RefSeq annotations and full snake genome annotations), parse the 10–12 individual canonical ORFs of `2019-nCoV` rather than "1 CDS", and evaluate whether viral codon usage reflects mutational bias ($\text{GC3s}$ / $\text{ENC}$ plot) rather than host translational selection.
* **HOW:** Download the complete RefSeq CDS FASTA (`*_cds_from_genomic.fna.gz`) for *R. sinicus*, *R. ferrumequinum*, *Manis javanica*, *Homo sapiens*, and reptilian outgroups; compute RSCU and Effective Number of Codons ($\text{ENC}$) vs. $\text{GC3s}$; verify that known bat coronaviruses (`ZC45`, `ZXC21`, `WIV1`, `RsSHC014`) cluster with `2019-nCoV` due to shared viral nucleotide composition rather than snake host adaptation; and remove the claim that snakes are the wildlife reservoir of `2019-nCoV`.

#### 2. Perform Formal Recombination Detection with Separate Sub-Genomic Trees
* **WHY:** A drop in sequence identity to Clade A (`ZC45`/`ZXC21`) in the Spike region of a SimPlot (Figure 2) does not distinguish recombination with an unsampled parental lineage from localized sequence divergence or positive selection unless topological incongruence with a second lineage is demonstrated.
* **WHAT:** Run formal recombination detection algorithms (e.g., `RDP4` suite: RDP, GENECONV, BootScan, MaxChi, Chimaera, SiScan, 3Seq; or `GARD`) across individual sarbecovirus genomes (uncollapsed into clades) and construct separate maximum-likelihood phylogenetic trees for ORF1ab, Spike (S1 vs. S2), and N.
* **HOW:** Align `MN908947` with all available individual *Sarbecovirus* genomes (without collapsing Clade C into a single consensus curve), present the actual BootScan plot (currently listed as "data not shown" in line 150) alongside side-by-side ML trees for the non-recombinant backbone vs. the putative recombinant Spike segment, and temper the text where no specific donor parent sequence is present in GenBank.

#### 3. Reconcile Genome Counts and Correct Factual/Editorial Errors
* **WHY:** Contradictory sequence counts (271 vs. 272 vs. 276) and inaccurate statements about coronavirus host range and Wuhan market wildlife sales undermine accuracy.
* **WHAT & HOW:**
  * **Sequence count discrepancy (lines 32, 45, 58, 150):** Reconcile why Section 2.1 states 271 + 1 = 272 sequences, Figure 1 and Section 4 state 272 sequences, and Section 3.1 states 276 sequences (with 2 + 2 + 267 = 271 in Clades A–C).
  * **Coronavirus host range citation (line 22):** Line 22 claims *"Coronavirus can infect humans and many different animal species, including... snake, and other wildlife animals [7, 9],"* but neither cited review (Cui et al., *Nat Rev Microbiol* 2019 [7]; Su et al., *Trends Microbiol* 2016 [9]) documents coronavirus infection in snakes. Remove "snake" from the list of established coronavirus hosts in line 22.
  * **Coding sequence parsing (line 50):** Replace the single concatenated `1CDS's, 9672 codons` extraction of `MN908947` with the properly spliced `ORF1ab` (accounting for the slippery sequence at nt 13,468) and structural/accessory ORFs annotated in `MN908947`.
