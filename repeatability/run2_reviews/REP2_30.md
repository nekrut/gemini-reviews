### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This manuscript introduces the Mixed Effects Model of Evolution (MEME), a phylogenetic codon-substitution framework designed to detect both episodic and pervasive diversifying positive selection at individual codon sites without requiring *a priori* specification of foreground lineages. Building on branch-site random effects models, MEME treats the site-level synonymous rate ($\alpha$) and a two-category non-synonymous rate distribution ($\beta^- \le \alpha$ with branch weight $q^-$ and unrestricted $\beta^+$ with branch weight $q^+ = 1 - q^-$) as site-specific fixed effects while marginalizing over the branch-specific selective category assignments as independent random effects directly within Felsenstein's pruning algorithm using mixture transition matrices $P(t_b) = q^- e^{Q(\beta^-)t_b} + (1 - q^-) e^{Q(\beta^+)t_b}$. Hypothesis testing against the null constraint $\beta^+ \le \alpha$ uses a calibrated $0.33 \chi^2_0 + 0.30 \chi^2_1 + 0.37 \chi^2_2$ asymptotic mixture distribution verified by parametric bootstrap under the worst-case neutral null ($\beta^- = \beta^+ = \alpha, q^- \in (0,1)$). Through extensive neutral and alternative simulations across empirical TreeBase phylogenies and three detailed simulation topologies (Japanese encephalitis virus *env*, vertebrate rhodopsin, and camelid VHH), as well as comparative analyses of 16 diverse empirical alignments against the constant-rate Fixed Effects Likelihood (FEL) method, the authors show that MEME controls Type I error at or below nominal levels, matches FEL's power under pervasive selection, dramatically outperforms FEL under episodic selection (identifying 237 sites across the 16 empirical datasets vs. 63 by FEL, including 56 sites where FEL infers overall purifying selection because background conservation masks lineage-specific bursts of diversification), and resolves the counterintuitive loss of power with increased taxon sampling previously reported in vertebrate rhodopsin and influenza H3N2 hemagglutinin.

This is a landmark methodological contribution to molecular evolution. The mathematical formulation (exact $O(B)$ marginalization over $2^B$ branch configurations via mixture transition matrices), calibration of the non-standard boundary likelihood ratio test statistic, symmetric benchmarking against FEL under both constant and variable selection regimes, and candid formulation of the "selection inference uncertainty principle" (demonstrating via simulation that empirical Bayes identification of specific branch-site pairs has inherently high false-positive or low-power trade-offs because sample size is $N = 1$ branch-site realization) are executed with exceptional rigor and intellectual honesty. Only minor modeling caveats—several of which the authors already explicitly discuss—warrant constructive suggestions: (1) sensitivity to multinucleotide mutations (simultaneous double/triple nucleotide substitutions within a codon, which violate the single-nucleotide instantaneous rate matrix assumption in Equation 1 and can mimic high $\beta^+$ on single short branches); (2) tree-wide shared relative branch lengths vs. site-specific branch length scaling; and (3) reporting guidelines for multiple-testing correction ($q$-values vs. nominal $p \le 0.05$ site counts).

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Exact marginalization over $2^B$ branch-specific $\omega$ configurations is computationally tractable in $O(B)$ time under branch independence via mixture transition matrices (lines 42–60, Equations 1–2): [Well Supported].**  
  By assuming conditional independence of selective categories across branches at a given site, the sum over $2^B$ branch configuration vectors factors cleanly into Felsenstein's pruning recursion by substituting each branch transition probability matrix with the convex combination $q^- e^{Q(\beta^-)t_b} + (1 - q^-) e^{Q(\beta^+)t_b}$. This mathematical identity is exact and reduces an exponential-time problem to a single standard pruning traversal per likelihood evaluation.

* **The $0.33 \chi^2_0 + 0.30 \chi^2_1 + 0.37 \chi^2_2$ mixture distribution controls Type I error at or below the nominal $\alpha = 0.05$ level under the worst-case neutral null across diverse tree sizes and divergences (lines 79, 115–117, Figure S1, Tables S1–S2): [Well Supported].**  
  Because testing $\beta^+ = \alpha$ when $q^+$ can lie on the boundary ($q^+ = 0$ or $1$, making $\beta^+$ or $\beta^-$ unidentifiable under the null) violates regular Wilks' theorem regularity conditions, the authors calibrate the asymptotic mixture against parametric bootstraps under strict neutrality ($\omega = 1$ across all branches, which is the least favorable composite null configuration compared to $\beta^- < \alpha$) and verify across 100 TreeBase trees and taxon counts from $N = 8$ to $N > 100$ that false-positive rates remain well controlled ($0.029$–$0.056$ at nominal $p = 0.05$).

* **MEME matches FEL power under pervasive selection and achieves a large power advantage when diversifying selection is episodic, including at sites classified as negatively selected by FEL (lines 122–147, 157–207, Tables 1–2): [Well Supported].**  
  When calibrated to an identical 5% empirical Type I error rate under constant $\omega$, MEME matches or slightly exceeds FEL (Table S3). Under episodic selection ($q^+ = 0.10$ or $0.25$ with background $\omega^- \in \{0, 0.2, 0.4\}$), FEL has near-zero power ($< 0.04$ at $q^+ = 0.10$) because the tree-averaged $\bar{\omega} = q^- \omega^- + q^+ \omega^+$ is $< 1$, whereas MEME achieves substantial power that increases with taxon count (Table 1). This directly explains the 56 empirical sites ($M^+ F^-$ in Table 2, exemplified by vertebrate rhodopsin codon 210 in Figure 2) where FEL infers significant purifying selection ($p \le 0.05$) while MEME detects a highly significant burst of episodic diversification ($\Delta \log L = 19.3, p = 10^{-8}$ at codon 210).

* **Modeling lineage-specific $\omega$ heterogeneity resolves the taxon-sampling pathology where adding sequences reduces the number of detected positively selected sites (lines 19, 193–195, 222, Tables S20–S21): [Well Supported].**  
  In vertebrate rhodopsin (Yokoyama et al., 2008) and influenza H3N2 HA (Chen & Sun, 2011), constant-$\omega$ random-effects models detected 7 sites in squirrelfish and 2 in fish, but 0 in the full vertebrate alignment because adding conserved tetrapod clades diluted the tree-wide average $\omega$. MEME detects 1 site in squirrelfish, 7 in fish, and 19 in the complete alignment (recovering 9 of the 10 subset sites plus 3 experimentally validated $\lambda_{\max}$-shifting sites: 96, 183, and 195), demonstrating that statistical power increases monotonically with taxon sampling once background purifying selection is decoupled from foreground diversification.

* **Empirical Bayes (EB) identification of specific branch-site pairs under selection has inherently limited accuracy ("selection inference uncertainty principle") and should be restricted to exploratory visualization (lines 102, 150–152, 239, Figures S2–S3): [Well Supported].**  
  Rather than overclaiming branch-site resolution, the authors run targeted simulations (Figures S2–S3) showing that even at EBF $\ge 20$, while individual positively selected branches in a strongly conserved background ($\omega^- = 0.1$) are detected ~45% of the time, at least one neutral background branch ($\omega^- = 1.0$) is falsely flagged at 48% of sites. Their theoretical explanation—that branch-site inference relies on a sample size of $N = 1$ realization of the Markov substitution process along a single branch at a single codon—is a model of statistical clarity.

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Discuss the Impact of Simultaneous Multinucleotide Substitutions (MNMs) and Alignment Errors on Single-Branch Episodic Signals
* **WHY:** Because MEME's instantaneous rate matrix (Equation 1, line 36) assigns zero instantaneous rate to codon substitutions requiring $>1$ nucleotide change, a single biological multinucleotide mutation (or a localized sequencing/alignment artifact on a single terminal branch) that changes 2 or 3 nucleotides within one codon along a very short branch ($t_b \ll 1$) forces the model to infer an extreme $\beta^+ \gg \alpha$ with small weight $q^+ \approx 1/B$ to explain multiple instantaneous single-step transitions in near-zero time.
* **WHAT:** Add a brief discussion caveat (and, where helpful, an diagnostic filter flag) noting that sites where the likelihood improvement $\Delta \text{LRT}$ is driven entirely by a single branch carrying a double/triple nucleotide substitution should be inspected for multinucleotide mutation or alignment error.
* **HOW:** Note in the Discussion (near line 239) how branch-level likelihood contributions or empirical Bayes factors (EBF) can be used diagnostically: if only a single short branch has EBF $\gg 20$ and involves a 2- or 3-nucleotide codon change while all other branches have 0 non-synonymous changes, users should verify alignment quality and consider whether a single-event multinucleotide mutation could explain the observation.

#### 2. Clarify Synonymous Rate Variation ($\alpha_s$) vs. Lineage-Specific Synonymous Rate Heterogeneity
* **WHY:** In Step 2 (line 77), MEME estimates a site-specific synonymous rate $\alpha$ (which is shared across all branches at that site, scaling the global branch lengths $t_b$), whereas the non-synonymous rate varies across branches between $\beta^-$ and $\beta^+$. If a single branch experiences a localized cluster of both synonymous and non-synonymous substitutions (e.g., due to gene conversion or localized hypermutation not captured by alignment-wide GARD breakpoints), fixing $\alpha$ across branches while allowing $\beta^+$ to vary across branches could absorb branch-specific rate acceleration into $\beta^+$.
* **WHAT:** Explicitly note in the Methods/Discussion that MEME models site-to-site synonymous rate variation ($\alpha_s$) but assumes that relative branch lengths for synonymous substitutions are proportional across the tree within a recombination-free partition.
* **HOW:** Add 2–3 sentences to Section "The MEME test for episodic diversifying selection" (line 77) clarifying that while site-level $\alpha$ protects against site-to-site synonymous rate heterogeneity [26], recombination partitioning (via GARD, as applied to JEV *env* in line 212) is essential prior to MEME analysis so that local tree/branch-length distortions do not mimic branch-specific $\beta^+$ bursts.

#### 3. Provide Guidance on Interpreting Site Counts at Nominal $p \le 0.05$ vs. Benjamini-Hochberg $q$-Values in Table 2
* **WHY:** In Table 2 (line 164), empirical site counts ($M^+ F^0 = 130$, $M^+ F^+ = 51$, $M^+ F^- = 56$) are tabulated at the unadjusted site-wise threshold $p \le 0.05$, while Discussion line 233 thoughtfully notes that Benjamini-Hochberg $q$-value upper bounds are provided in Tables S4–S19 and can be substantially larger than nominal $p$-values. Readers comparing Table 2 summary totals may overlook how many of the 237 sites survive FDR control (e.g., at $q \le 0.10$ or $0.20$), especially given that the neutral null mixture makes the nominal $p$-values conservative at predominantly conserved sites.
* **WHAT:** Add a parenthetical count or summary column in Table 2 (or in the Discussion paragraph at line 233) indicating the number of sites detected at $q \le 0.10$ (or $q \le 0.20$) alongside nominal $p \le 0.05$, and explain why Benjamini-Hochberg $q$-values computed from conservative upper-bound $p$-values are doubly conservative when most sites evolve under purifying selection ($\beta^- \ll \alpha$).
* **HOW:** Summarize the total number of empirical sites meeting $q \le 0.10$ and $q \le 0.20$ across the 16 datasets in the Discussion (line 233) and note that methods estimating the proportion of true nulls ($\hat{\pi}_0$) can be upward-biased when null $p$-values are stochastically larger than $\text{Uniform}(0,1)$.
