### 1. SUMMARY AND OVERALL IMPACT STATEMENT

This study tests whether the human lineage experienced more protein-coding positive Darwinian selection than the chimpanzee lineage since their divergence 6–7 million years ago. The authors assemble 13,888 human–chimpanzee–macaque orthologous gene alignments (covering 17,995,887 nucleotides with chimpanzee base quality $Q \ge 20$ and alignment length $\ge 100$ codons) and apply the PAML branch-site likelihood ratio test (Test 2) independently to the human and chimpanzee branches. At nominal $P < 0.05$, they identify 233 positively selected genes (PSGs) in chimpanzees versus 154 in humans (ratio $1.51$, $\chi^2$ test $P < 10^{-4}$); at a 5% false discovery rate (FDR), 59 chimpanzee genes versus 2 human genes remain significant. Conversely, the genome-wide mean nonsynonymous-to-synonymous substitution rate ratio ($\omega$) is significantly higher in humans ($0.259 \pm 0.002$) than in chimpanzees ($0.245 \pm 0.002$), which remains true across 13,508 shared non-PSGs ($0.252$ vs. $0.238$). The authors interpret both patterns through standard population genetic theory: a smaller long-term effective population size ($N_e$) in hominins reduced the efficacy of both purifying and positive selection. Sequence sources and quality scores are publicly accessible via Ensembl and UCSC, though the processed 13,888 sequence alignments and PAML control/output files are not deposited in a persistent public repository.

This is a well-designed comparative genomics study that corrects two major flaws of earlier genome-wide scans: using a distant rodent outgroup (replaced here by macaque) and analyzing unfiltered draft chimpanzee sequences (addressed by evaluating $Q0$, $Q10$, and $Q20$ thresholds and benchmarking synonymous branch-length symmetry). Nevertheless, several methodological limitations temper the strength of the conclusions: (1) the PAML branch-site test is highly sensitive to clustered nonsynonymous changes caused by local alignment errors, cryptic paralogy, or alternative splicing mismatches in draft Ensembl v36 chimpanzee gene models, which are asymmetrically less curated than human models; (2) higher intraspecific polymorphism in chimpanzees ($\pi_{\text{chimp}} \approx 2\text{--}3 \times \pi_{\text{human}}$) contributes more unfixed segregating variants to a single-genome divergence scan; and (3) downstream enrichment tests (such as the borderline association between human PSGs and OMIM Mendelian disease genes at $P = 0.049$) do not control for gene length or alignment depth, which jointly increase statistical power to detect PSGs and the probability of harboring disease mutations. These issues can be resolved through computational re-analysis using symmetric alignment filtering and covariate-adjusted enrichment models.

### 2. SPECIFIC CONCLUSIONS AND ASSESSMENT OF SUPPORT

* **Chimpanzees have significantly more detectable protein-coding PSGs than humans across quality thresholds and multiple-testing corrections: [Moderately Supported].**  
  The authors demonstrate that unfiltered ($Q0$) and intermediate ($Q10$) chimpanzee data inflate synonymous and nonsynonymous substitution counts, and that filtering at $Q20$ equalizes the chimp-to-human synonymous substitution ratio ($r = 0.985 \pm 0.008$; 29,644 vs. 30,083 synonymous changes). Under $Q20$, chimpanzees exhibit more PSGs at $P < 0.05$ (233 vs. 154), 5% FDR (59 vs. 2), and Bonferroni correction (21 vs. 2). However, while $Q20$ controls independent single-nucleotide sequencing errors, it does not eliminate gene-model annotation discrepancies in the 4x draft chimpanzee assembly (where Ensembl v36 projected human exons onto gapped draft contigs, frequently aligning frameshifted or intronic sequence at exon boundaries) or clustered multi-nucleotide errors. Because the branch-site test flags positive selection whenever a small fraction of sites exhibits $\omega_2 > 1$ on the foreground branch, even a single misaligned exon boundary generates extreme likelihood ratio test statistics, which helps explain why the chimp-to-human PSG ratio balloons from $1.51\times$ at $P < 0.05$ to $29.5\times$ at 5% FDR. Furthermore, re-testing only the 233 4x chimpanzee PSGs in the 6x assembly (where 9% lost significance) without re-running the full 13,888-gene screen in 6x is an asymmetric validation.

* **Genome-wide and non-PSG $\omega$ values are significantly higher in the human lineage than in the chimpanzee lineage, reflecting relaxed purifying selection due to smaller human $N_e$: [Well Supported].**  
  Across all 13,888 genes, humans accumulated 19,000 nonsynonymous and 30,083 synonymous substitutions ($\omega = 0.259$), whereas chimpanzees accumulated 17,701 nonsynonymous and 29,644 synonymous substitutions ($\omega = 0.245$, $P < 10^{-4}$). This pattern holds across the 13,508 non-PSGs ($\omega = 0.252$ vs. $0.238$) and runs in the opposite direction of any residual chimpanzee sequencing error (which would inflate chimpanzee $\omega$ toward 1.0). The conclusion that humans experienced less effective purifying selection is robust and consistent with independent demographic estimates of smaller hominin $N_e$.

* **Human PSGs are significantly enriched for human Mendelian disease genes compared to non-PSGs ($P = 0.049$) and chimpanzee PSGs ($P = 0.044$): [Fragile].**  
  In Table 2, 15 of 154 human PSGs (9.7%) are OMIM disease genes compared to 832 of 13,734 non-PSGs (6.1%, Fisher's exact $P = 0.049$). Aside from being borderline at $\alpha = 0.05$ with no correction for multiple downstream hypothesis tests, this 2x2 contingency test ignores a major confounder: coding sequence length and total substitution count. Both the power of the branch-site likelihood ratio test and the probability that a gene is cataloged in OMIM scale positively with coding sequence length and expression breadth. Without a logistic regression controlling for alignment length and background conservation, this enrichment cannot be distinguished from length-driven ascertainment bias.

* **The distribution of PSGs provides no support for the chromosomal speciation hypothesis or brain-specific acceleration in humans: [Well Supported].**  
  Testing 13,888 genes across rearranged (pericentric inversions plus human chromosome 2) versus colinear chromosomes shows slightly lower PSG proportions on rearranged chromosomes in both lineages ($P = 0.10$ in human, $P = 0.055$ in chimp; Fig. 3), directly contradicting the prediction that rearranged chromosomes accumulated adaptive divergence under parapatric introgression. Similarly, peak-expression microarray comparisons across 20 tissue groups show no significant enrichment of brain-expressed PSGs in humans versus chimpanzees ($\chi^2 = 1.74, P = 0.19$; Fig. 2).

### 3. CONSTRUCTIVE AND DOABLE SUGGESTIONS FOR IMPROVEMENT

#### 1. Audit and Filter Local Exon-Boundary Misalignments and Clustered Substitutions
* **WHY:** The PAML branch-site test assumes a correct codon alignment and independent single-nucleotide substitutions. In Ensembl v36, chimpanzee gene models on the 4x `panTro1` assembly were computationally projected from human transcripts; when a chimpanzee exon contained an assembly gap, automated gene builds frequently shifted splice junctions by a few codons, creating short runs of apparent nonsynonymous substitutions that pass $Q20$ base quality and the coarse $<10\%$ whole-gene divergence filter.
* **WHAT:** Apply a sliding-window cluster filter to remove local alignment artifacts and re-evaluate the 59 FDR-significant chimpanzee PSGs and 233 nominal PSGs.
* **HOW:** Flag and mask any 10-codon window containing $\ge 3$ lineage-specific substitutions or adjacent indels before running `codeml`, or manually inspect the 59 FDR-significant chimpanzee alignments against `panTro2` (6x) genomic contigs and cDNA evidence to verify that the foreground nonsynonymous changes do not fall within 15 bp of an intron–exon boundary or masked gap.

#### 2. Quantify the Impact of Segregating Polymorphisms on Apparent Lineage Divergence
* **WHY:** Because chimpanzee effective population size and nucleotide diversity ($\pi \approx 0.1\%$) are roughly two- to three-fold higher than in humans, a single sequenced chimpanzee genome contains more heterozygous sites and unfixed lineage-specific polymorphisms than a single human genome. Under purifying selection, deleterious nonsynonymous variants segregate at higher frequencies in polymorphism than in fixed divergence (the McDonald–Kreitman effect), which can inflate apparent foreground $\omega$ in the more polymorphic species.
* **WHAT:** Model or bound the excess of chimpanzee PSGs attributable to segregating polymorphism in a single diploid individual.
* **HOW:** Using the authors' existing branch-site simulation framework (SI Tables 3–4), add a coalescent polymorphism layer at the tips with $\theta_{\text{chimp}} = 0.001$ and $\theta_{\text{human}} = 0.0004$ (accounting for the empirical fraction of heterozygous calls removed by the $Q20$ filter) and measure the resulting difference in branch-site false-positive and detection rates between the two terminal branches.

#### 3. Control for Coding Sequence Length and Expression Level in the OMIM Disease Enrichment Test
* **WHY:** Longer genes contain more codons, giving the likelihood ratio test higher statistical power to reach $P < 0.05$, and simultaneously present a larger mutational target for human Mendelian disorders in OMIM.
* **WHAT:** Replace the unadjusted Fisher's exact test in Table 2 with a multivariable logistic regression controlling for alignment length ($L$), macaque branch $\omega$, and tissue expression breadth.
* **HOW:** Fit $\text{logit}(P(\text{OMIM} = 1)) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \mathbb{I}(\text{Human PSG}) + \beta_2 \log(L) + \beta_3 \omega_{\text{macaque}}$ across the 13,888 genes in R and report the adjusted odds ratio and $P$-value for $\beta_1$.

#### 4. Perform Symmetric Validation on the 6x Chimpanzee Assembly
* **WHY:** Re-testing only the 233 PSGs discovered in the 4x assembly using the 6x assembly can only remove false positives in chimpanzees without accounting for how improved 6x coverage changes the denominator or affects human foreground tests (where chimpanzee is part of the background branch).
* **WHAT:** Run the branch-site test symmetrically for both human and chimpanzee foreground branches on the 233 chimp PSGs and 154 human PSGs (or the full orthologous set) in the 6x assembly.
* **HOW:** Re-extract the orthologous alignments for at least the combined 380 candidate PSGs from the 6x assembly, apply the identical $Q20$ filter, and report both human and chimpanzee PSG retention rates side by side.

#### 5. Technical, Mathematical, and Editorial Cleanup
* **WHY:** Missing equations in the Methods section and inconsistencies in gene counts reduce technical clarity.
* **WHAT & HOW:**

Restore the explicit equation $\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{(x_i - \hat{x}_i)^2}{\hat{x}_i} + \frac{(y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\hat{y}_i}$, where $\hat{x}_i = (x_i + y_i) \frac{147}{373}$ and $\hat{y}_i = (x_i + y_i) \frac{226}{373}$.
  * **Gene count discrepancy (lines 24 vs. 117):** Line 24 states that the $Q10$ dataset contains $13,924$ genes, whereas line 117 states $13,925$ genes. Reconcile the exact gene count.
  * **Data availability:** Deposit the 13,888 fasta alignments and PAML summary output tables in Dryad, Zenodo, or the journal's supplementary archive so that the exact likelihood ratio statistics ($2\Delta\ell$) per gene can be audited.
