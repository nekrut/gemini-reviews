### 1. Executive Summary
This study investigates partner selection in human observational learning using a novel two-stage behavioral paradigm (Passive Observation of two simulated Q-learning agents differing in softmax decision noise [$\beta = 20.0$ vs. $\beta = 1.5$], followed by Partner Selection and a 60-trial Observational Learning three-armed bandit task without direct self-feedback). Across a pilot study ($N = 20$) and a preregistered confirmatory experiment ($N = 55$; combined exploratory $N = 74$), the authors show that participants robustly prefer low-noise (high-performing) partners ($b = -1.06 \pm 0.25, p < 0.001$). Using mixed-effects logistic regressions and hierarchical Bayesian reinforcement learning models fit in Stan (validated via model recovery, group-stratified parameter recovery, and posterior predictive checks), they further show that preference for low-noise partners is associated with a higher relative weight on action imitation ($w_A$) relative to reward-based value learning ($V$). The computational and statistical execution is exemplary, though the inherent experimental confounding between partner decision noise and partner reward rate in a simple stationary 3-armed bandit—as well as minor statistical details in the option-wise GLMM pooling and equation notation—should be addressed.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
1. **Exemplary Preregistration and Computational Model Validation:** The confirmatory hypothesis and sample size ($N = 55$, powered at $>80\%$ from the $N = 20$ pilot via bootstrap power analysis) were preregistered on OSF (`https://osf.io/g6etf`). The hierarchical Bayesian modeling in Stan (`CmdStanPy`) is accompanied by 40-run model recovery (`S2A Fig`), parameter recovery stratified by partner-preference group (`S2B–C Fig`), 500-run posterior predictive checks reproducing both learning curves and GLMM coefficients (`S3 Fig`), and comparison against a dynamic-weight switching model (`S1 Text`).
2. **Inherent Confounding of Decision Noise ($\beta$) and Performance in a Stationary Three-Armed Bandit:** Both simulated partners operated with learning rate $\alpha = 0.3$ on a stationary 3-armed bandit with reward probabilities $\{0.25, 0.50, 0.75\}$. Because there are only 3 options and reward contingencies never change, the low-noise agent ($\beta = 20.0$) identifies the $0.75$ arm within a few trials and exploits it $>90\%$ of the time (Fig 1E), whereas the high-noise agent ($\beta = 1.5$) continues choosing suboptimal arms $\sim 45\%$ of the time. Consequently, partner decision noise is strongly collinear with partner reward rate (performance) and predictability, and the low-noise agent incurs almost no exploration deficit because 30 trials are more than sufficient to sample 3 arms. The authors thoughtfully address this in an exploratory regression (Fig 2D), showing that partner performance ($b = 0.759 \pm 0.169$) and predictability ($b = 0.389 \pm 0.161$)—rather than information gain ($b = -0.142 \pm 0.193$)—drive partner choice.
3. **Endogeneity of Observed Partner Statistics in the Test Phase:** In the 60-trial Observational Learning task, participants observed the partner they had selected. A low-noise partner ($\beta = 20.0$) repeatedly selects the optimal arm, making action imitation ($A$) nearly optimal and providing very little sampling of the unchosen arms, whereas a high-noise partner ($\beta = 1.5$) frequently selects suboptimal arms, penalizing pure imitation and providing informative reward outcomes across all three arms. Therefore, the between-participant association between partner preference and imitation weight $w_A$ (Cohen's $d = 0.58$, Fig 3D) is partially driven by rational adaptation to the chosen partner's behavior during the Observational Learning phase, as confirmed by the within-participant analysis ($N = 52$, Fig 4) showing that the same participant suppresses imitation when paired with a high-noise partner ($b = -0.375 \pm 0.021$). Fitting the Full computational model with separate $w_A$ parameters for low-noise vs. high-noise blocks within the $N = 52$ participants, or evaluating baseline imitation tendency during the pre-task practice Observational Learning block (where all participants observed the exact same $\beta = 7.0$ agent) as a predictor of subsequent partner selection, would cleanly separate trait-level learning style from state-level adaptation!
4. **Non-Independence of Option-Wise Binomial GLMMs (Equations 1 and 2):** To analyze trial-by-trial choices across the three bandit options ($X, Y, Z$), the authors fit three separate binomial mixed-effects logistic regressions (`logit P(choice = X)`, `logit P(choice = Y)`, `logit P(choice = Z)`) and computed a variance-weighted average of the coefficients and standard errors across the three models. Because the three options are mutually exclusive and exhaustive on every trial ($\sum_{k \in \{X,Y,Z\}} Y_{k,t} = 1$), the three option-wise estimates are fit to the exact same trials and are not independent replicates; pooling their standard errors via inverse-variance weighting as if they were three independent studies understates the true standard error by a factor of $\sim \sqrt{3}$. (Note that the authors wisely refrained from reporting $p$-values for these exploratory GLMMs, and the Bayesian softmax model in Equation 7 properly models the 3-way multinomial choice simultaneously).
5. **Typographical Error in Dependent Variable Description of Equation (1):** In Materials and Methods ("Regression analysis," immediately following Equation 1), the text states: *"where the dependent variable was coded as 1 if the partner's choice was X on trial t, or 0 if the participant chose Y or Z."* This should read *"if the **participant's** choice was X on trial t"*, since `choice = X` is the participant's choice and $C_t$ is the partner's choice.
6. **Typo in Figure 1 Caption:** In the caption to Fig 3, WAIC is defined as "Widely-applicable Akaike Information Criterion (WAIC)" instead of the standard "Widely Applicable Information Criterion" (Watanabe–Akaike Information Criterion).

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
- **Claim 1:** Humans predominantly prefer to learn from low-noise (consistent, high-performing) partners rather than high-noise (explorative) partners in a stationary multi-armed bandit task. — `[Well Supported]`. Replicated across both the pilot ($N = 20$) and preregistered main study ($N = 55$, $p < 0.001$) after controlling for spatial position and presentation order.
- **Claim 2:** Partner selection is primarily driven by the partner's observed reward performance and behavioral predictability rather than theoretical information gain. — `[Well Supported]`. Clearly demonstrated by the model-based decomposition of the Passive Observation trials (Fig 2D).
- **Claim 3:** Reliance on action imitation vs. reward outcome learning co-varies with partner preference and adapts dynamically to partner noise level. — `[Well Supported]`. Supported by both trial-level GLMMs and hierarchical Bayesian parameter estimates ($w_A$), with the authors appropriately discussing the distinction between stable individual traits and partner-induced state shifts in the Discussion.

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
- **Open Science & Data Availability:** The preregistration is publicly available on OSF (`https://osf.io/g6etf`), and all raw behavioral data, R (`lme4`) scripts, and Python/Stan (`CmdStanPy v1.2.1`, `Stan v2.35.0`) hierarchical modeling, simulation, and recovery scripts are publicly archived on Zenodo (`https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15386571`).
- **Mathematical & Experimental Transparency:** All task timings, reward probabilities, Q-learning agent parameters ($\alpha = 0.3$, $\beta \in \{1.5, 7.0, 20.0\}$), prior distributions, parameter transformations, and recovery metrics are documented with complete clarity.
