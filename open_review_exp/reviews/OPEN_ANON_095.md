### 1. Executive Summary
This study systematically evaluates how reducing the temporal resolution of pathogen sampling dates (rounding exact dates to the midpoint of the month or year for patient privacy) biases Bayesian phylodynamic inference of the reproductive number ($R_0$ or $R_e$), substitution rate, and time to the most recent common ancestor (tMRCA). Combining 400 birth-death outbreak simulations in BEAST v2.7.6 (ReMaster + Seq-Gen, yielding 1,800 MCMC analyses across 3 date resolutions and 2 tree priors, with 1,670 chains passing $\text{ESS} \ge 200$) with empirical analyses of five pathogen datasets spanning diverse evolutionary rates and sampling intervals—2009 H1N1 influenza ($n = 161$), 2020 Victoria SARS-CoV-2 ($n = 112$), 2000–2005 H3N2 influenza ($n = 60$), *Staphylococcus aureus* ($n = 104$), and *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* ($n = 30\text{ or }36$)—the authors test the hypothesis that systematic bias emerges when the temporal resolution lost by date-rounding exceeds the average time required for a genome to accrue one substitution, $T_s = (\text{Genome Length} \times \text{Substitution Rate})^{-1}$. The study is well-designed, theoretically grounded, and insightful in demonstrating opposite directions of substitution-rate bias under birth-death versus coalescent exponential tree priors at month resolution. Minor revisions are needed to address several typographical/numerical inconsistencies, clarify prior parameterizations, and discuss the comparison between naive midpoint rounding and Bayesian tip-date uncertainty integration.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
1. **Comparison of Midpoint Rounding vs. Bayesian Uniform Tip-Date Sampling:** Throughout the simulation and empirical analyses, reduced date resolution is implemented solely by deterministic midpoint imputation (setting all samples within a month to the 15th, or within a year to June 15th). While this accurately reflects common user practice when metadata are truncated, BEAST2 natively supports integrating over tip-date uncertainty via uniform prior intervals (e.g., $\text{Uniform}(\text{2020-08-01}, \text{2020-08-31})$). Under the birth-death model, collapsing all samples in a month to a single identical timestamp (the 15th) creates an artificial synchronous sampling burst that directly inflates the birth-death likelihood for rapid transmission and high substitution rate—an artifact of deterministic tie-creation rather than temporal uncertainty *per se*. Including even a small comparison or explicit discussion distinguishing *deterministic midpoint collapsing* from *uniform interval tip-date integration* (or random jittering within the month) would substantially sharpen the methodological conclusions.
2. **Internal Discrepancy in *M. tuberculosis* Empirical Sample Size ($n = 30$ vs. $n = 36$):** In Methods Overview (line 66), the text states *"and 30 M. tuberculosis samples from an $\approx 25$ year outbreak studied by [33]"*, whereas the dedicated *M. tuberculosis* section (line 137) states *"The M. tuberculosis dataset consists of 36 sequenced isolates from a retrospectively recognised outbreak..."*. The exact sample size used in the BEAST XMLs should be harmonized.
3. **Minor Numerical and Typographical Errors in Tables and Methods:**
   - **Table 1 Calculation for *S. aureus*:** For *S. aureus*, $[\text{Genome Length} \times \text{Substitution Rate}]^{-1} = [2,900,000 \times 1 \times 10^{-6}]^{-1} = 1 / 2.9 = 0.3448\text{ yrs}$, whereas Table 1 reports `0.3458`.
   - **MCMC Chain Length Typo (Line 95):** Line 95 states *"We ran each MCMC chain for $5 \times 18^8$ steps"*, which is a typographical error for $5 \times 10^8$ (or $5 \times 10^7$ as in line 100).
   - **Ambiguous Prior Parameterization and Domain for H3N2 Sampling Proportion $p$ (Line 125):** Line 125 states *"We also placed a Lognormal($\mu = 0.001, \sigma = 1.25$) prior on the substitution rate and sampling probability ($p$)."* Because sampling probability $p$ is bounded on $[0, 1]$, placing an untruncated Lognormal distribution on $p$ is improper unless truncated to $[0, 1]$, and across lines 107, 116, and 125 the notation alternates between log-scale parameters ($\mu, \sigma$) and real-scale parameters ($\text{mean}, \text{sd}$).
4. **Convergence Filtering Bias Check (130 Discarded Replicates):** Out of 1,800 simulated BEAST analyses, 130 replicates (7.2%) were discarded for failing to reach $\text{ESS} \ge 200$ after $5 \times 10^7\text{–}5 \times 10^8$ steps (line 95). Because year-level date rounding collapses viral tips to a single time point and severely distorts the posterior surface (as seen in the empirical viral year-resolution runs that had to be omitted in Figure 3), the authors should report the breakdown of the 130 non-converged replicates across date resolutions and tree priors.

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
- **Claim 1:** *Phylodynamic parameter bias from date-rounding becomes pronounced when the rounding interval approaches or exceeds the expected waiting time for a single genomic substitution, $T_s = (L \times \mu)^{-1}$, affecting rapidly evolving RNA viruses (H1N1, SARS-CoV-2) at month resolution, intermediate bacteria (S. aureus) at year resolution, and sparing slowly evolving bacteria (M. tuberculosis) up to year resolution.* `[Well Supported]` — Both the 400-outbreak simulation study (Figure 2) and the empirical re-analyses (Figures 3–4) consistently support this scaling rule.
- **Claim 2:** *Birth-death and coalescent exponential tree priors bias substitution rates in opposite directions at month resolution because the birth-death prior treats identical rounded dates as informative synchronous transmission/sampling bursts, whereas the coalescent conditions on sampling times.* `[Well Supported]` — This is a sharp, mechanistically sound insight into how tree-prior likelihood formulations interact with discretized tip dates.
- **Claim 3:** *Uniformly translating all sampling dates by a secret random constant offset preserves phylodynamic inference accuracy while protecting patient confidentiality.* `[Moderately Supported]` — Mathematically, uniform time translation leaves relative branch lengths, substitution rates, and $R_e$ trajectories invariant. However, in real-world genomic surveillance, uniform translation of an entire outbreak cluster does not prevent re-identification if an adversary matches the temporal histogram of sample counts (or known lineage emergence dates) against public case-count curves, and it prevents combining sequences across independent data providers using different secret offsets. This caveat should be noted in the Discussion.

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
- **Data & Code Availability:** Excellent. All simulation scripts, BEAST2 XML files, empirical alignments, and plotting code are publicly available in the cited repository.
- **Model & Parameter Reporting:** All simulation parameters ($\delta, R_0, R_{e1}, R_{e2}, p, T, L, \mu$) and MCMC settings are clearly tabulated in Tables 1–2 and the Methods section. Correcting the minor typos noted above ($5 \times 18^8$, $n=30$ vs. $36$, and $0.3448$) will make the manuscript's technical presentation exemplary.
