### 1. Executive Summary
This study constructs a population of 11,847 virtual human atrial cardiomyocyte models (5,663 male and 6,184 female) by varying 14 ionic conductances in the Grandi et al. ODE model and filtering against experimental action potential (AP) biomarker ranges from 201 atrial fibrillation (AF) patients. The authors then simulate the pore-block effects of 12 antiarrhythmic drugs (6 Vaughan Williams Class III and 6 non-Class III) across the 11,847 models to generate 60,599 simulated recordings, extract 14 (later 15) $\Delta\text{AP}$ and $\Delta\text{CT}$ (calcium transient) features, and train six machine learning (ML) classifiers to predict whether a simulated recording belongs to a Class III or non-Class III drug. Despite its clinically important motivation, the study is undermined by fatal methodological and mathematical flaws: (1) catastrophic data leakage from performing a random row-wise 70/30 split across the 60,599 `(virtual_cell, drug)` pairs so that all 12 deterministic drug pore-block profiles appear in both the training and test sets; (2) explicit hyperparameter optimization (`GridSearchCV`) to maximize AUC on the test set; (3) mathematically impossible confusion-matrix metrics across Tables 1–7 where Accuracy exceeds both Sensitivity and Specificity because Precision (PPV) was mislabeled as Specificity; (4) linear redundancy among the 14 features ($\text{rank} = 11$) mistaken for a PCA dimensionality-reduction improvement; and (5) verbatim copy-pasted statistical methods and hallucinated experimental descriptions.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
1. **Catastrophic Data Leakage via Random Row-Wise Splitting Across Deterministic Drug Simulations (Methods, Sections 5.4 & 5.6, Lines 80 & 90):** Only 12 drugs exist in the entire study (6 Class III: amiodarone, dofetilide, dronedarone, ibutilide, sotalol, vernakalant; 6 non-Class III: flecainide, propafenone, quinidine, ranolazine, disopyramide, digoxin). Each drug is modeled as a single deterministic conductance-scaling vector via Eq. (1) ($g_{i,\text{drug}} = g_i [1 + (D/\text{IC}_{50,i})^h]^{-1}$) applied to the 11,847 baseline models, producing 60,599 recordings. Instead of splitting by drug (e.g., leave-one-drug-out cross-validation) to test whether a classifier can identify the class of an *unseen* drug, the authors randomly split the 60,599 recordings 70%/30% (`n = 42,419` train, `18,180` test). Because every one of the 12 deterministic drug scaling vectors is represented ~3,500 times in the training set and ~1,500 times in the test set (and the exact same baseline virtual cells appear in both sets under different drugs), the ML models are not learning generalizable electrophysiological signatures of Class III drugs; they are simply memorizing which of the 12 discrete deterministic perturbation manifolds a sample came from.
2. **Direct Test-Set Leakage in Hyperparameter Tuning (Methods, Line 90):** Section 5.6 explicitly states: *"Each ML model was optimized to be suitable for this drug classification task by using the GridSearchCV algorithm to find the combination of hyperparameters that maximizes its AUC on the test data set [96]."* Tuning hyperparameters to maximize performance on the test set invalidates the reported test metrics.
3. **Mathematically Impossible Accuracy, Sensitivity, and Specificity Values in Tables 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 7:** For any binary classifier, overall Accuracy ($\text{ACC} = \frac{\text{TP}+\text{TN}}{P+N}$) is a weighted average of Sensitivity ($\text{SEN} = \frac{\text{TP}}{P}$) and Specificity ($\text{SPE} = \frac{\text{TN}}{N}$), so $\text{ACC}$ **must** lie strictly between $\text{SEN}$ and $\text{SPE}$: $\min(\text{SEN}, \text{SPE}) \le \text{ACC} \le \max(\text{SEN}, \text{SPE})$. Yet throughout the manuscript's tables, $\text{ACC}$ is strictly greater than *both* $\text{SEN}$ and $\text{SPE}$:
   - **Table 1, Male LR (Line 88):** $\text{ACC} = 0.6553$, while $\text{SEN} = 0.6063$ and $\text{SPE} = 0.6198$ ($\text{ACC} > \max(\text{SEN}, \text{SPE})$ by +3.55%).
   - **Table 1, Female LR (Line 94):** $\text{ACC} = 0.6707$, while $\text{SEN} = 0.5790$ and $\text{SPE} = 0.6225$ (+4.82% above max).
   - **Table 2, Male-on-Female LR (Line 140):** $\text{ACC} = 0.6473$, while $\text{SEN} = 0.5248$ and $\text{SPE} = 0.6018$.
   - **Table 2, Female-on-Male LR (Line 146):** $\text{ACC} = 0.6446$, while $\text{SEN} = 0.5872$ and $\text{SPE} = 0.6079$.
   - **Table 3, Male Top-3 (Line 163) & Female Top-3/Top-5 (Lines 169–170):** $\text{ACC} = 0.7519 > \max(0.6406, 0.7424)$; $\text{ACC} = 0.7299 > \max(0.6576, 0.7129)$; $\text{ACC} = 0.7843 > \max(0.7116, 0.7834)$.
   - **Table 4 (Line 192) & Table 7 (Lines 265, 271):** Same mathematical impossibility.
   *Forensic root cause:* Checking the reported $\text{F1}$ column across every row of Tables 1–7 reveals that $\text{F1} = \frac{2 \times \text{SEN} \times \text{SPE}}{\text{SEN} + \text{SPE}}$ (e.g., Table 1 Male SVM: $\frac{2(0.7635)(0.9454)}{0.7635 + 0.9454} = 0.8448 \approx 0.8450$; Male NB: $\frac{2(0.9346)(0.5166)}{0.9346 + 0.5166} = 0.6654 \approx 0.6652$). Because $\text{F1}$ in `scikit-learn` is the harmonic mean of **Recall (SEN)** and **Precision (PPV)**, the authors' evaluation script extracted **Precision** ($\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP}+\text{FP}}$) and mislabeled it as **Specificity** ($\frac{\text{TN}}{\text{TN}+\text{FP}}$) across every table and text claim in the paper!
4. **Exact Linear Dependence Among Features Mistaken for PCA Dimensionality Reduction (Results, Line 183; Table 4):** Among the 14 input features, three are exact linear combinations of the other 11:
   - $\Delta\text{APD}_{\text{tri}} = \Delta\text{APD}_{90} - \Delta\text{APD}_{50}$
   - $\Delta\text{CTD}_{\text{tri}} = \Delta\text{CTD}_{90} - \Delta\text{CTD}_{50}$
   - $\Delta\text{CTA} = \Delta\text{CT}_{\text{max}} - \Delta\text{CTD}$
   Consequently, the $14 \times 14$ feature matrix has rank at most 11, and principal components 12, 13, and 14 have zero variance. That is why in Table 4 (Lines 197–200 and 206–209), the performance metrics for 11, 12, 13, and 14 PCs are identical to four decimal places (`0.8816, 0.7625, 0.9491, 0.8456, 0.8661, 0.7633` for Male; `0.8793, 0.7680, 0.9529, 0.8505, 0.8687, 0.7636` for Female). Unaware of this algebraic collinearity, the authors claim in Line 183 that keeping 11 or 12 PCs demonstrates that *"a reduced set of features could potentially achieve better performance."*
5. **Copy-Pasted Irrelevant Statistical Methods and Hallucinated Experimental Claims (Section 3.3 & Section 5.8):**
   - Section 5.8 (Line 132) states: *"Hazard ratios and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were calculated using conditional logistic regression models... GraphPad Prism 10... was used for all analyses."* This study performs no survival/time-to-event analysis, reports no hazard ratios, and fits no conditional logistic regressions.
   - Section 3.3 (Discussion, Lines 3–5 of second text block) contains promotional prose claiming that the study integrates *"(1) high-fidelity in vitro patch-clamp measurements"*, that *"the in vitro data serve not merely for validation, but as the foundational training set for our machine learning models, creating a closed-loop system"*, and that $\Delta\text{AREA}$ *"improves classification AUC for drug-induced proarrhythmia by ~2.1%."* In reality, the retrospective human dataset from [58] used sharp intracellular microelectrodes in trabeculae (not patch-clamp), no ML model was trained on in vitro drug data, and the classifiers predicted Class III vs. non-Class III identity, not drug-induced proarrhythmia.
   - Section 5.5 (Line 85) states *"We extracted 14 biomarkers... 8 biomarkers extracted from the AP recording included RMP, dV/dtmax, APA, APD20, APD40, APD50, APD90, AREA, and APDtri... while 6 biomarkers extracted from the CT recording included CTmax, CTD50, CTD90, CTDtri and CTD"*—listing 9 AP biomarkers (including `AREA`, which is the 15th feature introduced in Section 2.6) and only 5 CT biomarkers (omitting `CTA`).

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
- **Claim 1:** *Sex-specific ML classifiers significantly outperform non-sex-specific classifiers in predicting drug efficacy of Class III AADs.* `[Methodologically Flawed]` — First, the classifiers do not predict clinical or physiological *drug efficacy* in terminating AF; they classify whether the applied pore-block parameters came from the list of 6 Vaughan Williams Class III drugs or the 6 non-Class III drugs (which includes digoxin, a cardiac glycoside, alongside Class Ia/Ic sodium channel blockers). Second, because of the row-wise train/test leakage across the 12 drugs, the performance metrics do not measure generalization to unseen compounds.
- **Claim 2:** *Gender differences in drug responses are explained by lower $I_{K1}$, $I_{Na}$, and $I_{to}$ in females.* `[Fragile]` — The Grandi baseline model does not have intrinsic sex parameters; "male" and "female" virtual populations were selected by rejection sampling from the same uniform $[-100\%, +200\%]$ Latin hypercube solely by whether simulated APs fell within the min–max bounding boxes of male ($n=126$) vs. female ($n=75$) trabeculae recordings from [58]. Because the experimental female cohort in [58] had a slightly less negative RMP bound and smaller APA bound, rejection sampling mechanically retained models with lower $G_{K1}$ and $G_{Na}$. This is an inverse-problem consequence of the bounding-box filter on a single reference model rather than direct experimental measurement of female human atrial ion channel densities.
