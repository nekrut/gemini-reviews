### 1. Executive Summary
This manuscript introduces `HMFGraph`, a fast Bayesian Gaussian Graphical Model (GGM) framework employing a scaled hierarchical matrix-F prior (modeled as a scale mixture of Wishart distributions with a diagonal Gamma hyperprior on the target matrix $B$). The authors reparameterize the full conditional modes in terms of intuitive shrinkage weights $\alpha, \beta \in [0, 1]$, estimate the precision matrix $\Omega$ via a fast Iterative Conditional Modes (ICM) / Generalized Expectation-Maximization (GEM) algorithm, tune $\alpha$ by bounding the condition number of $\hat{\Omega}_\alpha$ against the Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage estimator (CC-method), and perform edge selection using normal-approximated credible intervals calibrated via permutation-based FDR control or $\hat{F}_1$-score maximization. The method is thoroughly benchmarked on simulated scale-free and cluster networks ($p = 100$, $n \in \{35, 75, 150, 300\}$) across two simulation engines (`huge` and `BDgraph`) against seven competing frequentist and Bayesian GGM methods, and illustrated on two biological datasets (*Bacillus subtilis* riboflavin gene expression, $n = 71, p = 101$; American Gut microbiome OTUs, $n = 296, p = 138$). The computational speedup over MCMC samplers and the consistent empirical performance across both `huge` and `BDgraph` benchmarks are clear strengths. Several statistical and algorithmic details warrant clarification in a minor revision.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
1. **Distinction Between Iterative Conditional Modes (Joint MAP) and Marginal EM/GEM (Section 2.4.1 & Algorithm 1):** The authors describe Algorithm 1 as a GEM algorithm where "we operate in a complete data situation, thus the E-step becomes an identity operation" and each step maximizes the full conditional distributions $p(B \mid \Phi, \Omega, Y)$, $p(\Phi \mid B, \Omega, Y)$, and $p(\Omega \mid \Phi, B, Y)$. Strictly speaking, coordinate-wise maximization of full conditional distributions (Iterative Conditional Modes, Besag 1986) ascends the *joint* posterior density $p(\Omega, \Phi, B \mid Y)$ rather than the *marginal* posterior density $p(\Omega \mid Y) = \iint p(\Omega, \Phi, B \mid Y)\,d\Phi\,dB$ (where a true EM/GEM algorithm treats $\Phi$ and $B$ as latent variables integrated out in the E-step). Because Wishart distributions carry nonlinear volume Jacobians under matrix inversion, the coordinate mode of the joint posterior $p(\Omega, \Phi, B \mid Y)$ does not coincide with the marginal MAP of $\Omega$ under the matrix-F prior. Clarifying that Algorithm 1 computes the joint coordinate mode (ICM) rather than the marginal MAP will improve theoretical precision.
2. **Simplification and Ground-Truth Edge Density Bias in $\hat{F}_1$ Maximization (Section 2.9, Equation 28):** Equation 28 defines $\hat{F}_1 = \frac{2\widehat{TP}}{2\widehat{TP} + \widehat{FP} + \widehat{FN}}$ with $\widehat{FN} = K - \widehat{TP}$ and $K = p$. Because $\widehat{TP} + \widehat{FP} = \hat{E}(\gamma)$ (the total number of selected edges at threshold $\gamma$) and $\widehat{TP} + \widehat{FN} = K = p$, the denominator algebraically simplifies to $\hat{E}(\gamma) + p$, so that:
   $$\hat{F}_1(\gamma) = \frac{2(\hat{E}(\gamma) - \widehat{FP}(\gamma))}{\hat{E}(\gamma) + p}$$
   Notably, in the scale-free benchmarks generated by `huge` and `BDgraph`, a spanning tree on $p = 100$ nodes has exactly $p - 1 = 99$ true edges, meaning the heuristic $K = p = 100$ is virtually an oracle match for the true edge count in the scale-free simulations. The authors should note this algebraic simplification and discuss how sensitive $\hat{F}_1$ threshold selection is when the true graph is much denser ($K \gg p$) or sparser ($K \ll p$).
3. **Within-Sample vs. Within-Variable Permutation Scheme for Null Edge Estimation (Section 2.8, Line 387):** Line 387 states that permuted data are obtained "by permuting variable values within each sample separately, assuming the variables have a similar scale." Permuting across variables *within each sample* (row-wise permutation) conflates differences in marginal variable distributions/scales with correlation structure and induces a weak negative equicorrelation ($-1/(p-1)$) within each row. Standard null permutation for covariance/GGM estimation permutes sample indices independently *within each variable* (column-wise permutation), which preserves every variable's exact marginal distribution regardless of scale while strictly breaking all inter-variable dependencies. The authors should clarify whether column-wise permutation is supported or preferable when variables have heterogeneous scales.
4. **Conditional vs. Marginal Posterior Variance in the Credible Interval Approximation (Section 2.7, Equation 22):** The variance formula $\widehat{\text{Var}}(\hat{\omega}_{ij}) = (\nu + n)(\hat{w}_{ij}^2 + \hat{w}_{ii}\hat{w}_{jj})$ is the conditional Wishart variance of $\Omega \mid \Phi = \hat{\Phi}, Y$, which treats the auxiliary matrices $\hat{\Phi}$ and $\hat{B}$ as fixed constants and omits the second term of the law of total variance, $\text{Var}_{\Phi \mid Y}(\mathbb{E}[\omega_{ij} \mid \Phi, Y])$. While Figure 3 shows that the conditional normal approximation closely tracks the Gibbs sampler at $\alpha = 0.80, \beta = 0.90$ and the subsequent permutation step calibrates $\gamma$ empirically, this distinction between conditional and marginal posterior variance should be explicitly acknowledged.
5. **Compositional Nature of Microbiome Data in Section 3.2.2:** For the American Gut OTU dataset ($p = 138, n = 296$), applying a marginal nonparanormal (rank-based Gaussian) transformation to raw or relative OTU counts does not remove compositional unit-sum constraints (which methods like `SpiecEasi` address via centered log-ratio transformation). While Section 4 briefly notes zero-inflation and compositionality as limitations, noting the absence of CLR transformation in the microbiome example would provide balanced context.

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
- **Claim 1: The ICM/GEM algorithm achieves major computational speedups (10–100$\times$ without permutation; 3–10$\times$ with parallelized permutation and $\alpha$-tuning) over MCMC-based Bayesian GGM samplers while maintaining competitive or superior network recovery.** `[Well Supported]` — Thoroughly demonstrated across $p \in \{100, 200, 300, 400\}$ in Table 2 and across 400 total simulated datasets per generator in Tables 3 and 4.
- **Claim 2: Larger values of $\alpha$ increase network clustering (ACC and NMI) at the expense of edge-level $F_1$ accuracy.** `[Well Supported]` — Consistently demonstrated in both the cluster simulations (Table 4) and the American Gut taxonomic clustering analysis (Figure 7A vs. 7B).
- **Claim 3: In the *B. subtilis* riboflavin dataset, `YXLD_at` is only indirectly associated with riboflavin production via its partial correlation with `YXLE_at`.** `[Moderately Supported]` — Plausible conditional independence hypothesis generated by the GGM (since `YXLD` and `YXLE` reside in the same operon), though observational and dependent on restricting the graph to the top 100 highest-variance genes ($p = 101$).

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
- **Code & Package Availability:** Exemplary reproducibility. The method is packaged in an open-source R package (`HMFGraph` on GitHub), and all simulation, benchmarking, and real-data analysis scripts are provided in a dedicated reproducibility repository.
- **Data Availability:** Both biological datasets are directly accessible via standard CRAN/GitHub R packages (`riboflavin` in `hdi`; `amgut2.filt.phy` in `SpiecEasi`), and all simulation parameters and random generation procedures (`huge` and `BDgraph`) are fully specified.
