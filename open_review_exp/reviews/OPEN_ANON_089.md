### 1. Executive Summary
This population genomics study investigates the geographic scale of local adaptation and the modes of convergent selective sweeps across five sympatric population pairs of traditionally cultivated open-pollinated maize (*Zea mays* ssp. *mays*) and its wild progenitor teosinte (*Zea mays* ssp. *parviglumis*), plus one additional teosinte population (11 populations total; $n = 10$ individuals per population, with Palmar Chico deeply sampled at $n = 55$ maize and $n = 50$ teosinte to construct non-overlapping replicate subsamples; 195 total individuals sequenced at 20–25$\times$ whole-genome coverage, plus 60$\times$ *Zea diploperennis* as an outgroup). Combining genotype-likelihood population genetics (`ANGSD`, `Treemix` $F_4$ tests, `NGSadmix`, `ngsRelate`), unfolded SFS demographic inference (`mushi`), homozygosity-by-descent validation (`IBDseq` vs. `msprime` coalescent simulations), hierarchical Bayesian asymptotic McDonald-Kreitman estimation of $\alpha$ (`brms`), demographic-calibrated selective sweep detection (`RAiSD` + `mop` + cubic spline smoothing), and composite-likelihood inference of convergent adaptation modes (`rdmc`), the authors demonstrate that: (i) rangewide estimates of $\alpha$ are commensurate with individual populations; (ii) teosinte has a significantly higher fraction of private sweeps than maize (22% vs. 14%, $p = 0.0026$); (iii) sympatric maize–teosinte pairs do not share more sweeps than expected by chance; and (iv) shared sweeps frequently arise via migration and standing variation across subspecies. The study is methodologically rigorous, self-critical (notably benchmarking sweep precision using both empirical Palmar Chico subsamples and `discoal` simulations of hard, soft, and incomplete sweeps), and well-grounded in evolutionary theory, with a few minor mathematical and editorial points to address.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
1. **Definition of $P$ and Hypergeometric Scaling for Sweep Inference Accuracy (Materials and Methods, Lines 108–122):** In Section "Assessing the accuracy of sweep inferences and the number of shared sweeps expected by chance," line 108 defines:
   $$P = 1 - \frac{n_S / n_{P1} + n_S / n_{P2}}{2}$$
   Because $n_S / n_{P1}$ and $n_S / n_{P2}$ are the proportions of sweeps shared between the two Palmar Chico replicates ($0.67$ for maize and $0.80$ for teosinte, Results line 82), $P = 1 - \text{shared proportion}$ equals the **false-positive/non-shared rate** ($0.33$ for maize and $0.20$ for teosinte, as stated in line 112). However, line 122 then states: *"We accounted for sweep inference accuracy by multiplying the raw values of $N$, $n_1$, $n_2$, and $x$ by the value of $P$ described above."* First, if the goal is to scale down the called sweeps ($n_1, n_2$) to the estimated number of *true* sweeps, the multiplier for $n_1$ and $n_2$ should be the precision $1 - P$ (i.e., $\approx 0.735$), not the false-positive rate $P$ ($\approx 0.265$). Second, multiplying all four parameters ($N, n_1, n_2, x$) of a hypergeometric distribution by the same constant factor leaves the expected overlap ratio $E[X]/x = (n_1 n_2 / N) / x$ completely unchanged and simply reduces the effective sample size (widening the distribution and increasing p-values uniformly), rather than modeling how independent false-positive calls in two populations affect observed overlap $x$. Clarifying the exact algebraic justification for scaling all four parameters by $P$ vs. $1-P$ would strengthen this section.
2. **In-Sample Hyperparameter Tuning on Palmar Chico Replicates (Line 99):** The RAiSD window size (24 SNPs), outlier quantile (99.9th percentile), and merge distance (50 kb) were selected via grid search specifically to maximize the proportion of shared sweeps between the two Palmar Chico $n = 10$ subsamples. Because Palmar Chico had the weakest demographic bottleneck and lowest inbreeding among teosinte populations (Fig 2C–E) and served as the optimization target for the grid search, applying the Palmar Chico precision estimate ($0.80$ for teosinte) to strongly bottlenecked teosinte populations (such as San Lorenzo and El Rodeo) may underestimate false-positive sweep calls in those populations.
3. **Sample Size of Rangewide SFS ($n = 5$ Maize, $n = 6$ Teosinte) in `mushi` and Asymptotic MK:** Rangewide samples were constructed by picking 1 diploid individual per population ($n = 5$ maize = 10 chromosomes; $n = 6$ teosinte = 12 chromosomes). In an unfolded SFS with only 9 or 11 polymorphic frequency bins, fitting the 3-parameter asymptotic MK curve $\alpha(x) = a + b e^{-cx}$ has sparse information in the high-frequency derived bins ($x \to 1$) that anchor the asymptote, even when regularized by the `brms` hierarchical random-effects structure across populations. Noting the smaller number of frequency bins in the rangewide SFS when comparing rangewide vs. population-level ($n = 10$ diploids = 19 bins) $\alpha$ credible intervals (Fig 3) would be helpful.
4. **Boundary Pileup in `rdmc` Parameter Grids and Fixed $N_e = 50,000$ (Fig 5E–F, Lines 129–138):** In `rdmc`, effective population size was fixed at $N_e = 50,000$ across all populations despite substantial demographic variation (Fig 2C). Furthermore, in Figure 5F, the inferred migration rate for nearly all maize-only and cross-subspecies sweeps hits the upper boundary of the tested grid (`migs` max $= 10^{-1}$), while all teosinte-only migration sweeps hit the lower boundary (`migs` min $= 10^{-3}$). Similarly, standing variation persistence times (`times = c(1e2, 1e3, 1e4, 1e5)`) only evaluated four order-of-magnitude points (producing the apparent "bimodal" jump between $10^2$ and $10^4$–$10^5$ generations in Fig 5E). Expanding the migration grid above $10^{-1}$ / below $10^{-3}$ and using a finer grid for `times` would verify that the bimodal standing-time distribution is not an artifact of the 4-point grid discretization.
5. **Truncated Text Blocks and Citation Substitution Glitch in Materials and Methods:** Several places in the Materials and Methods appear to have missing lines or formatting glitches that should be restored:
   - **Line 15:** *"sequenced to 60× coverage using PE250 on three lanes of Haines et al., 2000, rapid run (UC Davis Genome Center, Davis, USA)"*—where "Illumina HiSeq 2000" or "HiSeq 2500" was evidently auto-replaced by the bibliography software with the citation `Haines et al., 2000`!
   - **Line 26:** Under `ANGSD` flags, only a single bullet (`- only_proper_pairs 1`) appears, omitting the rest of the command-line filter list (e.g., `-uniqueOnly`, `-remove_bads`, `-minMapQ`, `-minQ`, `-GL`).
   - **Line 58:** Ends mid-sentence with *"along with an SNP p-value cutoff and maf filter of:"* followed by blank space.
   - **Line 90:** The `brms` formula ends with a trailing `+` sign (`(1+allele_frequency | nuc_type)+`).

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
- **Claim 1: Rangewide estimates of the proportion of nonsynonymous fixations driven by positive selection ($\alpha \approx 0.10\text{–}0.28$) are commensurate with individual local populations in both maize and teosinte.** `[Well Supported]` The hierarchical Bayesian asymptotic MK analysis across populations and mutation spectra (WS, SW, SS_WW) provides clear, well-quantified support that population structure does not collapse rangewide $\alpha$ to zero in *Zea mays*.
- **Claim 2: Selective sweeps are more frequently private in wild teosinte (22%) than in domesticated maize (14%), and sympatric maize–teosinte pairs do not share more sweeps than allopatric pairs.** `[Well Supported]` The contrast between subspecies is statistically robust ($p = 0.0026$) and carefully contextualized against empirical and simulated false-negative rates for hard, soft, and incomplete sweeps.
- **Claim 3: Convergent adaptation between maize and teosinte occurs predominantly via migration and standing variation rather than independent de novo mutations, with widely traded maize varieties (e.g., Crucero Lagunitas) acting as hubs for allele movement.** `[Well Supported]` The concordance between genome-wide $F_4$ introgression statistics (Fig 1E, Appendix 2) and `rdmc` composite-likelihood source-population assignments (Fig 5C–D) provides compelling, independent lines of evidence for human-mediated and cross-subspecies adaptive gene flow.

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness

- **Code & Workflows:** The authors provide complete open-source pipelines, custom R/Python packages (`mop`), `discoal`/`msprime` simulation repositories, and Jupyter notebooks to regenerate all figures and tables, archived with permanent DOIs. Restoring the truncated `ANGSD` filter flags in Lines 26 and 58 of the manuscript text will make the written Methods fully self-contained.
