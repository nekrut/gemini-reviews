### 1. Executive Summary
This study performs a secondary cross-sectional analysis of survey responses ($N = 17,880$; Canada $n = 11,768$, Colombia $n = 2,963$, Ireland $n = 3,149$) from the International COVID-19 Awareness and Responses Evaluation (iCARE) Study collected between April 2020 and December 2022. Using country-stratified multivariable binary logistic regression models adjusted for age, self-reported history of clinical anxiety/depression, and survey wave, the authors evaluate whether biological sex moderates the association between self-reported psychological distress or infection worry and unfavorable changes in three health behaviors (healthy eating, physical activity, and alcohol intake). They report that psychological distress is associated with unfavorable behavior changes across most outcomes, whereas a statistically significant sex $\times$ psychological distress interaction ($p < 0.01$) is detected only for healthy eating in Colombia. While the research question is straightforward and the sample size is large, the manuscript suffers from severe internal table and arithmetic errors, a conflation of biological sex and gender in the survey instrument that undermines its central biological premise, a questionable outcome dichotomization that pools opposite behavioral shifts ("increased" and "no change") into a single reference category, and unaddressed sample-size imbalances across countries.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
1. **Multiple Arithmetic, Typographical, and Confidence Interval Errors in Tables 1–4:**
   - **Table 1:** For Ireland, Female "Clinical anxiety: No" is reported as `1,101 (17.3)`. However, $1,101 / 1,544 = 71.3\%$, not $17.3\%$ (a severe digit-transposition error). In the "Psychological distress" row for Canada, the sex difference $t$-statistic is printed as `−.21.6*` (malformed double decimal).
   - **Table 3 (Colombia):**
     - In the row for `Infection worry` under `Healthy eating`, the point estimate and confidence interval are reported as `1.45 (.94–1.40)`. A point estimate of $1.45$ lies *outside* its own upper confidence bound of $1.40$ (likely a typo for $1.15$).
     - In the row for `Psychological distress x sex` under `Physical activity`, the OR and CI are reported as `1.78 (.95–1.46)`. Again, the point estimate $1.78$ lies completely outside the upper bound of its confidence interval ($1.46$, likely a typo for $1.18$).
     - In the row for `Wave 3` under `Alcohol intake`, the OR and CI are reported as `1.64 (.115–2.34)*`, where `.115` is a decimal placement error for `1.15` (since $\sqrt{1.15 \times 2.34} = 1.64$, whereas a lower bound of $0.115$ would span unity and could not be statistically significant at $p < 0.01$).
   - **Inconsistent Confidence Interval Levels (99% vs. 95%) Between Text, Table Captions, and Supporting Information:** Table 2 (Canada) caption states *"Odd ratios and 99% confidence intervals shown"*, whereas Table 3 (Colombia) and Table 4 (Ireland) captions state *"Odd ratios and 95% confidence intervals shown"*, yet the Supporting Information captions (lines 253–257) state *"99% confidence intervals shown"* for all three countries. Moreover, in Table 3, `Infection worry` for Physical activity `1.21 (1.01–1.45)*` and `Psychological distress x sex` for Healthy eating `1.31 (1.03–1.67)*` are marked with `*` ($p < 0.01$), even though their 99% confidence intervals would clearly cross $1.0$ if `1.01–1.45` and `1.03–1.67` are 95% CIs ($z \approx 2.09$ and $2.18$, corresponding to $p \approx 0.037$ and $p \approx 0.029$, NOT $p < 0.01$!). Let us verify: for $\text{OR} = 1.31$ with CI $(1.03, 1.67)$, $\ln(1.31) = 0.270$, $\text{SE} = (\ln(1.67) - \ln(1.03))/(2 \times 1.96) = 0.483 / 3.92 = 0.123$, yielding $z = 0.270 / 0.123 = 2.19$, $p = 0.028 > 0.01$ under a 95% CI, OR if $(1.03, 1.67)$ is actually a 99% CI labeled erroneously as 95% CI in the Table 3 footnote, then the footnote is wrong! In fact, comparing the widths shows that Table 3 and Table 4 footnotes say "95% confidence intervals" while the Supporting Info says "99% confidence intervals."
2. **Confounding Outcome Dichotomization ("Favorable or No Changes" Pooled Together):** In the Methods (line 59), the authors collapse 5-point self-reported change items into binary outcomes where "unfavorable changes" (e.g., increased alcohol intake) are contrasted against "favorable or no changes" (e.g., *decreased* alcohol intake pooled with *doing this as much as before*). However, in line 59, they define "favorable or no changes" as *"(i.e., increased physical activity, increased healthy eating or decreased alcohol intake)"* while omitting "as much as before" in the parenthetical, whereas Table 1 labels the row *"Increase/no change"* even for **Alcohol**! Look at Table 1 rows 101–103: for Alcohol, the rows are labeled `Increase/no change` (`60.0%` male, `51.3%` female in Canada) and `Decrease` (`17.6%` male, `16.6%` female). That means in Table 1, "no change" in alcohol was grouped with "Increase", or the row labels in Table 1 are inverted relative to the Methods text! Furthermore, pooling individuals who *increased* physical activity or *decreased* alcohol consumption during lockdowns (an active behavioral change) with individuals who experienced *no change* distorts the reference baseline, especially since proportional odds violation could have been handled naturally via multinomial logistic regression (Unfavorable vs. No Change vs. Favorable).
3. **Cross-Country Statistical Power Asymmetry and Unpooled Country Interactions:** Canada ($n = 11,768$) has nearly four times the sample size of Colombia ($n = 2,963$) and Ireland ($n = 3,149$). Furthermore, the survey waves were collected at completely different phases of the pandemic across the three countries (Waves 1–3 for Colombia, Waves 2–5 for Ireland, Waves 6–9 for Canada), without adjusting for calendar date or lockdown stringency index. Testing interactions separately within each country rather than fitting a pooled model with a three-way `country × sex × distress` interaction does not formally test whether the sex moderation slopes differ significantly across countries.
4. **Conflation of Main Effects and Conditional Effects in Models with Interaction Terms:** In Table 2 (Canada Healthy Eating), the model includes the interaction term `Psychological distress x sex` ($\text{OR} = 0.91$). Yet in the text (line 141), the authors interpret the coefficient of `Sex` ($\text{OR} = 1.39$) as an unconditional main effect (*"Compared to males, females decreased their odds of healthy eating by 39%"*). In a regression model containing an uncentered `Psychological distress x sex` interaction term, the coefficient on `Sex` represents the conditional effect of sex only when `Psychological distress = 0`, not the marginal/independent main effect across the sample. (Additionally, an OR of $1.39$ for the outcome "reduced healthy eating" means females had $39\%$ *higher odds of reporting a decrease in healthy eating*, not that they "decreased their odds of healthy eating by 39%").
5. **Misreporting of Cronbach's Alpha Threshold:** Line 55 states: *"An average of the four items was used for the analyses in Canada ($\alpha = .86$), Columbia ($\alpha = .81$) and Ireland ($\alpha = .87$) as all summary variables displayed moderate ($<.80$) internal consistency."* Values of $0.81\text{--}0.87$ are $> 0.80$ (good/high consistency), not $< 0.80$.
6. **Consistent Misspelling of "Colombia" as "Columbia":** Throughout the title, abstract, main text, and tables (except two cells in Table 1), the country Colombia is misspelled as "Columbia".

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
- **Claim 1:** Cross-country comparisons isolate biological sex mechanisms from sociocultural gender mechanisms. `[Methodologically Flawed]` — As the authors themselves admit in the Limitations (line 239), the survey asked participants to "identify their sex" with a single item that could easily be interpreted as gender identity, and no biological variables (e.g., hormonal status, reproductive stage) or separate gender-identity/gender-role scales were included. Observing whether a self-reported binary sex/gender label moderates self-reported stress coping across three countries collected during different pandemic waves cannot mechanistically disentangle biological sex from social gender norms.
- **Claim 2:** Increased psychological distress is consistently associated with unfavorable changes in health behaviors across Canada, Colombia, and Ireland. `[Moderately Supported]` — The cross-sectional association between self-reported distress and self-reported behavior deterioration is statistically robust in Canada and Ireland and for alcohol in Colombia, though subject to same-source retrospective recall bias and reverse causality (since both exposure and outcome were self-assessed simultaneously in a single cross-sectional survey).
- **Claim 3:** Sex significantly moderates the association between psychological distress and healthy eating only in Colombia. `[Fragile]` — While the interaction term reaches statistical significance in the Colombia model, the pseudo-$R^2$ of that model is only $0.02$, the survey waves in Colombia (Waves 1–3) do not overlap temporally with Canada (Waves 6–9), and no formal cross-country interaction test was performed.

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
- **Survey Instrument:** Links to the OSF repository (`https://osf.io/nswcm`) and iCARE portal (`www.icarestudy.com`) are provided, along with a CHERRIES checklist in S1 Table.
- **Data & Code Availability:** Individual-level data are not publicly deposited and require an application to the MBMC iCARE committee. Stata analysis scripts (`.do` files) used to recode variables and fit the models are not provided in the Supporting Information, which makes verifying the Table 1 alcohol coding discrepancy and Table 3 confidence interval typos impossible without raw outputs.
