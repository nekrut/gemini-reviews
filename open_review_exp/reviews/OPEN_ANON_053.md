### 1. Executive Summary
This qualitative study investigates experiences of intersectional stigma (related to HIV status and sexual minority identity), coping strategies, and perceived impacts on HIV care engagement among sexual minority men (SMM) living with HIV in Lagos, Nigeria. Between January and February 2022, the authors conducted 68 semi-structured individual interviews comprising 30 SMM living with HIV and 38 healthcare providers (HCPs) serving SMM at a partner community-based organization (CBO) and allied clinics. Using thematic analysis in NVivo 14, the study reports that sexual minority stigma was perceived as more pervasive in general Nigerian society than HIV stigma (whereas HIV stigma was more salient within the SMM community), documents adaptive and maladaptive coping mechanisms (self-acceptance, peer support, substance use, concealment, social isolation), and highlights a striking divergence: most HCPs believed stigma negatively impacted HIV care engagement, whereas most SMM reported that stigma had minimal or even motivating effects on their adherence.

While the study addresses an important and under-researched public health population in a criminalized environment, the manuscript exhibits major methodological and reporting deficiencies: (1) despite stating in the Methods that demographic data were collected from all participants, the manuscript contains *zero* demographic or clinical summary data (no tables, no age ranges, no ART/viral load metrics for SMM, and no professional role breakdown for HCPs); (2) recruitment exclusively through an LGBTQ-affirming CBO introduces profound survivorship/selection bias that directly explains the paradoxical finding on care engagement; and (3) there are contradictions between the theoretical framing in the Introduction and the purely inductive coding described in the Methods.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
1. **Complete Omission of Participant Demographic and Clinical Characteristics (Line 48 vs. Results):** In the Methods (Line 48), the authors state: *"We also collected demographic data from all participants."* Furthermore, inclusion criteria required SMM to have a *"lab-confirmed HIV positive diagnosis"* (Line 43) and HCPs to have *"current professional certification, and at least 6 months' experience providing services to SMM"* (Line 43). Yet the Results section contains **no table and no text summary** of participant demographics for either the 30 SMM (e.g., age mean/range, sexual identity self-classification [gay, bisexual, etc.], educational attainment, socioeconomic status, time since HIV diagnosis, duration on ART) or the 38 HCPs (e.g., how many were physicians, nurses, pharmacists, adherence counselors, or peer outreach workers, and what types of facilities they worked in). Without these basic sample descriptors, readers cannot contextualize the qualitative quotes or evaluate sample diversity.
2. **Survivorship and Selection Bias in Clinic-Based CBO Recruitment (Lines 43, 208–225, 253):** All 30 SMM participants were recruited during programming events at a dedicated community-based organization (CBO) that provides affirming sexual health and HIV services to SMM in Lagos. As noted in the Introduction (Line 31), only 31% of SMM living with HIV in Nigeria have initiated ART, but among those who initiate ART, 80% achieve viral suppression [8]. By sampling exclusively from the ~31% minority who have already overcome structural and anticipated stigma to link to and attend programming at a specialized SMM-friendly CBO, the study excludes out-of-care SMM and those attending hostile general public hospitals (indeed, an HCP quote on Line 102 describes a client who dropped out of a general hospital due to enacted stigma before coming to the CBO). This survivorship bias directly explains why most sampled SMM reported that stigma had "minimal to no effect" on their care engagement, whereas HCPs—who witness linkage failures, missed appointments, and loss to follow-up across their broader caseload—reported that stigma impedes care. The Limitations section must explicitly analyze this selection bias (and fix the garbled sentence on Line 253: *"thereby limiting the generalizability of sparticipants to underreport experiences of stigma"*).
3. **Methodological Contradiction Between Theoretical Framework and Inductive Coding (Lines 35 vs. 53):** In the Introduction (Line 35), the authors state that the study was *"Guided by this framework [The Conceptual Framework for HIV-Related Stigma, Engagement in Care, and Health Outcomes, ref. 20]"*, and the interview guide explicitly probed predefined theoretical domains (*"enacted, structural, community, anticipated, and intersectional"* stigma, Line 48). However, in the Data Analysis section (Line 53), the authors claim: *"The analysis was inductive (data-driven), with codes and themes generated from the data, rather than deductive (or driven by a theoretical framework)."* Yet the Results section is organized directly around the predefined deductive categories from the interview guide and framework (Anticipated stigma, Enacted stigma, Community stigma, Intersectional stigma, Coping strategies, Impact on engagement in HIV care). The analysis is clearly a hybrid deductive-inductive framework analysis and should be accurately described as such.
4. **Qualitative Coding Rigor and Interrater Reliability Reporting (Line 53):** A single team member coded 100% of the 68 transcripts, while a second team member double-coded 15% ($n \approx 10$ transcripts), yielding an "interrater reliability" of 88%. The authors do not specify whether 88% represents simple percent agreement or chance-corrected Cohen's $\kappa$, how coding discrepancies were resolved, whether thematic saturation was assessed (and at what sample size saturation was reached for SMM vs. HCPs), or whether SMM and HCP transcripts were coded using separate or unified codebooks.
5. **Unattributed Quotes and Lack of Participant Identifiers (Lines 65–234):** Throughout the Results section, block quotes are presented without anonymized participant IDs (despite Line 48 stating *"We assigned each participant a unique four-digit identifier"*) or basic descriptors (e.g., `[SMM, ID-1024, age 26]` or `[HCP, ID-2011, nurse]`). Consequently, it is impossible to verify whether the ~30 block quotes in the Results are drawn broadly across the 68 participants or repeatedly from a small handful of articulate respondents.

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
- **Claim 1:** *SMM living with HIV in Lagos experience both HIV and sexual minority stigma, with sexual minority stigma perceived as more pervasive in the general society and HIV stigma more salient within the SMM community.* `[Well Supported]` — Supported by convergent qualitative accounts from both SMM and HCPs, and well-grounded in the legal and sociopolitical context of Nigeria (Same-Sex Marriage Prohibition Act) vs. public health HIV sensitization campaigns.
- **Claim 2:** *SMM living with HIV utilize both maladaptive (substance use, concealment, social isolation) and adaptive (self-acceptance, peer support, hobbies) coping strategies.* `[Well Supported]` — Directly evidenced by detailed first-person narratives and corroborated by provider observations.
- **Claim 3:** *Stigma has minimal to no effect (or a positive motivating effect) on engagement in HIV care for most SMM, contrasting with HCP perceptions.* `[Fragile]` — While accurately reflecting what the 30 interviewed SMM stated, presenting this at face value without accounting for CBO selection bias and without objective clinical adherence/viral load verification overstates resilience to stigma across the broader population of Nigerian SMM living with HIV.

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
- **Missing Instruments and Codebook:** Neither the semi-structured interview guides (for SMM and HCPs) nor the NVivo thematic codebook (hierarchical parent/child nodes and definitions) are included in the manuscript or Supporting Information.
- **Missing Summary Tables:** As noted above, demographic and clinical summary tables for the $n = 30$ SMM and $n = 38$ HCP cohorts must be added to the manuscript.
- **Data Availability:** Because qualitative interview transcripts from a criminalized key population contain sensitive personal narratives, restricted access via institutional/non-profit request (Line 261) is ethically appropriate, provided the summary tables and coding instruments are published within the paper.
