### 1. Executive Summary
This cross-sectional study investigates associations between two candidate SNPs—*TAS1R3* rs307355 and *TRPV1* rs4790522 (with a third assayed SNP, rs8065080, excluded as monomorphic)—and aqueous sucrose taste detection thresholds, preferred sucrose concentrations, and cardiometabolic traits among $N = 89$ adults (42 non-diabetic hospital workers/students and 47 patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus [T2DM]) at Livingstone University Teaching Hospital in Zambia. While expanding genetic and sensory phenotype data in Sub-Saharan African populations is a laudable goal, the manuscript suffers from severe data-table corruption (verbatim copy-pasted means and standard deviations across distinct genotype columns, mathematically impossible sex percentages, swapped genotype counts, and contradictory sex distributions for the T2DM cohort across tables), extreme age and recruitment confounding between cases and controls (55.9 vs. 28.5 years), severe underpowering ($N = 42$ and $N = 47$) for candidate-gene interaction testing, and contradictory definitions of the "risk" versus "wildtype" allele.

### 2. Methodological & Statistical Audit
1. **Severe Internal Data Corruption and Copy-Paste Errors in Tables 1, 2, and 3:**
   - **Contradictory Sex Distribution for the T2DM Cohort (Table 2 vs. Table 3):** In Table 2 (lines 153 and 163), the T2DM cohort ($n = 47$) is reported as **68% female** (matching the genotype breakdown: TT $12/16 = 75\%$, CT $12/16 = 75\%$, CC $8/15 = 53.3\%$, total $32/47 = 68.1\%$). However, in Table 3 (line 196), the exact same T2DM cohort ($n = 47$) is reported as **48% female** with $p = 0.064$ versus Healthy (`50%` female, $21/42$)! Note that a comparison of $21/42$ (50.0%) vs. $22.5/47$ (48%) would yield $p > 0.85$, whereas $21/42$ (50.0%) vs. $32/47$ (68.1%) yields $\chi^2 p = 0.082$ (one-sided Fisher $p = 0.064$), proving that `48%` in Table 3 is erroneous.
   - **Mathematically Impossible Sex Percentages in Combined Genotype Columns (Tables 1 and 2):** In Table 1 (line 110, Healthy *TRPV1*), `Sex (%F)` is `50%` in `CC` ($n = 12$) and `50%` in `AC` ($n = 20$), yet in the combined `*AC/CC` group ($n = 32$) it is reported as **19%** (it must be $50\%$)! Similarly, in Table 2 (line 163, T2DM *TRPV1*), `Sex (%F)` is `83%` in `CC` ($n = 12$) and `62%` in `AC` ($n = 21$), yet in the combined `*AC/CC` group it is reported as **30%** (the weighted average must be $70\%$)!
   - **Verbatim Copy-Pasted Means and Standard Deviations Across Distinct Genotype Subgroups (Tables 1 and 2):**
     - In Table 1 (lines 111–114, *TRPV1*), every single mean and SD in the `*AC/CC` column ($n = 32$: BMI `24.4 ± 4.9`, SBP `114.4 ± 14.0`, DBP `70.1 ± 10.1`, Pulse `72.3 ± 12.8`) is a verbatim copy-paste of the `Total` column ($n = 41$), and the SDs of the `+ AA/AC` column ($n = 29$: `± 4.9`, `± 14.0`, `± 10.1`, `± 12.8`) are also verbatim copy-pasted from the `Total` column. Likewise, under *TAS1R3* (lines 103–106), the SDs of `*TT/CT` ($n = 29$) and `+ CT/CC` ($n = 33$) are identical across all four traits (`± 5.5`, `± 12.8`, `± 9.0`, `± 12.0`).
     - In Table 2 (lines 164–168, *TRPV1*), the entire `*AC/CC` column (`28.0 ± 5.5`, `135.0 ± 22.1`, `80.3 ± 12.2`, `85.9 ± 12.7`, `9.4 ± 4.7`) is again a verbatim copy-paste of the `Total` ($n = 47$) column, and all five SDs in `+ AA/AC` are identical to the `Total` column. Furthermore, for `TRPV1 CC` ($n = 12$, line 164), BMI is reported as `26.9 ± 0.0` (an SD of zero across 12 individuals).
   - **Swapped Sample Sizes and Misplaced Numbers (Tables 1 and 2):** In Table 2 (line 161, *TRPV1*), `CC = 12`, `AC = 21`, and `AA = 14`. Thus, `AC/CC` should be $21 + 12 = 33$ and `AA/AC` should be $14 + 21 = 35$, but Table 2 reports `*AC/CC = 35` and `+ AA/AC = 33`. In Table 1 (line 108), the number `42` is printed inside the `p-value` column of the `No.` row. Additionally, Healthy participant age is reported as `28.5 ± 13.3` (median ± IQR) in line 91 and Table 3, but as `31.2 ± 9.9` (mean ± SD) in Table 1 without labeling the parametric vs. non-parametric switch.
2. **Contradictory Definition of "Risk" vs. "Wildtype" Alleles and Subgroup Count Mismatch (Line 180):** In Methods (line 72) and Tables 1–2, *TAS1R3* rs307355 `TT` is explicitly defined as **wildtype** (`TT (wildtype)`) and *TRPV1* rs4790522 `CC` is defined as **wildtype** (`CC (wildtype)`), and the Figure 3 and Figure 5 captions state: *"Combined Risk; homozygous minor allele and homozygous minor/heterozygous groups, Combined No Risk; homozygous wildtype and homozygous wildtype/heterozygous groups."* Yet in the Abstract (line 21), Results (line 180), and Discussion (line 213), the authors refer to `TT/AA` as *"one or both homozygous risk alleles (77.8%; n = 21; TT/ AA), when compared to those who with a low preferred concentration (35.3%; n = 13 p = 0.022; Fig 5)."* Not only does calling `TT` a homozygous risk allele contradict its definition as the wildtype allele, but the reported subgroup counts ($n = 21$ represent $77.8\%$ implies a denominator of $21 / 0.778 = 27$, and $n = 13$ representing $35.3\%$ implies a denominator of $13 / 0.353 = 37$, summing to $64 > 47$ total T2DM participants!) do not add up to the cohort size of 47.
3. **Severe Recruitment and Age Confounding Between Cohorts:** The 42 healthy controls were recruited from young hospital health workers and students (median age 28.5 years), whereas the 47 T2DM cases were recruited from hospital clinics (median age 55.9 years, $p < 0.001$). Because sweet taste thresholds, blood pressure, and BMI change markedly over a 27-year age gap, unadjusted Mann–Whitney/Chi-square comparisons between cohorts (Table 3, Figure 6) cannot attribute differences in taste threshold, preference, or blood pressure to T2DM status rather than age and hospital-staff selection bias.
4. **Underpowered Sample Size and Uncorrected Multiple Testing:** With $n = 42$ and $n = 47$ (yielding cell counts as small as $n = 9$ for homozygous genotypes), the study lacks statistical power to detect realistic genetic effect sizes for single SNPs, let alone two-locus genotype combinations. Dozens of hypothesis tests were conducted across additive, dominant, recessive, and combined models for 6–7 traits in two cohorts without any multiple-testing correction, rendering the single nominal $p = 0.022$ finding highly likely to be a Type I error.

### 3. Biological / Experimental Grounding & Claim Proportionality
- **Claim 1:** *Sweet taste detection threshold positively correlates with preferred sucrose concentration in both healthy ($r = 0.53, p < 0.001$) and T2DM ($r = 0.64, p < 0.001$) participants.* `[Well Supported]` — This within-group psychophysical correlation is statistically robust, though Pearson's $r$ was applied to exponentially spaced discrete sucrose concentrations (5, 15, 30, 60, 120, 240 g/L) where Spearman's rank correlation or log-transformed concentrations would be more appropriate.
- **Claim 2:** *A higher proportion of PwT2D with elevated preferred sweet concentrations carried one or both homozygous risk alleles (77.8%, TT/AA, $p = 0.022$).* `[Methodologically Flawed]` — Undermined by contradictory allele labeling (`TT` defined as wildtype in Methods/Tables but called homozygous risk in Results), inconsistent numerator/denominator counts ($21/77.8\%$ and $13/35.3\%$ exceeding $N = 47$), and lack of multiple-testing adjustment across numerous genetic models.
- **Claim 3:** *PwT2D exhibit higher sweet taste thresholds and lower preferred sucrose concentrations than healthy controls.* `[Fragile]` — Completely confounded by the 27.4-year age difference between young student/healthcare-worker controls and older diabetic patients, as well as active dietary counseling among hospital-recruited T2DM patients.

### 4. Reproducibility & Data Completeness
- **Data Availability:** Underlying participant-level data are stated to be provided in Supporting Information S2.
- **Assay & Genotyping Details:** TaqMan SNP genotyping protocols and sucrose staircase concentrations (5–240 g/L) are clearly described (note a typo in line 68 where `rs307355` is written as `rs307335`). However, the pervasive numerical and copy-paste errors across Tables 1, 2, and 3 require a complete statistical re-analysis from raw data.
